Tread: A Visuo-Inertial Framework for Terrain
and Locomotion Context-Aware Gait-Phase and
Speed Estimation in Wearable Robotics
Debadrata Sarkar, Akash Bachhar, Aman Arora *
Abstract— A wearable assistive robot must continu-
ously estimate terrain, locomotion mode, gait phase,
and walking speed. These quantities are commonly
addressed by separate, terrain-specific models, with
datasets rarely collected under a common protocol.
This paper presents a visuo-inertial framework that
connects these estimates through a causal pipeline
using a chest-mounted camera and four lower-limb
inertial measurement units (IMUs). Anticipatory Ter-
rain Perception (ATP) classifies incoming terrain with
96.3% accuracy and anticipates terrain transitions by
1.46 s on average. Terrain-Aware Locomotion Mode Identification (TALMI) achieves 93.7% and 98.8% accuracy for its
level-ground and stairs experts respectively, with mean transition latencies being below 200 ms. The relevant TALMI
posterior is retained as a Probabilistic Locomotion Context (PLC) vector and provided to a shared Gait Kinematic Encoder
(GKE), which supports 50-Hz bilateral gait-phase estimation and 2-Hz walking-speed estimation. Leave-one- subject-
out evaluation yields gait-phase RMSEs of 1.75%, 2.97%, and 4.55% for level walking, stair ascent, and stair descent
respectively while estimating speed over 3.5-5.5 km/h with a RMSE of 0.218 ± 0.072 km/h. All inertial sensing based models
use sensor-fused Euler angles as input rather than raw accelerometer and gyroscope signals, reducing dependence on
sensor mounting variation.
Index Terms— Wearable robotics, exoskeleton, terrain classification, locomotion mode identification, gait phase estima-
tion, walking speed estimation, inertial sensing, context-aware estimation, edge inference.

I. INTRODUCTION
P
OWERED lower-limb exoskeletons, exosuits, and active
prostheses require timely information about the user’s lo-
comotion for adapting assistance to gait phase, walking speed,
and terrain. Errors in timing or locomotion mode can reduce
assistance quality and compromise stability [8], [15]. Recent
reviews identify intention recognition and synchronization
with the user as key requirements for wearable-robot autonomy
[14]. Accordingly, a practical perception layer should estimate
four quantities continuously: upcoming terrain, locomotion
mode, gait phase, and walking speed. Although these quan-
tities are often estimated separately, their integration into a
single body-worn pipeline remains less explored.

Several limitations in state-of-the-art literature motivate the

The main contribution of this work is a connected visuo-
inertial pipeline that produces terrain, locomotion mode, bi-
lateral gait phase, and walking speed estimation using a
unified sensing suite deployed for onboard edge inference.
Anticipatory Terrain Perception (ATP) uses vision to esti-
mate the upcoming terrain, while Terrain-Aware Locomotion
Mode Identification (TALMI) estimates the locomotion being
executed. Rather than passing only a hard locomotion label
downstream, the full TALMI softmax posterior is retained
as a Probabilistic Locomotion Context (PLC) vector. Gait
phase and walking speed are then decoded from a shared Gait
Kinematic Encoder (GKE) through lightweight task-specific
heads. A dedicated fidgeting class separates stationary leg
movements from walking and a terrain-conditioned hierarchy
allows datasets collected under different protocols to be com-
bined within one estimation chain.
All inertial models use pitch and yaw Euler angles obtained
from onboard sensor fusion rather than raw accelerometer and
gyroscope channels. This representation is gravity-referenced
and expresses limb orientation in an anatomically mean-
ingful frame, reducing dependence on sensor-frame rotation
and small mounting variations [16], that otherwise make
the reproducability and scalability difficult when used in a
new wearable hardware. The experiments therefore evaluate
whether this compact representation, combined with terrain
and locomotion context, is sufficient for terrain-aware mode
identification, continuous bilateral gait-phase estimation, and
walking-speed regression.
II. HARDWARE AND DATA COLLECTION
A. Instrumentation
The wearable platform is shown in Fig. 1. A smartphone
RGB camera is mounted at sternum height and tilted 10–15◦
from vertical to capture upcoming terrain while retaining the
near-field ground. Four IMUs (BNO085, Adafruit Industries)
are mounted laterally on the right thigh, right shank, left thigh,
and left shank respectively. Each bilateral pair is interfaced
to an ESP32 over I2C and both microcontrollers stream data
via USB to a Jetson AGX Orin (NVIDIA Corporation). The
Jetson, microcontrollers, and a 6S 13,500-mAh Li-ion battery
with its power-distribution system are carried in a backpack,
enabling fully onboard sensing and inference. Data acquisition,
preprocessing and edge inference use ROS 2 and micro-ROS,
providing a common time-stamped middleware layer. During
data collection, sensorized insoles with an FSR beneath each
heel provided heel-contact events for gait-phase annotation.
These were used only as ground truth gait event markers and
are excluded from the deployed system.
B. Participants
Data were collected from 10 male participants (mean age:
28.7 ± 4.6 years; mean height: 171.2 ± 7.4 cm; 8 right-foot
dominant), all of whom provided informed consent. Two ad-
ditional male participants, excluded from training, validation,
and test sets, were used only for unseen-user demonstrations
(ages 29 and 28 years; heights 173 and 169 cm).
C. Acquisition Protocols
For terrain perception, participants walked at self-selected
speeds through indoor and outdoor environments containing
level ground, stair ascent and descent. Images were recorded at
720×1280 pixels and 30 frames per second. For inertial data,
participants followed routes containing level ground, stairs
and turns while an observer followed behind and recorded
a second-person video. Participants also stopped, turned and
fidgeted naturally in addition to when prompted to do so,
to avoid over-representation of steady walking. Turns were
annotated for heading changes of approximately 60–90◦. The
video was used for inertial-mode annotation, with heel-contact
FSR traces providing additional temporal support. Approxi-
mately 20 minutes of overground inertial data were collected
per participant for locomotion-mode annotation, in addition to
the treadmill sessions for annotated walking speed described
below..
For each limb, a gait cycle was defined between successive
ipsilateral heel contacts and phase was linearly interpolated
from 0–100% within each cycle. Speed-labeled data were
collected on a treadmill at 3.5, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, and 5.5 km/h
using three sessions per speed, where each session was
approximately of 2 minutes. Only four participants could
maintain a continuous 5.5 km/h walk comfortably on treadmill.
The treadmill and overground protocols were intentionally
separate. The former provided controlled speed labels, whereas
the latter provided terrain and turning data without direct speed
labels. Their integration within the proposed framework is
described in Sec. V.
III. ANTICIPATORY TERRAIN PERCEPTION
A terrain classifier that responds only after a new surface
has been treaded over cannot provide advance information for
control. ATP therefore classifies the terrain being approached,
providing look-ahead information for downstream estimation
as well as several supporting functions utilized during de-
ployment, such as gain scheduling, hardware initialization or
actuator pre-charging.

The ATP corpus (Fig. 2) combines the public ExoNet
dataset [1], which contributes 54.48% of the frames, with a
custom recorded chest-camera dataset (45.52%). Both datasets
use the same annotation rule; the upper 60% of each frame
is considered the dominant region and the frame is assigned
the terrain class which occupies at least the upper 60% region.
This associates the image with the incoming terrain observed
ahead of the user rather than the terrain directly underfoot,
while providing a deterministic label for mixed-terrain frames.
The annotated frames are divided into training, validation,
and test sets using a 70%/20%/10% split, performed at the
sequence level so that no frames from the same video appear
in more than one split. Images are resized with the shorter
side set to 256 pixels, center-cropped to 224 × 224 pixels and
normalized using ImageNet statistics. ATP uses an ImageNet-
pretrained ResNet-18 backbone [17] as a frozen feature extrac-
tor(Fig. 3). Global average pooling yields z ∈R512, followed
by a trainable MLP with 1024, 1024 and 3 units. While ReLU
activations are used in the first two layers, the final softmax
layer produces the terrain posterior q ∈R3; the predicted

50 epochs using Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 10−3, step
decay of 0.1 every 7 epochs, batch size of 32 and cross-entropy

IV. TERRAIN-AWARE LOCOMOTION MODE
IDENTIFICATION
TALMI identifies seven locomotion modes: standing (ST),
fidgeting (FG), level walking (LW), right turn (RT), left turn
(LT), stair ascent (SA), and stair descent (SD). Fidgeting
denotes leg movement while remaining stationary, such as
weight shifting, foot repositioning, or stepping in place. It
is treated separately from standing and walking to avoid
triggering assistance during preparatory movement.
TALMI uses pitch and yaw from the four IMUs. Pitch
captures sagittal-plane motion, while yaw changes primarily
during turns, enabling separation of turning from straight
walking. Differences in pitch amplitude and waveform further
distinguish stair ascent from descent. The synchronized heel-
contact and IMU traces used for annotation are shown in
Fig. 5.

B. Shared Gait Kinematic Encoder
The sequence Xn is processed by a 64-unit LSTM layer,
the Gait Kinematic Encoder (GKE), whose final hidden state
hn ∈R64 is shared by both decoders (Fig. 9). The GKE
is first trained jointly with the gait-phase decoder and then
frozen while the speed decoder is trained. This is essentially
due to the fact that the gait phase estimation provides the more
detailed temporal constraint throughout a gait cycle during
training, whereas speed estimation is a much more aggregated
effect. Thus, this allows the speed estimation decoder to be
trained much conveniently without retraining the encoder.

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presents a single visuo-inertial framework for
estimating four locomotion descriptors relevant to wearable-
robot control: upcoming terrain, locomotion mode, bilateral
gait phase and walking speed. A chest-mounted RGB camera
provides anticipatory terrain information, while four lower-
limb IMUs provide inertial locomotion cues. These compo-
nents are connected causally, with the TALMI locomotion
posterior passed as probabilistic context to a shared Gait
Kinematic Encoder and its phase and speed decoders.
ATP achieves 96.3% terrain-classification accuracy and an-
ticipates terrain transitions by 1.46 s on average. TALMI
achieves 93.7% and 98.8% accuracy for the level-ground
and stairs experts respectively, with mean transition detection
latencies below 200 ms. The shared encoder yields leave-one-
subject-out gait-phase RMSEs of 1.75%, 2.97%, and 4.55%
for level walking, stair ascent, and stair descent respectively,
while speed estimation covers 3.5–5.5 km/h with a LOSO
RMSE of 0.218 ± 0.072 km/h. Two design choices are
central to the framework; probabilistic locomotion context
rather than hard mode switching and a terrain-conditioned
hierarchy that combines datasets collected under different
protocols. The inertial pathway also uses gravity-referenced
pitch and yaw Euler angles instead of raw inertial channels,
reducing dependence on sensor mounting. For qualitative
validation, a supplementary video is provided demonstrating
real-time inference of the complete pipeline, including terrain
anticipation, locomotion-mode detection, bilateral gait-phase
estimation and walking-speed estimation during indoor and
outdoor locomotion.
Speed estimation was evaluated only for level walking using
treadmill speed based annotated labels, and terrain perception
was limited to level ground and stairs. Future work will
extend speed estimation to stairs and overground walking,
broaden terrain coverage and evaluate closed-loop exoskeleton
assistance using kinematic and metabolic outcomes.
