Abstract— We propose Compliant GMT (C-GMT), a mod-
ular framework for full-body compliant responses in general
motion tracking. As humanoid robots move into real world
deployment, they not only need to perform any motion but
also respond safely and robustly to interaction forces. Existing
general motion tracking policies can imitate diverse motions
but are trained to be stiff, leading to unexpected and un-
safe behaviors under disturbance. Prior work in compliant
humanoid control trades off compliance and generality. There
exist examples of full-body compliant responses, but these are
heavily curated to single-reference policies. On the other hand,
general motion trackers (GMT) are trained to rigidly imitate
massive motion capture datasets. Our key insight is to leverage
the generality of pretrained GMTs for motion tracking while
introducing compliance from a residual adapter that conditions
on a history of proprioception. This enables our approach,
C-GMT, to achieve both general motion tracking and whole-
body compliant responses. Compared to state-of-the-art general
motion trackers and single-motion compliant policies, C-GMT
demonstrates competitive performance in both motion tracking
accuracy and compliance adherence. We further demonstrate
improved safety, robustness, and error tolerance in simulation
and real-world G1 experiments. Project website: https://
venki1899.github.io/c-gmt/
I. INTRODUCTION
As humanoid robots move toward operation in human-
centered environments, they must combine agile and robust
whole-body control with the ability to interact safely and
effectively with people and objects. General motion tracking
(GMT) has emerged as the predominant approach for hu-
manoid whole-body control, using a single policy trained on
massive human motion capture datasets to imitate diverse and
highly dynamic motion references while rejecting external
disturbances [1]–[3]. Although this stiffness is desirable
for free-space motion tracking, unexpected contacts during
physical interaction will cause the policy to forcefully drive
the robot toward its reference position, potentially damaging
the robot or environment. This is a fundamental barrier to
deploying humanoids alongside people.
A compliant robot mitigates these effects by yielding
appropriately to external forces, improving safety and robust-
ness. However, recent work on learned compliant humanoid
control trades off generality and compliance. Works that
demonstrate coordinated full-body compliant responses are
restricted to single motion imitation [4] or command the
lower body only with base velocity and height [5]. Alter-
natively, other works perform general motion tracking but
restrict compliant responses to the upper-body [6], [7]. These
approaches jointly train motion tracking and compliance
within the same policy, requiring the agent to simultane-
ously follow the commanded motion, maintain balance, infer
external wrenches, and learn to respond compliantly. Con-
sequently, single-stage training poses significant challenges
with long training times, many infeasible force events, and
sensitive reward tuning. Our key insight is that we can
achieve both competitive generality and compliance in a
single policy by exploiting the capabilities of pretrained
general motion trackers and introducing compliance through
a residual adapter conditioned on a proprioception history.
We introduce Compliant GMT (C-GMT), the first frame-
work for general motion tracking with unified full-body

compliant responses. Rather than asking the motion tracking
policy itself to learn both tracking and compliance, we
formulate compliance as a reference adaptation problem.
This decomposition separates the two objectives: a force-
robust GMT based on [3] is responsible for faithfully ex-
ecuting the commanded motion and maintaining balance
under disturbances, while a learned residual adapter modifies
the reference motion to realize the desired compliance. To
generate compliant reference motions for the downstream
GMT, we construct an analytical teacher that observes the
applied external force and uses a spring law and an inverse-
kinematics (IK) solver to compute compliant adaptations.
We can use the teacher and pretrained GMT to tune the
desired response and verify its feasibility before training the
adapter. To learn to estimate force and react appropriately
at deployment time, we distill the compliant behavior into a
residual adapter conditioned on proprioception that provides
compliant references to the GMT. Our contributions are
twofold:
1. We introduce Compliant GMT, a modular framework
that unifies general motion tracking with adjustable whole-
body compliance through residual reference adaptation. We
propose a proprioceptive residual adapter distilled from a
force-privileged teacher to generate compliant references.
2. Through simulation and real world experiments on
the Unitree G1, we show that C-GMT matches both the
compliance adherence of previous compliant single-motion
tracking policies [4] and maintains tracking performance
comparable to state-of-the-art GMTs. We further demonstrate
C-GMT’s novel advantages of compliance during highly
dynamic whole-body motions and coordinated whole-body
compliant responses.
II. RELATED WORK
A. General Motion Tracking
Advances in sim-to-real reinforcement learning [8], [9],
motion retargeting [10], [11], and large-scale human mo-
tion capture datasets [12]–[14] have enabled impressive hu-
manoid general motion tracking policies capable of playing
back diverse dynamic motions in the real world. GMTs
convert target trajectories of joint or keypoint positions from
a reference motion clip or planner output into physically
feasible joint motor commands [1], [2], [15]. They have
formed the critical foundation of humanoid visuomotor
policy training as both a teleoperation interface for data
collection [3], [16] and the module responsible for physically
executing vision-language-action (VLA) model outputs [17].
Recent work has used LoRA [18] to finetune open-sourced
GMTs [3] for perceptive control [19] and cross-embodiment
transfer [20]. Currently, leading GMTs are trained to stiffly
track target positions, exerting large, uncontrolled forces [21]
when the robot deviates from its reference. This problem is
exacerbated when reference motions are generated by higher-
level systems with delayed, imperfect feedback [22], [23]
and performed by a GMT that may introduce subtle, hard-
to-model tracking error [24]. These issues can be alleviated
by a compliant GMT that yields to unexpected forces, reduc-
ing the necessary precision and reactivity of a higher-level
control system. Motivated by these observations, we propose
a compliant GMT based on SONIC [3] which supports plug-
and-play integration with open-source teleoperation systems
and VLAs.
B. Learned Compliant Humanoid Control
Reinforcement learning is a promising method for training
compliant control policies in simulation [25] and deployed
on real legged robots [26]–[29]. A common approach in
both classical [30], [31] and learning-based methods is to
model the end effector as a spring-mass-damper system
and alter reference trajectories based on force feedback.
For humanoids, balancing force interaction, motion tracking,
and stability objectives to achieve compliant responses is
nontrivial, and recent learning-based methods approach this
problem by trading off generality and compliance. CHIP [7]
and GentleHumanoid [6] are built on trained-from-scratch,
whole-body motion-tracking policies, but only the upper
body participates in compliant responses. SoftMimic [4]
demonstrates coordinated whole-body compliant responses,
but trains a separate policy for each reference motion.
More recently, LAC [5] learns to respond compliantly with
coordinated whole-body responses, but only supports base
velocity and height commands for the lower body. C-GMT
is the first system to achieve state-of-the-art performance
in both general motion tracking and coordinating compliant
responses across the whole body.
C. Residual Models for Humanoid Robotics
It is very appealing to enable compliant behaviors without
retraining foundational GMTs from scratch. Prior work in
residual policy learning adds corrective residual layers over
frozen base controllers [32]. Recent work in humanoid whole
body control continues to use residual models to preserve the
base motion tracker’s stability and generality while enabling
dynamic trajectory modification based on sensory feedback
[24], [33], [34]. Notably, Huang et al. apply learned resid-
ual offsets to stabilize payloads upstream at the kinematic
reference layer before the motion tracking policy [35]. To
make modifications without onboard force sensors, teacher-
student distillation is commonly used to infer dynamic and
environmental properties from joint position and velocity
histories [36]. Drawing from these works, C-GMT uses
a lightweight residual adapter that computes an adapted
reference from proprioception and passes it to a pretrained
general motion tracker to execute.
III. METHODS
Compliant GMT decomposes compliant general motion
tracking into two components: (i) a force-robust general
motion tracker that faithfully executes reference motions
under external loads, and (ii) a residual adapter that mod-
ifies the reference to produce a desired compliant response.
This decomposition allows the desired compliant behavior to
be specified independently of the motion-tracking objective.

A. Force-Robust General Motion Tracker
Many GMTs take as input kinematic reference quantities
like root motion, joint positions, and joint velocities. Direct
residual adaptation in these spaces is challenging because
small changes in quantities can induce large changes in the
resulting motion, making the residual mapping difficult to
learn. In earlier experiments, we specifically observed a lot
of unintended walking under small variations in root velocity.
We build on SONIC [3], which encodes reference motions
into a learned latent representation and quantizes them into
universal motion tokens. This provides a compact reference
space for adaptation while leveraging a tracker pretrained on
a large and diverse set of motions.
The original SONIC policy is not trained to withstand
sustained loads applied to the arms. Under such disturbances,
the arm can deviate substantially from the reference motion
and sufficiently large forces can destabilize the robot. We
therefore finetune the SONIC decoder to be robust to forces
while keeping its motion encoders frozen. We otherwise
retain the original SONIC training procedure, including
its objectives, domain randomization, and adaptive motion
sampling, while additionally introducing load perturbations.
During finetuning, each hand is subjected to a combina-
tion of carried payloads and sustained external forces. The
payload masses are independently sampled up to 2 kg, while
external forces with randomly sampled directions and mag-
nitudes are held constant for 2–4 s before being resampled.
The combined gravitational and externally applied load on
each arm is constrained by a force budget, which gradually
increases from 10 to 30 N during training.
Different reference motions tolerate different external
loads. We therefore maintain a per-motion load scale µc
that is updated throughout training based on the cumulative
failure rate of motion clip c. The force budget for clip c is

directions, and multiple stiffness values. We retain force
ranges for which the teacher remains stable without falling
and use these during adapter training.
C. Residual Adapter Architecture
The residual adapter learns to reproduce the reference
modifications generated by the privileged teacher using
proprioceptive observations and previous actions. Rather
than generating a new motion representation from scratch,
it predicts a residual correction to the original reference,
allowing the underlying motion to pass through unchanged
when adaptation is unnecessary.
SONIC uses finite scalar quantization (FSQ) to map an
encoded motion into a universal token z. FSQ independently
quantizes each latent dimension to one of a fixed number
of discrete levels, producing two motion tokens in SONIC.
Applying the residual before FSQ causes small changes
in the continuous latent to cross quantization boundaries,
producing abrupt changes in the resulting token and visible
jitter. We therefore apply the residual after FSQ, directly to
the universal motion token z.
The adapter receives a 10-step history of proprioceptive
observations and previous actions, together with the motion
token z and the commanded stiffness k. Since stiffness spans
40–600 N/m in this work, we provide log k to the network
to better represent this range.
The adapter is implemented as a lightweight MLP with
residual, gating, and auxiliary force-prediction heads. The
residual head predicts a 64-dimensional correction ∆z, while
the gating head predicts one value in [0, 1] for each of
SONIC’s two motion tokens. The modified token is

where each gate is applied to the residual corresponding to

objectives and are additionally encouraged to be zero when
no external force is present. After gating, residual elements
with magnitude below 0.005 are set to zero, providing
a deadband around the original token and allowing the
reference to pass through unchanged when little adaptation
is required. The auxiliary force-prediction head estimates the
forces acting on the hands and encourages the proprioceptive
representation to capture information about the external inter-
action that is useful for determining the appropriate reference
modification.
D. Residual Adapter Training
We train the residual adapter by distilling the privileged
teacher. The primary imitation objectives match the decoded
teacher action and the teacher-modified motion token, with
additional emphasis on aligning with the teacher action
when an external force is present. We additionally supervise
the auxiliary force-prediction head, regularize the residual
magnitude, and encourage the adapter to recover the original
GMT behavior when no external force is applied by driving
the residual action, token correction, and gates toward zero.
Residual action-rate and jerk losses provide additional tem-
poral regularization.


Training proceeds in three stages. We first train for 500
updates using teacher-driven rollouts. Over the following
1000 updates, we linearly transition from teacher-driven
to student-driven rollouts. Training then continues using
student-driven trajectories until convergence. Allowing the
student to drive the rollout exposes the adapter to states
induced by its own prediction errors, improving robustness
beyond the state distribution encountered when following the
teacher.

E. Experimental Setup
We finetune SONIC for force robustness on the BONES-
SEED dataset [13] using 3 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for 5 days.
The resulting force-robust GMT is then kept fixed and used
to train the residual adapter on the same dataset using 4
NVIDIA H100 GPUs for 2 days. All experiments use the
same force-robust GMT and residual adapter.
We evaluate this policy with three reference-motion in-
terfaces. For direct motion playback, reference motions are
encoded using SONIC’s G1 motion encoder. For whole-
body teleoperation, a PICO 4 Ultra provides tracked human
body poses, which are converted to the SMPL representa-
tion [37] expected by SONIC’s Human encoder. For VLA
experiments, we generate references from fine-tuned GR00T
N1.7-3B VLAs [17] and provide them to the residual adapter
together with the proprioceptive inputs. The predicted resid-
ual is applied to the reference before execution by the same
force-robust GMT. Thus, all three interfaces use the same
residual adapter and underlying force-robust GMT.
IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
A. C-GMT accurately tracks compliance levels
We first evaluate whether C-GMT produces the compli-
ance specified by the commanded stiffness. In simulation,
we apply external forces to a standing robot and measure the
resulting displacement over the range of stiffness values used
during training. We compute the effective stiffness as the
ratio between the applied force and resulting displacement.
Fig. 3 reports the measured effective stiffness as a function
of the commanded stiffness on a log–log scale.
C-GMT responds consistently to the stiffness command
across the full training range, producing increasingly smaller
displacements as stiffness increases. We additionally com-
pare against SoftMimic, which is trained specifically on the


standing motion used in this evaluation. Despite C-GMT
being trained to operate across diverse reference motions,
its compliance response closely matches that of the motion-
specific SoftMimic controller. This indicates that C-GMT’s
general motion tracking capabilities do not degrade the
accuracy of the commanded compliant response.
B. C-GMT is an accurate general motion tracker
We next evaluate whether the residual compliance adapter
degrades the nominal tracking performance of the underlying
GMT. We compare C-GMT against SONIC, our force-robust
GMT without the adapter, TWIST2, and GentleHumanoid
under no external force perturbations. We evaluate all meth-
ods on motions from the LAFAN1 dataset [14], separat-
ing the evaluation into walking and dynamic motions. The
evaluation contains 12 walking clips and 17 dynamic clips,
corresponding to 48 and 63 minutes of motion, respectively.
Notably, GentleHumanoid is trained using motions from this
dataset. We report success rate, defined as the fraction of
evaluation windows in which the robot remains upright, to-
gether with global and local position and orientation tracking
errors. Position and orientation errors are averaged across the
links of the robot. Table II summarizes the results.
C-GMT maintains tracking performance close to SONIC,
with only a modest increase in global position error. Most of
this increase is already present in the force-robust GMT, even
before adding the residual adapter. At the same time, C-GMT
substantially outperforms TWIST2 and GentleHumanoid in
success rate, particularly on dynamic motions.
Tracking performance also remains nearly unchanged be-
tween stiffness k = 100 and k = 300. This indicates that the
adapter does not interfere substantially with the underlying
motion in the absence of external forces and can distinguish
external interaction from the robot’s own motion.
C. C-GMT enables safe interaction during dynamic motions
To investigate the efficacy of C-GMT in reducing interac-
tion forces during dynamic motion execution, we place the
robot next to a rigid wall and execute the same dynamic
dance motion using C-GMT, SONIC, and TWIST2. For C-
GMT, we use a commanded stiffness of k = 60 N/m and
measure the contact force exerted on the wall throughout
the motion. Fig. 4 shows the resulting contact forces over
time. C-GMT produces the lowest interaction forces, SONIC
generates larger forces, and TWIST2 produces the largest
impacts. The larger interaction forces also affect the resulting
robot motion: SONIC pushes itself away from the wall and
recovers, while TWIST2’s high impact force launches the
robot far away. In contrast, C-GMT yields to the contact
while still closely tracking the commanded motion. Thus, C-
GMT remains compliant even during highly dynamic motion
execution, safely reducing interaction forces while limiting
the disturbance to the robot’s commanded motion. We deploy
the same dancing motion on a real robot placed next to a wall
or being pulled by a human; we observe safe disturbance
attenuation as shown in Fig. 5.

that support general motion tracking, C-GMT is the only
one to support full-body compliant responses. We examine
whether allowing the entire body to participate in the compli-
ant response improves robustness. We compare C-GMT with
SoftMimic and GentleHumanoid under a downward force
applied to the hand. The magnitude of the force is gradually
increased during the experiment.

upright because the legs do not participate in the compliant

V. CONCLUSION
We present C-GMT, a modular framework that equips a
pretrained general motion tracker with adjustable full-body
compliant responses through residual reference adaptation.
In contrast to prior work that makes tradeoffs between
compliance and generality, C-GMT achieves both compet-
itive motion tracking accuracy and compliance adherence
relative to state-of-the-art general motion trackers and single-
motion compliant policies. We further demonstrate improved
safety and robustness to disturbances and errors from high-
level controllers like teleoperation and VLAs. Future work
will extend compliant responses to interactions at any link
on the robot’s body and develop higher-level policies to
automatically select and adapt the stiffness command based
on context.

A. Force-Robust Tracker Training
We initialize the force-robust tracker from SONIC [3] and
fine-tune its decoder and critic using PPO, while keeping
the motion encoders and FSQ quantizer frozen. We retain
SONIC’s original rewards, domain randomization, and adap-
tive motion sampling.
Training is performed in Isaac Lab on BONES-SEED [13],
with a physics timestep of 5 ms and a policy frequency of
50 Hz. We use 24 rollout steps per environment, 5 PPO
epochs, 4 minibatches, a clipping ratio of 0.2, γ = 0.99,
and GAE λ = 0.95. The actor and critic learning rates are
2 × 10−5 and 10−3, respectively.
Each hand receives randomly sampled payloads up to
2 kg and sustained external forces with random directions,
resampled every 2–4 s. The combined load is limited by
a motion-dependent force budget, gradually increased from
10 to 30 N. Additional training settings are summarized in
Table V.
B. Compliant Teacher Implementation
The compliant teacher uses a spring law to compute
the desired hand displacement from the applied force and
commanded stiffness. A whole-body inverse-kinematics (IK)
solver then generates a modified reference motion.
The IK problem is formulated as a weighted, box-
constrained quadratic program. The primary objectives are
tracking the displaced hand position, maintaining foot place-
ment, and preserving balance through center-of-mass track-
ing. Additional regularization keeps the resulting configura-
tion close to the original motion. The IK task weights are
summarized in Table IV.
The solver runs at 50 Hz with joint velocity and position
limits. Desired hand displacement is constrained to avoid
self-collisions and abrupt reference changes.
We perform an automated feasibility sweep over 4096
representative reference motions from BONES-SEED [13],
eight force directions, and multiple commanded stiffness
values. We retain force ranges for which the teacher remains

C. Residual Adapter Architecture
The residual adapter takes a 10-step proprioceptive history
(930 dimensions), a 64-dimensional motion representation,
and the logarithm of the commanded stiffness.
The network consists of a two-layer MLP with hidden
dimensions [512, 256] and ELU activations, followed by
three output heads:
• Residual head: Predicts a 64-dimensional motion-token
correction using a tanh activation.
• Gating head: Predicts two sigmoid gates, one for each
motion token.
• Force prediction head: Predicts the external forces on
both wrists using an additional 128-dimensional hidden
layer.
The residual head is zero-initialized to preserve the orig-
inal tracker behavior at initialization. The gated correction
is applied after FSQ, with a deadband of 0.005 to suppress
small residual changes.
D. Residual Adapter Training
We train the residual adapter in mjlab (MuJoCo-Warp)
using a 2 ms physics timestep and a 50 Hz control frequency.
The force-robust tracker remains frozen throughout training.
External forces are applied to randomly selected hands,
with directions sampled uniformly on the sphere. Com-
manded stiffness is sampled log-uniformly between 40 and
600 N/m. Force magnitudes are sampled within the feasibil-
ity limits determined by the teacher, capped at 140 N. We
additionally randomize link masses, friction, joint damping,
initial states, and proprioceptive observations.
The training objective combines action and motion-token
imitation, auxiliary force prediction, residual regularization,
and temporal smoothness. Additional penalties on force-
free samples encourage the adapter to preserve the original
tracker behavior. The loss weights are summarized in Ta-
ble VI.

E. Deployment
We use a laptop equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3080
GPU to run C-GMT on the Unitree G1. The residual adapter
and force-robust GMT operate at 50 Hz. During motion play-
back, to prevent unstable motion under external interactions,
the reference motion is paused when the exponential moving
average (EMA) of the predicted force magnitude exceeds
a stiffness-dependent threshold. Playback resumes once the
EMA falls below the threshold.
F. Effect of Feasibility Tuning

a residual adapter on a standing reference motion and com-

sampling reduces falls during training by approximately 13×
(0.4% vs. 5.4% of training steps) and accelerates compliance
learning. As shown in Fig. 9, our method reaches over 95%
give-way adherence within 6k updates, while uniform sam-
pling remains around 74%. This faster convergence reduces
the overall training time required to achieve the desired
