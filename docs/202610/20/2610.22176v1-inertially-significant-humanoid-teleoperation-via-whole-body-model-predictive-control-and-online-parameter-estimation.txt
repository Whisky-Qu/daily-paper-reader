# Inertially Significant Humanoid Teleoperation via Whole Body Model Predictive Control and Online Parameter Estimation 

James Foster 1,2, Beomyeong Park 1, Luigi Penco 1, Robert Griffin 1,2

Abstract — The effective deployment of humanoid robots in practical tasks of interest will require a capability of sensing and adapting to variations in inertial parameters, as occurs during heavy tool use or when carrying significant payload. Such payloads constitute a significant proportion of the combined payload-robot system, the effects of which cannot be ignored by delegating to feedback control. This paper showcases a general solution to this problem by integrating a whole body model predictive controller (WB-MPC) with an extended Kalman filter (EKF) for online estimation of fully physically consistent time-varying inertial parameters. These estimates are fed through to the WB-MPC’s full rigid body dynamics model, resulting in more accurate feedforward torques for task demands under external load. Additionally, a virtual reality (VR) interface is provided that enables an operator to provide task-specific references without regard to payload while delegating dynamic balance to the WB-MPC. The framework is validated with simulation and hardware experiments on a humanoid system. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A key promise of humanoid robots lies in performing tasks that involve exerting significant force to manipulated objects, such as lifting a heavy tool or carrying a payload. In many such tasks, the inertia of the manipulated object is a significant fraction of the robot’s own. A promising candidate solution is whole body model predictive control (WB-MPC), which iteratively solves a trajectory optimization over the robot’s full rigid body dy-namics. It was first demonstrated on a humanoid in [1], and subsequent humanoid implementations have largely targeted locomotion [2]–[4]. Far fewer works apply WB-MPC to manipulation [5], and fewer still to objects whose inertia is significant relative to the robot. The closest is [6], which remains in simulation and estimates a modest payload inertia only after task execution. Other model-based approaches either add the load to a centroidal model explicitly [7], or ab-sorb it implicitly through direct adaptive control laws bound to a reference model [8, 9]. Reinforcement learning (RL), meanwhile, has produced increasingly robust humanoid lo-comotion and manipulation [10, 11], but typically absorbs load variation in one of two ways. The first is domain randomization [12, 13], which trades nominal performance for robustness and tends toward conservative behavior; the second is a learned “extrinsics” estimator, whose abstract signals are tightly coupled to the policy that consumes them [14]. A WB-MPC over the full rigid body dynamics can in principle directly command the feedforward effort an added payload demands, but only insofar as its rigid body dynamics model accurately captures the manipulated inertia [16]. A structure that underpins most identification methods for rigid body inertial parameters is that the inverse dynamics are linear with respect to the inertial parameters [17]. The system identification literature usually focuses on offline identification [18]; control-oriented online work has instead tended to focus on estimating gross quantities such as the center of mass and momentum [19], or a payload from previously identified dynamics coefficients [20] or a mo-mentum observer [21]. For an estimate to be safely fed into a downstream controller, it must be fully physically consistent [22], which can be enforced via semidefinite programming [23], smooth nonlinear reparameterizations of the inertial parameters [24], or geometric methods [25, 26]. Model bias is also problematic when operating the robot via a virtual reality (VR) interface. VR provides operators with an intuitive control interface [27], and has become common both as a primary operation method and for data collection of learned control policies [28]. One such exception is the impressive but complex VR/haptic teleoperation suite in [29], where online inertial estimates are used to adjust the posture of a wheeled humanoid modeled as a cart-pole system, allowing the manipulation of heavy payloads relative to robot weight. In this paper we propose a framework coupling three com-ponents to enable intuitive VR teleoperation of humanoids manipulating significant loads via WB-MPC. Firstly, a WB-MPC trades off dynamic balance objectives with tight track-ing of operator-specified taskspace objectives. Secondly, an extended Kalman filter (EKF) coupled in closed-loop with the WB-MPC estimates fully physically consistent inertial parameters online and passes them to the WB-MPC’s rigid body dynamics model, allowing feedforward torque compen-sation of a priori unknown loads. We include EKF extensions that increase numerical robustness and aid interpretation: we regularize the estimate through a fictitious nominal measure-ment that keeps the estimate well-conditioned under the weak excitation of teleoperated motion, and estimate deviations from a well-characterized nominal body using a generalized log-Cholesky parameterization [24, 30]. Lastly, a minimal VR teleoperation interface, combined with the aforemen-tioned WB-MPC and EKF, allows an operator to command desired motion without the burden of compensating for added payload, while dynamic balance control is delegated to the WB-MPC. We validate the framework on loaded trajectory tracking and balance tasks in simulation, and showcase VR teleoperation under load on hardware for a 23 degree of freedom humanoid system (Fig. 1). II. D. Practical Implementation Details 

The framework in Fig. 2 is implemented on two machines. The WB-MPC, EKF, and state estimator are deployed inside a single real-time process on an onboard control computer. The state estimator and low-level control run on a 1 kHz thread; the EKF and WB-MPC run on a 100 Hz thread. The VR kinematic streaming toolbox of Section III-C runs on a separate operator desktop computer and streams its references via ROS2 messages at a best-effort rate of 50 Hz, decoupling the operator interface from the real-time loop. Each tick, the estimate is mapped back to πest , rate-limited to prevent abrupt jumps, and written to the WB-MPC’s dynamics model. The WB-MPC horizon length K = 30 nodes with an Euler discretization of ∆t = 20 ms which resolves to a prediction horizon of 0.6 s. A. Simulation 1) Pre-recorded trajectory tracking with/without EKF: 

To isolate the estimator’s effect on tracking from operator variability, we drive the robot with a pre-recorded teleopera-tion trajectory, consisting of a window-washing motion that sweeps both forearms across the frontal plane. Though pre-recorded, it is fed through such that the WB-MPC cannot anticipate the trajectory. At 3 s, the left forearm (nominal mass 0.62 kg) has a 5 kg payload mounted at the forearm tip ( 0.41 m down from the elbow joint), while the right is unloaded and serves as a within-trial reference. We compare taskspace tracking of the loaded forearm with the EKF disabled versus enabled, feeding estimates to the WB-MPC’s dynamics model in Fig. 3 and Table IV. With no adaptation, the loaded left forearm sags significantly from its unloaded path, accumulating a vertical taskspace root mean square error (RMSE) of 389 .2 mm, an over 25 × increase compared to the error with adaptation. The horizontal taskspace error is less severe, though still significant; without adaptation the RMSE is 67 .8 mm while with adaptation it shrinks over 10 ×

to 6.4 mm. The estimated mass and center of mass track the added payload within two seconds of application, (Fig. 4). V. C ONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

In this paper we showcased practical VR teleoperation of unknown payloads with a combination of whole body model predictive control and physically consistent inertial extended Kalman filtering on a humanoid robot. Simulation experiments showed the necessity of online inertial estimation for trajectory tracking via the WB-MPC with significant added payload, and how closed-loop cou-pling of the EKF and WB-MPC allows an emergent ad-justment of posture under load to maintain balance. The framework is further validated on hardware by an operator test of the VR interface; the robot squats down to pick up and manipulate a large asymmetric load. In future work, we would like to incorporate perception techniques for producing inertial priors of objects that are due to be manipulated, to reduce the estimation transient response.
