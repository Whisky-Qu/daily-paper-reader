# Robust Real-Time Monocular Human Pose Estimation with Insole-Based Sensor Fusion 

Liang Yu , Tao Zhu , Senior Member, IEEE , Xiangqing Lu, Wenjian Luo, Senior Member, IEEE , and Liming Chen , Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Real-time monocular human pose estimation is an important sensing component for human-centered intelligent systems. While recent camera-based methods have achieved impressive accuracy, fundamental challenges persist, particularly when visual observations are incomplete, such as during lower-body occlusion or short out-of-frame intervals. In these challeng-ing but common scenarios, vision-only systems often struggle to reconstruct physically plausible lower-limb motion and reliable foot-ground contact. Due to the highly unobtrusive and wearable nature of smart insoles, this paper investigates whether simple plantar pressure and acceleration sensors can effectively resolve these visually induced limitations. To systematically investigate this, we first collect a synchronized multimodal dataset and then propose a part-decoupled temporal fusion framework. The proposed model fuses foot-sensing features into the lower-body branch to compensate for incomplete visual evidence, while a lightweight coordinator exchanges information between the upper- and lower-body temporal representations to maintain whole-body consistency. The framework is evaluated with three representative real-time visual backbones based on 2D keypoints, 3D landmarks, and SMPL-based human mesh recovery. We evaluate our framework on three representative real-time visual backbones. Quantitative experiments demonstrate that incorpo-rating simple foot sensors consistently improves pose accuracy, motion smoothness, and gait-related measurements over vision-only baselines, with the most significant gains observed under occlusion and short visual-dropout conditions. Ultimately, our results demonstrate that unobtrusive foot sensing provides vital and complementary physical observability when camera obser-vations are temporarily unavailable. 

Index Terms —Monocular pose estimation, plantar pressure, foot-mounted acceleration, multimodal sensor fusion, human motion estimation.

A. Real-Time Monocular Human Pose Estimation 

Monocular human pose estimation has been widely studied for low-cost and deployment-friendly motion sensing. Existing methods can be roughly grouped into 2D keypoint estima-tion, 3D landmark estimation, and human mesh recovery. For 2D pose estimation, YOLO-based, top-down, and whole-body pose estimators have improved real-time inference and keypoint localization accuracy [1], [2], [16]–[19]. Lightweight landmark systems such as BlazePose further support on-device human pose tracking for mobile and interactive ap-plications [20]. For monocular 3D pose estimation, recent temporal models exploit transformers, frequency-domain rep-resentations, or graph-based reasoning to improve video-based 3D pose stability [3], [4], [21]. Human mesh recovery methods estimate SMPL-like body representations and have advanced from image-based recovery to video-based and promptable reconstruction [6], [7], [22]–[24]. B. Foot-Sensing-Based Human Motion Estimation 

Foot sensing provides complementary physical information for human motion estimation. Plantar pressure reflects con-tact state, center-of-pressure variation, load transfer, and gait phase, while foot-mounted inertial signals capture short-term lower-limb dynamics. Wearable pressure sensors, smart shoes, and IMU-based systems have therefore been widely explored for gait analysis, locomotion recognition, and biomechanical motion assessment [15], [25]–[28]. Recent studies further show that foot sensing can support full-body pose or motion reconstruction. SolePoser estimates full-body pose from a pair of insole sensors, PressInPose integrates pressure and inertial sensing for activity-level pose estimation, and FoRM reconstructs SMPL motion from dual-modal plantar pressure and inertial signals [11]–[13]. Sparse-IMU and mobile-device-based systems also demonstrate the potential of compact wearable sensing for full-body motion estimation [14], [29]. Nevertheless, foot-only or sparse-sensor-only reconstruction is inherently under-constrained because upper-body posture and arm motion cannot be directly observed from foot signals alone. Our work therefore studies plantar-pressure and foot-acceleration signals as complementary physical evidence for monocular pose estimation rather than as a replacement for vision. 

C. Vision and Foot-Sensing Fusion for Human Motion Cap-ture 

A closely related direction is to incorporate foot-ground interaction into vision-based motion capture. Physics-aware methods use contact, center-of-pressure, or body-ground con-straints to reduce foot sliding and improve physically plausible pose estimation [7], [30]. Multimodal datasets and systems such as MMVP and MotionPRO further demonstrate that pres-sure information can improve visual motion capture, especially for foot contact, global translation, and human–world inter-action modeling [10], [31]. These studies confirm the value of pressure and contact cues for vision-based human motion estimation. However, many existing works focus on dataset construction, offline fitting, pressure-mat settings, or dedicated multimodal MoCap pipelines. In contrast, this paper targets real-time monocular pose estimation and studies whether four-point plantar pressure and foot-mounted acceleration can im-prove real-time monocular pose estimation under incomplete visual observations. B. Training Objective 

The training objective is designed to supervise both frame-level pose accuracy and temporal motion quality. Since the output is a continuous root-relative skeleton sequence, only minimizing the joint position error may still lead to frame-wise fluctuations or locally distorted limb configurations. Following standard practices in temporal 3D human pose estimation [32]–[34], we combine a joint position loss with first- and second-order temporal smoothness terms, and a bone-length consistency constraint: 

L = Lpos + λv Lvel + λaLacc + λbLbone . (7) Here, Lpos penalizes joint position errors in the root-relative skeleton space and provides the main supervision for pose accuracy. Lvel and Lacc constrain first- and second-order temporal differences, respectively, encouraging the predicted motion to follow the reference dynamics and reducing high-frequency jitter. Lbone preserves local kinematic structure by penalizing deviations in bone lengths, which helps avoid implausible limb deformation during temporal fusion. In our implementation, λv = 0 .1, λa = 0 .4, and λb = 0 .02 .To improve robustness to temporary visual failures, we ap-ply visual feature dropout during training. For selected training windows, a continuous temporal block of visual features is masked to zero while the synchronized insole stream remains available. The masked block length is randomly sampled within the sequence, so the model learns to use pressure and acceleration cues when visual observations are temporarily unreliable. This augmentation is used only during training. 

C. Real-Time Streaming Inference 

During inference, the proposed fusion module operates in a streaming manner. At each time step, it receives the current visual pose feature and the synchronized insole measure-ment, while maintaining recurrent hidden states from previous frames. The current pose is predicted as: 

ˆPt = f online θ (vbt , st, ht−1), (8) where ht−1 denotes the temporal memory from the previous frame. This design avoids reprocessing the full sequence and keeps the additional overhead of the insole-enhanced module small, making it compatible with real-time visual pose backbones. V. E XPERIMENTS

B. Baselines and Compared Variants 

We compare the proposed method with a vision-only tem-poral baseline and several fusion variants. The vision-only temporal baseline uses the same visual backbone output and temporal modeling strategy as our method, but removes the insole stream. This baseline is important because it separates the benefit of pressure–inertial sensing from the benefit of temporal smoothing alone. For each visual backbone, the baseline is trained and evaluated in the same root-relative 22-joint output space as the proposed model. We also evaluate a naive temporal fusion variant, which di-rectly concatenates visual and insole features before temporal modeling without part decoupling or cross-branch coordina-tion. This variant tests whether simple multimodal concatena-tion is sufficient. In addition, we report ablations without the global coordinator, without pressure, and without acceleration. These variants examine the contribution of cross-branch infor-mation exchange and the two sensing modalities. All compared methods use the same data split, window length, training objective, and evaluation metrics for a fair comparison. 

C. Implementation Details 

All models are trained on 120-frame windows. The training and validation windows are sampled with a step size of 5 frames, while testing uses a step size of 1 frame to obtain dense frame-level predictions. The proposed fusion model uses two-layer LSTM temporal modules with a hidden dimension of 256. The upper- and lower-body branches use separate temporal memories, and the global coordinator predicts resid-ual updates for both branches. We train the model using AdamW with cosine learning-rate scheduling and select the best checkpoint according to validation PA-MPJPE. For fair comparison, the vision-only temporal baseline and fusion variants are trained under the same data split, window length, and evaluation protocol. 

D. Evaluation Metrics 

We report MPJPE and PA-MPJPE for pose accuracy, and jerk-based jitter for temporal smoothness. MPJPE measures the mean Euclidean distance between the predicted and ref-erence joints in the root-relative skeleton space, while PA-MPJPE is computed after Procrustes alignment. Jitter is com-puted from the third-order temporal derivative of the predicted joint trajectories and reflects high-frequency motion instability. For gait-related evaluation, we report stance accuracy, step-distance error, and cadence error. These metrics are computed from the reconstructed foot trajectories and are used to ex-amine whether the estimated pose preserves contact-sensitive motion properties beyond joint-level accuracy. 

To ensure biomechanical validity and clinical relevance, gait-related metrics are evaluated exclusively on stan-dard cyclic locomotion sequences within the test split (specifically, walking and jogging). This selective evaluation prevents the introduction of ill-defined noise from static or non-locomotive activities (such as sitting, eating, or upper-body exercises), where the concepts of stride geometry and gait cycle are physically inapplicable. 

Stance accuracy evaluates frame-level left/right foot-contact correctness. For each frame, we infer the predicted contact state from the reconstructed foot trajectory and compare it with the reference contact label derived from the motion-capture system. The smart-insole pressure signal is not used as the ground-truth contact label for this metric, preventing the evaluation from directly reusing the input sensing modality. Step-distance error measures the accuracy of step geometry. We first identify heel-strike events from the reference contact sequence. At each detected heel-strike frame, we compute the horizontal distance between the left and right ankle joints. The reported error is the mean absolute difference between the predicted and reference step distances. Cadence error measures the accuracy of temporal step rhythm. Cadence is computed as the number of detected 7

steps per minute over an evaluated locomotion sequence. Together, these gait-related metrics strictly evaluate whether the reconstructed motion is useful for downstream gait and contact analysis. 

E. Main Pose Estimation Results 

Table II reports the main pose estimation results across three evaluation scopes: clean visual conditions, lower-body occlusion, and visual dropout (out-of-frame). Under clean visual observations on standard subsets, the proposed method consistently improves MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and jitter across MediaPipe, YOLOPose26, and SAT-HMR. This indicates that the insole stream provides useful comple-mentary cues even when the visual input is not deliberately degraded. The improvement becomes more pronounced under lower-body occlusion. Since the bottom 30% of the video frame is masked, the visual backbones receive weaker lower-body evidence, while the insole stream still provides pressure and acceleration cues related to foot-ground interaction. As a result, the proposed method substantially reduces pose error and temporal jitter compared with the vision-only temporal baselines. Crucially, we also report quantitative results under the ex-treme visual-dropout condition (Table II, bottom row). During visual dropouts, the subject temporarily leaves the camera view. Because vision-only baselines completely lose physical observability and degenerate to static holding or unpredictable extrapolation without image evidence, they cannot serve as a meaningful comparative baseline (denoted as ‘—‘). In stark contrast, the proposed insole-enhanced method reliably lever-ages continuous plantar pressure and acceleration to maintain active tracking of the lower limbs, achieving an MPJPE of 123.63 mm and a PA-MPJPE of 59.65 mm. This further supports our core claim: unobtrusive foot sensing provides critical physical constraints exactly when monocular cameras fail. 

F. Gait-Related Evaluation 

Table III reports gait-related measurements specifically eval-uated on cyclic locomotion (walking and jogging). In the clean setting, the proposed method improves stance accuracy for all three backbones and reduces both step-distance and cadence errors. These results indicate that the insole-enhanced model does not merely reduce average joint error, but fundamentally improves foot-contact timing, relative step geometry, and step rhythm. On average, stance accuracy increases by 8.99 percentage points, step-distance error de-creases by 27.8%, and cadence error decreases by 50.9%. The improvement is larger under lower-body occlusion. Vision-only baselines produce less reliable foot-contact and step-event estimates when lower-body observations are de-graded. In contrast, the insole-enhanced model remains re-markably stable because plantar pressure and foot acceleration directly encode contact and foot dynamics. Under occlusion, the average stance accuracy increases by 16.25 percentage points.

H. Real-Occlusion Stress Test 

We further evaluate the proposed method on two held-out real-occlusion sequences recorded with a physical desk. In both sequences, the desk is placed between the camera and the subject so that the lower body is fully invisible to the monocular camera. The first sequence is a stationary lower-body occlusion case, where the subject keeps the lower body nearly static while performing free upper-body movements. This setting tests whether the model can avoid visually induced lower-body drift when only upper-body motion is observable. The second sequence is a desk-occluded walking case, where the subject walks while the legs and feet remain hidden behind the desk. This setting is more challenging because the visual stream provides little direct evidence about step timing, foot displacement, or lower-limb articulation. The synthetic and real occlusion settings serve different purposes. Synthetic lower-body occlusion masks the same actions used in the standard protocol, enabling controlled comparison with paired clean sequences. In contrast, desk-induced occlusion introduces physical scene-level obstruction, viewpoint-dependent visual ambiguity, and unpaired action sequences. Therefore, the real-occlusion actions are not used as a standard benchmark with clean counterparts, but as a stress test for practical obstacle-induced visual degradation. As shown in Table V, the proposed method remains sub-stantially more robust than the vision-only temporal baseline under physical occlusion. It reduces MPJPE from 93.17 mm to 62.06 mm, PA-MPJPE from 65.58 mm to 45.79 mm, and jitter from 178.25 to 94.00 m/s3. These results complement the synthetic occlusion experiment: the synthetic setting provides controlled comparison under the same actions, while the real-occlusion setting verifies robustness under practical obstacle-induced visual degradation.

J. Short Visual-Dropout Analysis 

In the previous sections (Tables II and III), we quantita-tively demonstrated the exceptional robustness of the proposed method under short visual-dropout conditions. To intuitively understand the underlying tracking mechanism during these extreme visual degradation scenarios, we conduct a qualitative behavioral analysis. This setting is fundamentally different from lower-body occlusion. Under lower-body occlusion, the visual backbone still receives partial image evidence (e.g., upper body or surrounding context). During out-of-frame dropout, however, the visual stream provides absolutely no valid observation of the ongoing motion. In our implementation, the vision-only baseline therefore completely freezes, degenerating to last-observation holding or unpredictable temporal-prior extrapo-lation. As illustrated in Figure 4, the rows show continuous tempo-ral frames transitioning from an in-frame moment to an out-of-frame dropout. When the visual stream becomes unavailable, the vision-only MediaPipe baseline remains stuck in its last visible posture. However, our proposed model continues to accurately update lower-body articulation (e.g., knee lifting and step advancing) using continuous plantar pressure and foot-mounted acceleration. This result visually corroborates the quantitative metrics: while foot sensing is not intended to replace vision for infinite long-duration blind tracking, it decisively provides the essential physical observability needed to survive short, inevitable camera failures in real-world de-ployments.

VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This paper presented a part-decoupled vision–pressure– inertial fusion framework for real-time monocular human pose estimation. We investigated whether sparse plantar-pressure and foot-acceleration signals can provide complementary cues when monocular visual evidence is incomplete or unavailable. The framework maps heterogeneous visual observations into a unified root-relative skeleton space, incorporates foot sensing into lower-body temporal modeling, and uses a lightweight global coordinator to maintain whole-body consistency. Experimental results show that the insole-enhanced frame-work improves pose accuracy and temporal smoothness over vision-only temporal baselines under clean conditions, with larger gains under lower-body occlusion. Analysis of short visual dropouts further demonstrates the value of continuous foot-contact and foot-dynamic cues. The method also im-proves contact-sensitive gait measurements, including stance accuracy, step-distance error, and cadence error. These results indicate that sparse pressure–inertial foot sensing provides useful physical priors for robust human motion estimation. Although the experiments use I-SOL smart insoles, the for-mulation only assumes synchronized sparse plantar-pressure and foot-mounted acceleration streams with comparable tem-poral resolution. The method is therefore not limited to a specific insole model. Data organization, action definitions, and preprocessing scripts will be released to support repro-ducibility. The method nevertheless has several limitations. First, it estimates root-relative pose rather than long-term global tra-jectories. Because the current sensing setup lacks reliable global orientation, extended visual dropouts may still cause drift. Second, broader evaluation across users, environments, and motion types is needed. Third, downstream applications 10 

such as ergonomic risk assessment, rehabilitation scoring, and abnormal gait screening require further validation. Future work will improve long-horizon robustness through lightweight global motion constraints and explicit contact-state modeling. We will also explore mobile and edge deployment with real-time visual front-ends and Bluetooth insole streams, and extend evaluation to industrial monitoring, home rehabil-itation, and long-term daily activity assessment.
