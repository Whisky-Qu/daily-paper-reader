Abstract 

Humanoid robots operating in human-centric environments must coordinate the entire body to traverse tight, cluttered spaces while maintaining balance. Existing methods often struggle to jointly consider dynamic stability and collision avoidance from high-dimensional perceptual inputs. Moreover, for humanoids, safe behavior is inherently multimodal: the same obstacle may admit multiple valid traversals, which is difficult to represent with a single deterministic policy. To address these challenges, we propose SWAP ( Steerable Whole-body Avoidance Policy ), a hierarchical framework that separates collision-aware decision making from stabilization. A 

1arXiv:2610.25711v1 [cs.RO] 19 Oct 2026 high-level, perception-conditioned diffusion policy generates multimodal, collision-aware mo-tion proposals, while a low-level whole-body RL controller tracks these proposals and maintains dynamic balance. To efficiently train the high-level policy, we obtain scalable supervision via hindsight labeling: we roll out the low-level policy under randomized commands, place obsta-cles only in the space that each executed motion did not sweep, so the motion is collision-free by construction, and re-render the perception the robot would have seen. At deployment, Monte Carlo sampling with simple cost signals steers the diffusion model toward safe, task-aligned behaviors without heavy reward tuning. Experiments in simulation and on a real Unitree G1 humanoid show reliable avoidance with static and dynamic obstacles. Test-time steering further enables customization, improving tar-geted rewards (e.g., speed, orientation, or posture) by 34% on average while maintaining a high collision avoidance rate. Together, these results support a practical solution to humanoid whole-body collision avoidance with test-time steerability. Project website:

# 1 Introduction 

Humanoid robots possess the kinematic flexibility to navigate cluttered, human-centric environ-ments in ways that simpler platforms cannot: ducking under obstacles, twisting through narrow gaps, and sidestepping around barriers while maintaining balance. Exploiting this flexibility requires 

whole-body coordination —orchestrating not just footsteps, but whole-body posture in concert [Ra-dosavovic et al., 2024, Cheng et al., 2024 ]. This capability represents both the defining advantage of the humanoid form factor and its central control challenge. Yet, achieving reliable whole-body obstacle avoidance remains fundamentally difficult precisely be-cause of the high degree-of-freedom, inherently unstable nature of humanoid robots. A successful policy must simultaneously satisfy dynamic feasibility, control stability, and collision avoidance—a combination that existing methods struggle to achieve. Reinforcement learning struggles with the multimodality of avoidance: many valid traversals exist, but reward-driven optimization collapses to a narrow mode. Shaped, multi-term objectives introduce spurious local minima and reward-sensitive behavior as shown in both humanoid and other legged robots [Wang et al., 2025b, He et al., 2024 ]. Model-based planners face analogous issues—whole-body planning with hand-crafted costs is nonconvex and initialization-sensitive, often yielding stiff, conservative motions that underutilize the humanoid’s flexibility [Sun et al., 2025, Zucker et al., 2013 ]. Behavior cloning can learn natural motions from demonstrations but requires large-scale data; teleoperation on humanoids is difficult and does not scale. In manipulation, imitation-based generative policies can represent multimodal manipulation while achieving collision avoidance, but still depend on demonstration coverage [Li et al., 2025, Jung et al., 2025, Ze et al., 2024, Chi et al., 2023 ]. Moreover, policies trained under any of these paradigms cannot easily adapt to changing objectives at deployment—any shift in user preference (e.g., prioritizing certain gestures) typically demands retraining. In this work, we propose SWAP ( Steerable Whole-body Avoidance Policy ), a hierarchical framework that decouples high-level collision-aware decision making from low-level locomotion control. This separation is key to resolving the stability and collision avoidance dilemma that plagues joint opti-mization: the low-level RL controller focuses solely on stable trajectory tracking, while the high-level planner handles collision-aware motion generation. For the high-level policy, inspired by diffusion-based whole-body motion synthesis [Huang et al., 2025, Liao et al., 2025 ], we adopt a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) [Peebles and Xie, 2023 ] conditioned on LiDAR observations and trained on tra-jectories derived from the LAFAN1 human motion dataset [Harvey et al., 2020 ]. The high-level diffusion policy generates collision-aware whole-body trajectories from perception observations, which are tracked by a low-level RL locomotion controller. To obtain collision labels without teleoperation, we use a hindsight labeling [Andrychowicz et al., 2017, Gu et al., 2024 ] pipeline in simulation. We roll out the low-level controller under randomized commands, then run geometric collision checking along the executed motion by testing the robot’s swept volume against the environment, producing collision-free labels for each trajectory. This yields scalable collision-aware supervision while avoiding reward shaping: collision enters only as a label on motion-prior trajectories, which helps retain multimodality and naturalness while learning to avoid obstacles. This self-supervised pipeline provides a more scalable way of collecting data and bypasses the need for teleoperation. At deployment, we further steer the diffusion sampling process using Monte Carlo sampling with lightweight cost signals. Together, these components transform avoidance from a fixed reactive mapping into a flexible, steerable generative task, enabling zero-shot adaptation to new objectives without retraining. The key capability of SWAP is not any single motion primitive, such as ducking or sidestepping, but the ability to produce collision-aware whole-body proposals that remain steerable at deployment . A user can plug in analytical objectives such as speed, heading, height, or posture, or use a learned collision-aware Q-function, and the same trained diffusion prior can adapt by selecting among its sampled trajectory modes. This distinguishes SWAP from approaches that require a new reward, curriculum, or demonstration set whenever the deployment objective changes. Our main contributions are three-fold: 1. A hierarchical framework combining a perception-conditioned DiT with an RL-based controller for whole-body obstacle avoidance, validated on the Unitree G1 in simulation and real-world deployment. 2. A hindsight labeling pipeline that uses synthetic environments to generate collision-aware supervision for the high-level diffusion policy at scale, without teleoperation. 3. A lightweight test-time steering mechanism that adapts to new objectives without retraining, improving targeted metrics by 34% on average. We validate SWAP extensively in simulation and on a Unitree G1 humanoid equipped with Li-DAR sensors in the real world. Our experiments demonstrate reliable avoidance of both static and dynamic obstacles, with the robot exhibiting diverse whole-body maneuvers including ducking, sidestepping, and twisting. Together, these results establish a practical framework for humanoid whole-body collision avoidance that integrates naturally with downstream navigation stacks. 32 Related Work 

Our approach lies at the intersection of learning-based locomotion, generative models for control, and safe humanoid navigation. We review relevant work in each area. 

2.1 Learning-Based Humanoid Locomotion 

Humanoid locomotion has seen significant progress through reinforcement learning (RL), mov-ing from basic bipedal balance [Radosavovic et al., 2024 ] to perceptive locomotion in rough ter-rains [Zhuang et al., 2024 ]. Recent hierarchical approaches, such as HWC-Loco [Lin et al., 2025 ],decompose the problem into high-level planning and low-level control to achieve robust gait. Simi-larly, HumanPlus [Fu et al., 2024 ] demonstrates the effectiveness of shadowing and imitation learn-ing for whole-body coordination. Generative motion priors, such as NaturalGMP [Zhang et al., 2025a ], further enhance motion naturalness without hand-crafted style rewards. While these re-search efforts provide a solid foundation for mobility, the integration of these motor skills into active collision avoidance in complex, cluttered environments remains a central challenge. 

2.2 Diffusion Models for Motion Planning 

Diffusion models have emerged as powerful generative priors for robot policy learning. In manipu-lation, diffusion policies have shown strong performance [Chi et al., 2023, Ze et al., 2024 ], including on humanoid robots [Ze et al., 2025 ]. For navigation, NavDP [Cai et al., 2026 ] combines a diffu-sion policy with privileged information guidance and a safety critic, achieving successful sim-to-real navigation transfer. For humanoid control specifically, recent works leverage diffusion as a motion prior. DreamCon-trol [Kalaria et al., 2026 ] uses a diffusion prior over human motions to help an RL policy solve long-horizon humanoid tasks. Most relevant to our work, BeyondMimic [Liao et al., 2025 ] demon-strates that guided diffusion can achieve zero-shot task adaptation, including obstacle avoidance, by applying cost-guided sampling to a diffusion controller. While these frameworks excel at gener-ating feasible behaviors, steering them toward task-specific safety in cluttered environments often requires expert data or costly online optimization; this motivates our hindsight collision labeling as an efficient supervision signal for systematic whole-body obstacle avoidance. 

2.3 Safe Navigation for Legged Robots 

Ensuring safety in humanoid navigation remains challenging, as it requires satisfying dynamic feasibility, control stability, and collision avoidance simultaneously. Control barrier functions (CBFs) [Ames et al., 2019 ] provide formal safety guarantees but can be conservative. Wang et al. 

[2025b ] formulate humanoid locomotion as a constrained MDP with CBF costs, achieving safe and comfortable walking in crowds with the perception module of Omni-Perception [Wang et al., 2025a ]. However, such constraint-based approaches often require careful tuning and may not generalize across diverse environments. To address these limitations, reactive approaches like HumanoidPF [Xue et al., 2026 ] encode humanoid-obstacle relationships into an artificial potential field to guide RL policies. While ef-fective for narrow passage traversal, such methods typically rely on explicit geometric mappings and require intensive reward engineering to balance stability with safety. Other learning-based ap-proaches to safety include training with collision penalties, using safety critics as seen in NavDP [Cai et al., 2026 ], or employing curriculum learning. For example, Kim et al. [2024 ] propose ARMOR, 4which uses an inverse hindsight strategy by synthetically placing obstacles around known collision-free human motions to guarantee safe demonstrations. In contrast, our approach introduces a physically grounded hindsight labeling strategy derived from the robot’s own swept volume. This enables the policy to internalize diverse, multimodal avoidance maneuvers consistent with its kinematic limits, eliminating the need for manual reward shaping or external expert demonstrations. 

Positioning. Prior systems provide important pieces of the humanoid avoidance stack, but none combines whole-body obstacle avoidance with plug-and-play test-time objectives. Motion-imitation and generative locomotion methods provide natural behavior priors but do not directly solve perception-conditioned collision avoidance. ARMOR [Kim et al., 2024 ] and HumanoidPF [Xue et al., 2026 ] target perception-driven avoidance, but their deployment behavior is tied to the trained objective or hand-designed potential field. Model-based planners such as SPARK [Sun et al., 2025 ] can optimize explicit costs, but scaling them to real-time whole-body humanoid motion with learned perception remains difficult. SWAP instead learns a multimodal diffusion prior over feasible whole-body commands and adapts online by scoring sampled trajectories, preserving the hierarchy while avoiding task-specific retraining. 

# 3 Steerable Hierarchical Policy for Whole-Body Obstacle Avoidance 

We consider a humanoid robot navigating cluttered, human-centric environments. The robot must reach goal locations while avoiding collisions using its full body—not only via footstep adjustments, but through coordinated motions of the torso, arms, and legs. Appendix A summarizes our notation. 

Why Hierarchical Control? Directly generating joint-level commands with diffusion models poses challenges for humanoid control: the extensive computation of iterative denoising struggles to meet high-frequency requirements (30–50 Hz), and end-to-end policies must simultaneously learn colli-sion avoidance and dynamic stability—competing objectives that are difficult to balance. We adopt a hierarchical decomposition: a high-level diffusion planner generates trajectory commands at lower frequency, while a low-level RL controller runs at high frequency for stable execution. As we show in Section 4, this separation yields significantly stronger performance compared to end-to-end diffusion baselines.

3.2 Hindsight Labeling for High-Level Diffusion Policy 

Training the high-level diffusion policy requires perception–trajectory pairs. We obtain supervision via hindsight labeling using executed trajectory replay: we first generate diverse motions by rolling out randomized commands through the low-level controller, then retrospectively construct a labeled obstacle representation from the swept volume of the executed motion, and finally re-render per-ception observations along that trajectory. 

Procedure. We first roll out commands from a human motion prior in simulation: we sample upper-body motion segments from LAFAN1 and concatenate them with randomized base waypoints (position /heading /height targets) to form a desired high-level command sequence {τt }Tt=0. Exe-cuting {τt } with the frozen low-level controller yields an actual joint trajectory {qt }Tt=0. We use this executed trajectory, not the commanded reference, to compute the swept volume—the set of workspace points occupied by the robot body over time. Multimodality and Natural Motion. A key advantage of hindsight-augmented training is multi-modal behavior. Consider two obstacle configurations O1 and O2 from different base rollouts: their intersection O1 ∩ O 2 may admit multiple collision-free trajectories, each originally labeled for one configuration but valid for both. # 4 Experiments 

We evaluate SWAP in both simulation and real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Our experiments address the following questions: 1. Can SWAP successfully solve the obstacle avoidance problem in both simulation and the real world? 2. How does hindsight labeling benefit the data? Is it scalable and how well does it generalize? 3. How does test-time steering benefit the framework? Is it easy to integrate as a plug-in module? 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Task Design. We use Isaac Gym [Makoviychuk et al., 2021 ] for physics simulation with a Unitree G1 humanoid model (29 DoF). We evaluate on five obstacle avoidance tasks: Walk sideways —navigating through a narrow lateral gap between two close obstacles; Ducking —lowering the body to pass under a head-height horizontal bar; Lift hand to pass —raising one arm to clear an arm-height obstacle; 

Fast dynamic obstacle —avoiding a ball swinging on a rope; and Slow dynamic obstacle —upper-body adjustments to avoid a slowly moving obstacle while the base remains stationary. 

LiDAR Simulation. We simulate a spinning LiDAR sensor mounted on the robot’s torso, providing 360-degree coverage with 16 vertical channels [Wang et al., 2025a,b ]. Point clouds are generated at 10 Hz with realistic noise characteristics. 

Training Details. We train the low-level controller with PPO [Schulman et al., 2017 ] for 50K iter-ations. The perception encoder is trained for 200K steps on 8 × L40S GPUs (6 hours) and produces a 32-dimensional embedding. The high-level diffusion model is trained for 500K steps on 8 × L40S GPUs (5 hours). Test-time steering uses N = 32 Monte Carlo samples. Unless otherwise stated, all static obstacle tasks use the same high-level diffusion policy and perception encoder; adaptation across speed, heading, and posture preferences is performed only through test-time steering. Dy-namic obstacle tasks use a separately trained high-level policy because the 10 Hz LiDAR stream is insufficient for high-frequency dynamic obstacle state estimation. 

Sim2Sim Validation. To validate simulation fidelity, we perform sim2sim transfer by deploying policies trained in Isaac Gym to MuJoCo [Todorov et al., 2012 ], confirming consistent behavior across physics engines.

4.3 Analysis of Hindsight Labeling (Q2) 

We investigate three aspects of hindsight labeling: (1) its effectiveness and scalability, (2) the role of cost augmentation ratio, and (3) generalization to unseen scenarios. 

Hindsight labeling is effective and scalable. Figure 6(a) shows that both increasing the number of base trajectories and applying data augmentation consistently improve success rate. The aug-mentation process requires only simulation rollouts with automatic collision annotation, making it straightforward to scale data collection. 

Cost augmentation ratio is critical. Hindsight labeling augments the dataset with both safe (pos-itive) and collision (negative) trajectory samples. The negative samples provide a correction signal that guides the Q-function away from collision-prone behaviors. However, the ratio of negative to positive samples significantly impacts performance. As shown in Figure 6(b), a 10% negative ratio achieves optimal performance by balancing safety awareness with behavioral diversity. Excessive negative samples (50%) undermine the quality of the training distribution, leading to overly con-servative policies that degrade at high augmentation levels. Notably, when combined with test-time steering, performance saturates earlier (around 50 × augmentation) compared to the no-steering setting. This indicates that steering effectively exploits the Q-function learned from hindsight data, reducing the data requirements for the diffusion policy. 

Generalization to unseen scenarios. The results of Figure 6 are evaluated on held-out obstacle configurations disjoint from those used during training. Here, “unseen” means new wall positions, yaw angles, gap locations, and gap sizes sampled from the same distribution family with a separate random seed. Figure 7 visualizes the closest training configurations for representative held-out scenes. Although the closest training obstacle overlaps 84–90% with the test configuration, the remaining geometric differences are sufficient to change the feasible whole-body maneuver. This supports the claim that the perception encoder learns a spatial representation useful for obstacle reasoning rather than memorizing exact obstacle grids. We evaluate six such objectives: base speed, turning direction, base goal reaching, base height, and left /right arm lifting. This plug-and-play design requires no retraining: users specify the tar-get posture, and Monte Carlo sampling selects the trajectory that best satisfies it. Compared to gradient-based guidance, which requires careful tuning per objective, sampling-based selection is robust across objectives and less sensitive to hyperparameter choices. 

Objective dimensionality affects steering effectiveness. Figure 8 shows that, with dynamic obsta-cles, base-related objectives (speed, turning direction, goal reaching) achieve larger improvements (41–44%) than upper-body objectives (arm lifting, 34–35%). With static obstacles, all six objectives improve by a similar 29–31%. Lower-dimensional objectives (1–2 dimensions for base control) are easier to optimize via sampling, whereas higher-dimensional objectives (arm configurations) are more sensitive for gradient-based guidance. The base objectives gain more with dynamic obstacles because the feasible non-collision set is larger and the diffusion policy’s output distribution is wider, providing more diverse candidates for selection. 

Steering induces distinct avoidance modes. Figure 9 compares three steering configurations: holding both arms (a), nominal without steering (b), and turning left (c). Two key observations emerge. First, steering objectives are not orthogonal—when steering to hold both arms (a), the turning objective, which is not steered, drops below its nominal value, indicating that the diffusion policy couples these behaviors rather than treating them as independent joint-level controls. Second, steering does not merely override specific joints but guides the robot into fundamentally different avoidance strategies. The hold-arm objective induces lateral sidestepping behavior, while the left-turning objective triggers upper-body adjustments with accompanying postural changes. This holistic response demonstrates that Monte Carlo steering operates at the level of motion modes rather than individual degrees of freedom. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented SWAP, a hierarchical diffusion-based framework for whole-body obstacle avoidance in humanoid robots. By combining a perception-conditioned diffusion planner with a low-level RL 15 locomotion controller, our approach enables natural, multimodal avoidance behaviors that exploit the full kinematic capabilities of a humanoid body. Hindsight data collection automatically generates collision-aware supervision without manual labeling, while test-time Monte Carlo steering enables robust navigation with minimal reward engineering. Experiments on the Unitree G1 demonstrate significant improvements over baselines, with diverse avoidance strategies—sidestepping, ducking, twisting—emerging naturally from the learned motion prior. 

Limitations. Our framework has two primary limitations. First, test-time steering currently operates with a limited batch size ( N = 32 Monte Carlo samples) due to running the high-level diffusion policy at 20 Hz; larger batch sizes could improve steering capability for more challenging environments. Second, our perception module generalizes to held-out obstacle configurations within our training distribution, but may struggle with highly diverse unseen environments that differ significantly from training scenarios. 

Future Work. Promising directions include deeper integration with foundation navigation models, where hindsight labeling combined with procedural scene generation could produce more diverse training environments. Additional directions include extending SWAP to loco-manipulation tasks, incorporating vision or language for semantic navigation, and investigating efficient diffusion archi-tectures to increase sampling throughput.

Based on these results, our deployment configuration uses an action chunk length of 5, a sample batch size of N =32, and the CUDA + IO binding + FP16 execution provider. The high-level diffusion policy runs as fast as possible at approximately 20 Hz: at each planning step it generates 32 candidate trajectory chunks, selects the best one via Monte Carlo steering, and writes it to a shared action buffer. The low-level RL controller reads from this buffer at 50 Hz, converting the selected trajectory waypoint into motor commands.
