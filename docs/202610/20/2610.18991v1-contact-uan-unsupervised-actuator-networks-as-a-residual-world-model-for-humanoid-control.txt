Abstract: Humanoid policies trained in simulation can transfer qualitatively to
hardware while failing to transfer the force interactions learned during training,
compromising torque efficiency, load regulation, and robustness to impacts. This
failure can arise from actuator sim-to-real gaps because forces depend on actua-
tor torques. We introduce Contact-UAN, a residual world model that preserves a
simulator’s physics priors while correcting actuator dynamics to align simulated
rollouts with recorded hardware rollouts. For humanoid motion with contact, this
requires contact-consistent replay: if hardware playback in simulation begins with
misaligned contact wrenches, the residual model learns to correct reset artifacts
rather than actuator discrepancies. Using only onboard proprioception, Contact-
UAN infers the simulator-side stance-foot pose needed to initialize replay with
consistent contact forces. We show that a UAN trained on 6.5 minutes of real-
world walking data generalizes to tasks that benefit from accurate force and im-
pulse regulation and produces policies that transfer seamlessly to the real world,
yielding 20% lower running torque, 43% lower force-tracking error, extreme com-
pliance, accurate impedance control, and dynamic, robust jumps.

Introduction
Humanoid robots can perform diverse tasks by virtue of their general-purpose morphology, and
recent advances in learning-based control within physics simulators have enabled increasingly com-
plex behaviors [1–4]. For sim-to-real humanoid control, qualitative motion transfer is only a coarse
measure of success; the robot must also generate force profiles with the magnitude and timing
needed to move efficiently, regulate loads, and absorb impacts. Policy optimization can exploit
imperfect actuator dynamics, producing policies that may transfer qualitative motions but not force
interactions, leading to excessive torques, poor commanded-force tracking, or failures during high-
impulse phases (Section 4.2). Because these forces are mediated by joint torques, we study how
improving the actuator model used during policy training reduces force-level sim-to-real gaps.
The actuator model fidelity required for sim-to-real transfer is task-dependent: simple models may
suffice for quasi-static behaviors, while behaviors such as efficient running, force tracking, jumping,
and landing depend on accurate actuator response, force generation, and impact absorption. Analytic
simulators [5, 6] provide useful physical structure, yet they often approximate actuator effects such
as torque saturation and load-dependent nonlinear friction, precisely the effects that shape ground-
force generation, external-force regulation, and impact absorption [7, 8]. Domain randomization
improves robustness by training over parameter ranges, but it can produce conservative policies that
under-utilize hardware [9–12]. Online adaptation and SysID can recover performance by estimating
dynamics parameters, but require choosing which simulator parameters to randomize and identify [1,
2, 13–17]. Fully learned world models (WMs) take another approach: fit dynamics directly from
data and can, in principle, capture effects omitted by analytical simulators [18–21], but must model
the full robot-environment dynamics needed for policy optimization. We therefore follow prior
work in residual modeling and actuator learning [7, 22–27]: retain the physical priors of an existing
simulator and learn only the sim-to-real gap in the actuator command channel.
Learning such an actuator-channel residual to improve downstream transfer requires a training sig-
nal. Supervised actuator networks use measured motor torques as this signal [7], but reliable output
torque sensing is unavailable on many humanoid platforms. Without such labels, residual methods
can use trajectory replay-based training instead: reset the simulator to a recorded hardware state,
apply the recorded commands, and train residual commands so the simulated rollout matches the
recorded hardware trajectory [8, 28]. Critically, this objective isolates modeling error only if re-
play begins from a hardware-equivalent simulator state. Otherwise, the residual model may learn to
correct simulation reset artifacts rather than underlying actuator discrepancies.
Matching a given hardware state in simulation is a kinematics problem for suspended, free-space
motions [8, 15].
However, such motion lacks the contact information needed to capture load-
dependent actuator behavior during stance, force generation, impact, and landing. He et al. [28]
collect data with contacts and use a motion capture system to provide ground-truth body poses.
However, consider a humanoid robot standing still: on hardware, ground-reaction forces balance its
weight, but initializing a soft-contact simulator with the feet exactly at the ground surface may not
produce equivalent forces. Instead, matching the hardware force interaction requires introducing
slight ground penetration via adjustments to the simulated foot height and orientation, not a precise
height measurement. This is necessary because millimeter-scale foot-height and degree-scale orien-
tation errors can substantially change simulated contact wrenches (Fig. 3). These observations point
to a critical need to address contact-consistent state initialization for replay-based residual learning.
We introduce Contact-UAN, a residual world-modeling approach to training humanoid policies for
force- and impulse-sensitive behaviors. The core contribution is contact-consistent state initializa-
tion from proprioception, which enables replay-based residual learning from hardware trajectories
with contacts. From proprioception alone, Contact-UAN infers the simulator-side stance-foot pose,
including the small ground penetration and tilt needed to initialize replay with contact wrenches
consistent with the hardware trajectory, together with the base velocity needed to complete the reset
state. With replay initialized in this contact-consistent state, the UAN residual can learn to correct
true actuator modeling errors in simulation.

We evaluate Contact-UAN on a Unitree G1 using the state-of-the-art BeyondMimic G1 parameters
as the nominal simulator [2, 29], and compare policies trained with and without UAN (Section 4).
We choose downstream tasks that expose distinct actuator-sensitive force and impulse regimes: run-
ning requires torque-efficient ground-force generation, force and impedance control require accurate
external-force regulation, and jumping requires takeoff impulse and landing absorption. A single
UAN trained from 6.5 minutes of real-world walking data improves transfer across these behaviors.
Training in this corrected simulator yields a running policy that preserves velocity tracking while re-
ducing hardware torque usage by 20% relative to the no-UAN baseline, showing that the UAN gives
policy learning a more accurate torque signal. The same residual reduces force-tracking error by
43% and improves jumping: both reference-tracking policies transfer, while task-reward fine-tuning
for higher jumps transfers with UAN but fails without it (Section 4.2). Sim-to-sim experiments
reveal the importance of contact-consistent initialization and show that Contact-UAN remains effec-
tive under larger actuator gaps (Sections 4.1 and 4.3).
2
Method
Contact-UAN has three stages (Fig. 4): relabel hardware data for contact-consistent replay initial-
ization (Section 2.1); train the UAN residual (Section 2.2); and train downstream policies in the
augmented simulator (Section 2.3). Only the task policy runs on hardware.
2.1
Contact-Consistent Replay Initialization
Accurate replay requires each window to begin with simulated contacts that reproduce hardware
contact wrenches. Onboard sensing provides neither the contact wrench nor absolute base height,
and reconstructing global pose is inadequate because soft-contact wrenches depend on simulator-
specific, millimeter-scale foot penetration and tilt (Fig. 3). We therefore infer the simulator-side
contact-consistent reset state from proprioception.
Contact affects joint motion and IMU response, making contact wrenches inferable from a short
proprioceptive history [4, 30]. The state estimators are trained in simulation and assume flat terrain,
enabling use of a root-xy and -yaw invariant frame. They predict contact probabilities, stance-foot
pose, and base linear velocity from a K-step history of IMU measurements, joint states, and previous
joint targets.
Although proprioception could be used to estimate contact force directly, a force estimate alone
does not specify the simulator state used at reset. We therefore regress simulator-side stance-foot
pose, learning in one step the map from load-dependent proprioception to the penetrating height
and orientation that reproduce the contact wrenches. Initialization from this pose avoids separately

We first estimate contact status and train the pose regressors only on stance samples. Swing poses
largely map to zero contact wrench, so their variation dominates the objective without informing
the contact model. In stance, however, small pose differences strongly affect the wrench: in our
MuJoCo model (Table 4) [6], a +2 mm root-height offset reduces normal force by roughly 50%,
while a −2◦pitch offset changes the support moment by roughly 45% (Fig. 3). Stance conditioning
therefore focuses the regression on the contact-sensitive variation needed at reset (Figs. 5 and 15).
Each confident stance-foot estimate yields a candidate root height and orientation through forward
kinematics. We fuse candidates using contact-probability weights and discard windows with no con-
fident contact; full equations are in Appendix A.4. Because the estimators are trained in simulation,
their key transfer assumption is that load-dependent proprioceptive cues remain informative across
domains. Figure 5A probes this assumption in controlled sim-to-sim: the stance estimator, trained
in the base simulator, achieves low contact-height error in the hard target simulator despite actuator
parameter perturbations unseen during training. Figure 5B and Appendix Figure 14 provide comple-
mentary end-to-end evidence through improved hardware replay. The estimators are used only for
offline relabeling and are absent from downstream policy training and deployment. Together with
estimated base velocity, the fused root pose initializes contact-consistent UAN replay, enabling the
UAN to learn to correct actuator discrepancies rather than simulator reset artifacts.

To improve data efficiency, we share network parameters across joints with identical actuator hard-
ware [7, 8]. Each actuator type has its own two-layer MLP with hidden dimensions (128, 128) and
ELU activations. This enforces a hardware-structured residual model while having enough capacity
to model actuator-specific nonlinearities.
Data collection. We first train a Walk-These-Ways (WTW)-style locomotion policy [31] in the
nominal simulator without a UAN, using domain randomization to support initial hardware transfer.
We then deploy this policy to collect the hardware trajectories used for UAN training, varying gait
frequency and swing height to diversify stance durations, swing speeds, and impacts. We record
onboard proprioception: base angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, and
commanded joint targets; then relabel each hardware window with base velocity and pose following
Section 2.1. Appendix C gives WTW policy-training details.
UAN task design. The UAN is trained in a replay environment built from labeled hardware win-
dows via PPO [32]. At reset, each simulated robot is initialized from a randomly sampled replay
window. We use a plane terrain, 4096 parallel environments, a 24-step (480 ms) replay horizon, and
PPO rollouts of the same length. The reward penalizes joint-position MSE, joint-velocity MSE, and
residual-rate magnitude. This replay task encourages the UAN to learn corrections that make sim-
ulated joint transitions match hardware while discouraging unnecessarily high-frequency residual
commands to aid with exploration.
2.3
Policy Training and Deployment
After UAN training, we freeze its weights and insert it into downstream task training as a deter-
ministic residual correction. We remove all robot-specific DR, but still randomize the terrain mesh,
ground friction, and external pushes to encourage robust, deployable behaviors. The task policy
outputs joint-position targets, but the simulator receives the UAN-corrected target from (2). This ex-
poses the downstream policy to actuation behavior closer to the hardware, so high-reward behaviors
must remain feasible under the corrected actuator response. At deployment, only the learned task
policy is run on the robot.

Results
4.1
Residual World Model Accuracy
Does our estimation pipeline accurately recover foot penetration? We evaluate the contact-pose
estimator in the hard sim-to-sim setting, where ground-truth contact pose is available. Fig. 5A
shows that directly regressing foot height over all gait phases yields large errors, because swing-foot
variation dominates the supervised objective. In contrast, our contact-conditioned estimator, which
first identifies contact status and then estimates pose only for confident contacts, reduces contact-
height RMSE by an order of magnitude. This shows that the proposed estimation pipeline provides
sufficiently accurate contact initialization for replay-based residual learning.
Does Contact-UAN reduce the sim-to-real gap? Relabeling improves replay over direct initial-
ization, and adding the UAN further reduces 24-step joint-position MSE on WTW hardware data
(Fig. 5B), with representative knee trajectories following held-out hardware running data (Appendix
Figure 14). On held-out running data, we replay identical recorded commands from the same rela-
beled initial states in the nominal and UAN-augmented simulators. For commanded torques, define
aggregate usage as U(τ) = P
t,j |τt,j| and relative mismatch as |U(τ sim) −U(τ hw)|/U(τ hw).
Contact-UAN reduces this all-joint aggregate mismatch by 69.2% (Fig. 6C). Figure 6A–B pro-
vides representative knee-torque traces. Because both simulators use identical commands and initial
states, the reduced torque mismatch reflects improved actuator modeling on motions with contacts.

4.2
Sim-to-Real Task Transfer
Does Contact-UAN improve torque efficiency? We compare the baseline and UAN policy at
commanded forward speeds of 1.0, 2.0, and 2.5 m s−1. For each policy and command, we run five
hardware trials over a fixed 30 ft course. We compute achieved speed from the traversal time and
log joint torques over the same interval to measure torque usage. The UAN policy preserves velocity
tracking while reducing torque usage relative to the baseline policy (Fig. 7A,B). Since both policies
achieve similar speeds, the torque reduction reflects improved efficiency rather than reduced task
execution. The UAN policy also transfers to a 3.0 m s−1 command, achieving 3.13±0.06 m s−1 on
hardware (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the baseline policy triggers motor-overheating protection and enters
safe-damping mode after a few seconds of tracking this command.
Does Contact-UAN improve force control precision? We evaluate end-effector force tracking
using a dynamometer. For each policy and commanded force, we perform four hardware trials,
increasing the commanded downward force in increments of 5 N. The robot is connected to the
dynamometer through an exercise band, requiring whole-body coordination rather than solely arm
motion to realize the commanded force (Fig. 1B). The UAN force policy reduces overall force-
tracking error by 42.8 ± 12.1% relative to the baseline (Fig. 7C). The improvement is largest at
higher commanded forces, where the robot must bend its legs and coordinate lower-body posture to
generate the desired force, increasing sensitivity to actuator-modeling errors. At zero commanded
force, the UAN policy is qualitatively compliant: the end-effector can be pulled in different direc-
tions while the body flexibly yields rather than resisting (Fig. 1C). We also compose the force-control
policy with a proprioceptive end-effector estimator and PD outer loop to perform impedance position
control. The resulting controller tracks a circular trajectory using onboard sensing alone (Fig. 1D
and Fig. 10), suggesting that Contact-UAN’s force-control gains transfer to higher-level compliant
interaction behaviors; details are in Appendix D.2.
Does Contact-UAN enable robust, dynamic motions? We train jumping policies in two stages:
reference-motion tracking [2], then task-reward fine-tuning for increased jump height.
Both
reference-tracking policies transfer to hardware, but UAN tracks better and jumps higher. After
task-reward fine-tuning, the no-UAN policy fails during landing on hardware, whereas the UAN
policy transfers with greater jump height and stable landings, even under pushes (Fig. 1E). This be-
havior is consistent with the no-UAN policy exploiting actuator-model inaccuracies. The same UAN
uses only walking training data. Appendix D.3 and Fig. 9 provide the training stages and images of
the hardware experiments.

4.3
Sim-to-Sim Evaluation
Can Contact-UAN overcome larger sim-to-real gaps? To test whether the UAN is effective when
the nominal simulator is substantially inaccurate, we evaluate transfer in sim-to-sim experiments
with progressively larger deterministic actuator perturbations. The WTW UANs were trained on 5
minutes of WTW data, while the WTW+Vel UAN was trained on 5 minutes of WTW data and 5
minutes of running data. Across the easy, medium, and hard target simulators, the baseline policy
degrades sharply as the gap increases, while policies trained with the UAN achieve expert-level
tracking error and torque usage (Fig. 8B–C). Adding task-specific running data to the UAN training
set provides little additional benefit over the WTW data alone, suggesting that this walking dataset is
sufficient to learn a residual model that generalizes to running. These results indicate that the UAN
can compensate for larger actuator-modeling errors than those observed on hardware.
How does replay initialization affect transfer? We train separate UANs on hard target data using
direct-estimator, contact-consistent, and oracle reset variants. For each UAN, we train a down-
stream policy and evaluate transfer to the target simulator. Contact-consistent resets increase 20-s
survival from 49.8% to 96.7% relative to direct-estimator resets, while component and full-oracle
resets yield 96.6–98.4% (Fig. 8A). These results show that contact-consistent initialization sub-
stantially improves downstream transfer, approaching the performance obtained with oracle resets.
Appendix G.1 details the reset protocols and oracle components; Appendix G.2 reports additional
retraining experiments with structured sensor corruption.
How do alternative architectures compare? We also examine alternative choices for the learned
dynamics model. A matched simulation comparison gives similar downstream performance for po-
sition residuals, torque residuals, and supervised torque models. Position residuals avoid torque
supervision and retain the deployed position-control interface; Appendix G.3 reports the complete
comparison. Appendix F provides an additional data-efficiency comparison with our G1 implemen-
tation of a fully learned offline world model [19, 33].
5
Limitations
Our current Contact-UAN does not capture coupled actuator effects or motor-temperature-dependent
effects [34]. Coupled actuator effects could be introduced by using a single model that observes
the whole body or through cross-attention. Temperature effects are harder to identify from short
datasets because motor temperature evolves slowly relative to the control loop. Capturing them may
also require heat modeling or additional sensors.

6
Related Work
System Identification and Domain Randomization. System identification estimates inertial and
dynamic parameters from robot data, traditionally via least-squares regression on rigid-body dy-
namics [35, 36], with recent geometric, convex, differentiable, and exploration-based variants im-
proving robustness and excitation [37–41]. Other approaches identify simulators as black boxes
using trajectory-matching [15] or distribution-matching objectives [42]. These methods remain lim-
ited by the structure of the chosen parametric model. Domain randomization instead trains over
parameter distributions [9, 10, 43–46], but wide randomization can yield conservative or ineffi-
cient policies [11, 12]. Online adaptation mitigates this by inferring latent dynamics at deploy-
ment [13, 14, 47–49]. Recent humanoid systems have therefore moved toward less randomization
and more accurate models [1, 2, 15]. Our work follows this direction by replacing actuator random-
ization with a residual actuator correction learned from proprioceptive hardware data.
Robotic World Models. Fully learned world models replace hand-designed simulators with dynam-
ics models trained from robot data. Dyna-style approaches alternate data collection, model learning,
and policy optimization through imagined rollouts [20, 21, 50], while RWM and RWM-U extend
this paradigm to legged robots in online [19] and fully offline settings [33]. Such models reduce
the structural bias of parametric simulators but require large datasets and mechanisms to prevent
exploitation outside the data distribution [18]. We instead learn a residual world model: the simula-
tor retains rigid-body dynamics, contact, and integration, while learning is restricted to the actuator
channel. This physics prior mitigates reward hacking.
Residual Models and Actuator Networks. Residual learning combines analytical physics with
learned corrections across object, system, simulator, and calibration levels [22–27, 51]. At the ac-
tuator level, supervised actuator networks predict output torques from tracking histories and have
enabled effective sim-to-real transfer [7], but require torque sensing; current-based alternatives re-
duce sensing requirements but cannot capture all gearbox effects [39]. UAN removes torque super-
vision by learning residual actions that match joint transitions in simulation with recorded hardware
data [8], but was previously demonstrated only on a manipulator arm mounted on a quadruped.
Closely related humanoid methods such as ASAP and MOSAIC also learn residual corrections,
but rely on motion capture or target interface-specific gaps rather than reusable actuator dynam-
ics [28, 52]. We extend UAN to a floating-base 29-DoF humanoid by using proprioceptive state esti-
mators for base velocity and contact pose, enabling contact-aligned residual model learning without
external sensing.
7
Conclusion
We present Contact-UAN, a residual world-modeling approach for humanoid control that preserves
the structure of an analytical simulator while learning actuator-level corrections from offline proprio-
ceptive data. To enable replay-based learning from humanoid motion with contacts, we estimate base
velocity, contact status, and simulator-side contact pose from onboard observations, allowing hard-
ware trajectories to be initialized in simulation with consistent contact forces. Our UAN-augmented
simulator improves transfer of whole-body policies across diverse tasks using only 6.5 minutes of
real-world walking data: 20% lower running torque usage, 43% lower force-tracking error, compli-
ant interaction, accurate impedance control, and higher jumps with stable landings after task-reward
optimization. Reset ablations support contact-consistent initialization, and the residual’s benefit
persists under ground-truth initialization. Sim-to-sim experiments further show that Contact-UAN
remains effective under larger controlled actuator gaps. These results suggest that residual world
models provide a data-efficient alternative to fully learned dynamics models, retaining the gener-
alization benefits of physics simulation while improving fidelity where the sim-to-real gap is most
consequential.

episodes last 20 s. Actions are joint-position offsets around the default standing configuration, ap-
plied to per-joint PD controllers. During training, the frozen UAN deterministically adds its residual
correction to the commanded target before the simulator applies its actuator and contact models; at
deployment, only the downstream task policy is exported. The observation-noise model is identical
to that used during state-estimation data collection (Table 2), except that the jumping task uses a
smaller joint-velocity noise of ±0.5 rad/s (Table 9).
All tasks randomize foot–ground friction (uniform in [0.3, 1.2], shared across all foot geoms) and
push the robot by perturbing its root velocity at random intervals of 1–3 s, with task-specific push
ranges. For running and force control, policies trained without the UAN additionally randomize
per-joint encoder bias (uniform in ±0.015 rad, applied to joint position observations) and the torso
center of mass (additive offset, uniform in ±2.5 cm in x and y, ±3 cm in z). Jumping uses the
ranges in Appendix D.3. In the UAN-augmented simulator, all such robot-specific randomization is
removed (Sec. 2.3). PPO hyperparameters for all tasks are listed in Table 5.
The running and jumping policies are trained with a torque-speed actuator envelope [54]. For joint
j, denote stall torque by ¯τj and no-load speed by ¯˙qj, and write rj = ˙qj/¯˙qj. The nominal PD torque

D.1
Running Policy
To train a running policy, we adapt the default mjlab G1 velocity tracking task [29] with several
modifications. We remove the base linear velocity observation to encourage the policy to rely on
proprioception rather than privileged state, and extend the observation history to 5 steps. We add
rough-terrain randomization and the torque-speed clipping of Eq. (7) to improve robustness. To
improve motion quality, we lower the standing-command threshold for slow walking, add a low-
height termination at 0.5 m to prevent kneeling local optima, and increase the penalty weight on
joint position limits to −10. Table 7 gives the reward terms.
On the command side, we remove heading-tracking environments and instead dedicate 20% of
environments to pure turning. Commands follow a curriculum over training iterations: vcmd

D.2
Force Control Policy
The commanded force is realized by a virtual force field: an impedance spring–damper attached to
the right wrist and anchored at the end-effector position of the default standing pose. Every 10 s, a
target force is sampled per axis from ±30 N (zero force with probability 0.2) together with a band
stiffness k ∈(25, 400) N/m and damping 0.1 k; horizontal force commands are clipped so that the
implied per-axis displacement |F|/k stays within a 0.3 m reach. The virtual force is recomputed
from the current end-effector position and velocity and applied at every physics substep (200 Hz),
ramped up over 2–4 s, held for 2 s, and ramped back down, with 2 s of zero force at the start of
each episode; the applied force is clamped to 50 N. The policy observes the commanded force and
uses a 20-step observation history. The reward combines standing stabilization and force tracking
(Table 8), inspired by [30].

D.3
Jumping Policy
Overview. The jumping task requires the G1 to perform repeated standing vertical jumps: starting
from rest, the robot must leave the ground with both feet, gain height, and land softly back into a
stable stance. We first train a reference-conditioned policy to track a retargeted jumping motion. The
task is built on a BeyondMimic-style motion tracking formulation [2]. We then fine-tune the policy
with an additional reward for the predicted ballistic rise of the robot’s center of mass at takeoff.
The motion-tracking rewards retain their original weights during fine-tuning, while reference-error
termination conditions are replaced with general fall conditions, allowing the policy to increase jump
height while preserving the reference motion’s structure. The reference motion is the jump from
CMU MoCap clip 02 04 (“jump and balance”) [55], retargeted to the G1 and resampled at 50 Hz
(200 frames, 4 s). The clip is padded with 1 s of standing at the beginning and end (300 frames, 6 s
total), so the policy must initiate the jump from rest and hold a stable stance after landing.
Episode structure and reference-state initialization. The initial reference frame is sampled using
failure statistics accumulated over temporal bins, followed by uniform sampling within the selected
bin. The corresponding reference state is perturbed with uniform noise on the root pose (±5 cm
in xy, ±1 cm in z, ±0.1 rad roll/pitch, ±0.2 rad yaw), root velocity (±0.5 m/s linear xy, ±0.2 m/s
vertical, ±0.52 rad/s roll/pitch, ±0.78 rad/s yaw), and joint positions (±0.1 rad).
Observations and actions. The actor combines onboard proprioception with features of the current
reference frame; it does not observe the robot’s global position or measured root linear velocity.

World Model Details
Overview and scope. We compare Contact-UAN with our best-effort G1 adaptation of the fully
learned offline RWM-U [33] on a single hard target environment for omnidirectional base velocity
tracking on flat ground. This is a controlled data-efficiency reference, not a definitive benchmark of
the original method.
Data-efficiency results. A policy trained in the nominal physics simulator collects data in the
hard target environment for model learning. In Fig. 11, UAN trained with 5 minutes of target
data reaches the expert policy’s normalized episode length (100%). Our fully learned world model
uses approximately 33 hours of target data (about 400× as much): a policy initialized from scratch
inside it reaches 6.8% normalized episode length on the target, and a policy fine-tuned from the
data-collection policy reaches 38.2%, compared with 32.6% for the nominal-simulator baseline. All
policies reach 100% in their training environments. These results support the value of the simulator’s
physics priors in this controlled setting.

Robotic world model. The world model is a GRU ensemble (5 members, hidden size 256, 2 stacked

λ = 1.0 [33]. Imagined rollouts terminate when the learned termination head exceeds 0.9; lower
thresholds collapsed training because the policy learned to exploit short, high-reward fragments
inside the model.
Policy training. Given a trained world model, we run PPO on imagined rollouts using the same
rsl rl algorithm and hyperparameters as the baseline (Table 14). The outer loop runs for 5,000
iterations, following [33]; training longer overfits the policy to the inaccurate WM and degrades
target performance. UAN is trained using only 5 minutes of data.

G.1
Reset and Estimator Protocols
Training under different reset conditions. The reset-training ablation uses the hard target and
compares UANs and downstream policies trained with direct, estimated, component-oracle, or full-
oracle resets. The component oracles replace estimated base velocity (Base), contact state (Cnt.), or
stance-foot pose (Pose) with simulator ground truth; the full oracle (All) replaces all three. The pose
being initialized is the simulator-side contact pose, including the stance penetration and orientation
needed for contact-consistent replay. The reported 20-s survival rises from 49.8% with direct reset
to 96.7% with estimated reset; component/full-oracle variants yield 96.6–98.4%. Linear and yaw
tracking errors differ by less than approximately 10% between estimated and oracle variants. The
separately trained direct, estimated, and full-oracle conditions have 24-step joint-position replay
RMSEs of 0.0318, 0.0239, and 0.0120 rad, respectively.
