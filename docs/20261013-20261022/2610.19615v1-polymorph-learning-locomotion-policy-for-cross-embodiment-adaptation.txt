PolyMorph: Learning Locomotion Policy for
Cross-Embodiment Adaptation
Hyunsik Oh, Seunghun Jeon, Donghoon Youm, Jungwoo Hur, Hyeongjun Kim,
Jeong Hyun Lee, Moonkyu Jung, and Jemin Hwangbo∗
Abstract—Reinforcement learning has enabled robust loco-
motion for legged robots, but learned policies are typically
tied to a specific robot morphology and require additional
engineering when the number of legs, actuators, or mechani-
cal properties change. We present PolyMorph, a morphology-
conditioned framework that learns a single locomotion policy for
heterogeneous legged robots. PolyMorph converts each robot’s
URDF into joint-link modules and combines a recurrent encoder,
graph-based Transformer, and per-joint decoder to generate
actions across variable leg counts and joint configurations.
To improve training stability, PolyMorph uses morphology-
normalized rewards, adaptive robot sampling, and URDF-derived
PD-gain initialization. We evaluate PolyMorph in simulation
across bipedal, tripedal, and quadrupedal robots, including unseen
morphology variants and platforms, and deploy the policy on real
quadruped platforms operated in multiple configurations. The
results show that a single PolyMorph policy generalizes across
diverse robot morphologies without per-robot policy retraining.
Index Terms—Reinforcement Learning, Multi-Embodiment
Learning, Legged Robots
I. INTRODUCTION
I
N the field of robotics, significant progress has been made
through various control methodologies, including Model
Predictive Control (MPC) [1] and learning-based approaches
such as imitation learning (IL) [2], [3] and reinforcement
learning (RL) [4], [5]. Among these, model-free RL has
become a powerful tool for legged robot control because
it directly optimizes policies from environment interactions
without requiring explicit dynamics models. This makes RL
particularly effective for complex and underactuated systems,
enabling robust locomotion over challenging terrains such as
rough slopes, stairs, and deformable terrain [6], [7].
Most existing studies on legged locomotion focus on enabling
a single platform to perform a specific task [8], [9]. However,
policies trained for one robot often fail to transfer to another
due to differences in morphology, including degrees of freedom,
link masses, inertias, and kinematic configurations. Adapting
a policy to a new robot typically requires redesigning the
environment, observations, action space, and reward functions,
which demands substantial engineering effort. This motivates
the development of general controllers that can operate across
diverse legged robots.
In this work, we address the following question: Can we
train a single locomotion policy that operates across legged
robots with diverse morphologies, including different numbers
of legs and actuators, and deploy it on real hardware without
per-robot retraining? We argue that such a controller must not
only observe the robot’s current state but also reason about
its morphology, and that achieving this in practice requires
addressing training-level imbalances that arise when robots
with very different reward magnitudes and learning difficulties
are trained together.
To this end, we propose PolyMorph, a cross-embodiment
locomotion framework with a URDF-conditioned policy inter-
face. As shown in Fig. 1, the same policy is deployed across
diverse legged morphologies on various rough terrains in both
simulation and the real world. Each robot is represented as
a sequence of joint-link modules parsed from its URDF, and
padding with valid-action masking allows the same policy
to handle variable leg counts, actuator numbers, and joint
configurations without redesigning the action interface. The
policy combines a recurrent state encoder that summarizes
proprioceptive history, a Transformer that conditions on URDF-
derived graph structure, and per-joint decoder heads that
generate actions only for actuated joints.
A unified locomotion policy across heterogeneous morpholo-
gies can shorten the robot design-test cycle by allowing new
designs or modified configurations to be evaluated without
retraining a separate controller for each platform, which is
particularly useful for rapid robot design workflows [10],
[11]. It also enables policy reuse across heterogeneous robot
platforms and exposes the policy to broader morphology and
dynamics variations.
Beyond designing a policy that can process robots with
different kinematic structures, we observe that jointly optimiz-
ing across morphologies introduces training-level obstacles:
reward magnitudes can scale with the number of joints and
legs, biasing optimization toward certain morphologies; easy-
to-learn robots can dominate uniform sampling and reduce the
learning signal for harder morphologies such as bipeds; and
manually tuned PD gains reintroduce per-platform engineering
effort. Complementary to prior multi-embodiment methods
that emphasize morphology-conditioned policy architectures,
PolyMorph addresses these training-level obstacles through
morphology-normalized rewards, adaptive robot sampling,
URDF-derived PD-gain initialization, together with curriculum
learning and domain randomization for robust training. The
same policy parameters are shared across all robots, and only
morphology-dependent inputs are derived from each robot’s
URDF.

follows:
• We identify and address training-level obstacles in cross-
embodiment optimization, including reward-scale mis-

II. RELATED WORK
A. Transformer architectures for robot control
In legged robot control, Transformer-based policies have
been used to process observation histories or sensory inputs
for locomotion [12]. Beyond such temporal use, Transformers
also model relationships among input tokens through self-
attention [13], making them suitable for control problems where
multiple body parts must interact. This spatial formulation
is particularly attractive for multi-embodiment locomotion,
since variable-length token sequences naturally correspond
to robots with different numbers of joints. However, standard
Transformers do not explicitly encode the physical structure of
the robot and must infer morphology-dependent relationships
only from data, which can be sample-inefficient when training
across heterogeneous robots. Body Transformer [14] uses the
robot’s adjacency graph as an attention mask to inject structural
bias for a fixed morphology. PolyMorph adopts this structural
prior in a policy shared across heterogeneous morphologies,
where URDF-derived morphology inputs and attention masks
vary across embodiments and are combined with recurrent state
encoding.
B. Cross-embodied policy learning
Cross-embodied policy learning aims to train a single
policy that can operate across different robot embodiments.
In legged robotics, Metamorph [15] uses a Transformer to
control modular robots but is evaluated only in simulation.
GenLoco [16] achieves real-world transfer through morphology
randomization but focuses on quadrupeds. URMA [17] learns
multi-embodiment locomotion across quadrupedal and bipedal
robots with an attention-based encoder–decoder. Graph neural
networks [18] encode robot morphology as graphs. GCNT [19]
further combines graph-based morphology encoding with
Transformer communication to learn controllers across different
robot structures. LocoFormer [20] takes a different approach,
combining large-scale procedural training with long-context
adaptation to generalize locomotion across unseen embodi-
ments. While these works have advanced policy architectures,
the training-level aspects of jointly optimizing heterogeneous
legged robots have received less attention. PolyMorph also
targets them directly. Beyond locomotion, Chen et al. [21] and
Gupta et al. [22] study cross-robot transfer from demonstrations
through intention-aligned representations and kinematics-aware
policy synthesis, respectively.

transitions, and recovery from disturbances. We therefore
process these observations with a recurrent encoder and provide
the resulting latent vector to the action decoder together with
the morphology-conditioned Transformer features.

D. Training Techniques for Generalization
When a single policy is trained to control robots with
heterogeneous morphologies, three practical bottlenecks arise
simultaneously: unequal learning difficulty across robots,
morphology-dependent reward scales, and per-platform PD-
gain tuning. PolyMorph integrates adaptive robot sampling,
morphology-normalized rewards, and URDF-derived gain
initialization to address these bottlenecks, respectively.
Adaptive robot sampling. Uniformly sampling robots
during training can under-train difficult morphologies such
as bipeds. To balance learning, we increase the sampling
probability of robots with lower rewards. The probability of
sampling robot i is computed as
Pi =

where ri is the reward of robot i and ¯r is the average reward
over all robots, and the denominator ensures the probabilities
sum to 1. To avoid overfitting to difficult robots, half of the
environments are still sampled uniformly.
Reward normalization. When learning a robot-agnostic
policy, the reward should encourage motion generation and
command tracking without favoring a specific robot morphol-
ogy. However, if reward magnitudes depend strongly on the
number of joints or legs, the policy can converge to a local
optimum where only certain robot configurations achieve high
rewards. Large reward-scale differences across robots can also
destabilize PPO training and may even cause learning failure.
To avoid this, we normalize morphology-dependent reward
terms by the number of physical components that generate them.
Joint-related penalties, such as joint torque, position, velocity,
acceleration, and action smoothness, are divided by the number
of actuated joints. Similarly, leg- or foot-related terms, such as
airtime and foot clearance, are divided by the number of legs.
This keeps reward scales consistent across morphologies and
helps the policy learn robust behaviors without bias toward a
particular robot configuration.

positions, which are converted to torques through a standard
PD controller. To avoid robot-specific gain tuning in multi-
embodiment training, we use a simple morphology-aware gain
rule based on the URDF-derived mass matrix evaluated at a
nominal posture.

IV. RESULTS
Our experiments evaluate whether the overall PolyMorph
framework enables stable training across heterogeneous robots,
whether a single policy achieves locomotion across seen
and unseen morphologies and transfers to real hardware,
and whether PolyMorph improves performance relative to
alternative architectures and multi-embodiment baselines.
A. Implementation Details
Training was conducted in RaiSim [23] using 350 parallel
environments for 20,000 iterations on an AMD EPYC 9654
CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, requiring approximately
2.7 days. We trained three random seeds and evaluated each
method on the same 100 episodes per robot. The deployed actor
contains approximately 0.33 million parameters. For real-world
deployment, the 100 Hz policy was exported to ONNX format
and executed on the onboard computers of Raibo1 and Raibo2,
with an average latency of 0.366 ms, as shown in Fig. 3.

B. Framework Evaluation
We trained a single locomotion policy across diverse legged
robots, including Raibo1, Raibo2, Hound, Aliengo, Anymal-
C, Atlas, Cassie, Go1, Laikago, Mini-Cheetah, and multiple
morphology variants. The training set covers bipedal, tripedal,
and quadrupedal configurations with different kinematic and
dynamic properties, with masses ranging from 11kg to 135kg.

the corresponding leg is kept fixed instead of being removed.

C. Framework Analysis
We analyze PolyMorph from two perspectives: cross-
morphology control performance and morphology-aware repre-
sentation learning. Using training curves and command-tracking
results, we first evaluate whether a single policy maintains
stable performance across robots with different leg counts, body
configurations, and mechanical properties. We then use t-SNE
embeddings to examine whether the graph-based Transformer
captures meaningful morphology-dependent structure.
As shown in Fig. 5, PolyMorph converges stably across
diverse robot morphologies. Quadrupeds generally achieve
higher rewards due to their greater static and dynamic stability,
whereas bipeds obtain lower rewards because balancing with
fewer contacts is more difficult on rough terrain. Nevertheless,
the terminal rates decrease and remain low after training, indi-
cating stable locomotion even for challenging morphologies.
For command-tracking evaluation, each robot was tested
over multiple rough-terrain episodes with randomized velocity
commands. The commands vx

a transient, and the RMSE was computed over the remaining
8 s and averaged across evaluation episodes. Table IV sum-
marizes the command-tracking performance on rough terrain.
PolyMorph achieves low RMSE for both seen and unseen
robots, showing that the learned controller generalizes beyond
the training morphologies. This generalization is enabled
by representing each joint-link pair as an individual token
and using graph-based attention to condition actions on the
robot structure. Real-world experiments further confirm that
the same policy can be deployed on different configurations
while maintaining stable locomotion using only proprioceptive
observations.
Among the unseen entries in Table IV, B2 and Solo12
evaluate quadrupedal extrapolation beyond the training set,
which spans 11kg (Mini-Cheetah) to 52kg (Anymal-C). B2
(70kg) and Solo12 (2.5kg) represent heavy- and light-side ex-
trapolation cases, respectively. PolyMorph achieves command-
tracking accuracy on B2 comparable to seen platforms, while
on Solo12, whose mass is less than one quarter of the
lightest training quadruped and whose body scale and actuator
characteristics differ substantially, it maintains command-
following behavior with reduced tracking precision. Overall,
PolyMorph transfers reliably across the quadrupedal training
distribution and generalizes to morphologically similar out-of-
distribution platforms, while extreme scale differences remain
a direction for broader training coverage.
To examine whether the graph-based Transformer captures
morphology information, we visualize the learned token
embeddings using t-SNE. As shown in Fig. 5, the embeddings
form clusters according to the number of legs and robot body
structure. Robots with similar size and kinematic properties,
such as Raibo2 and Hound, appear close to each other, while
different configurations of the same robot form nearby but dis-
tinct clusters. Unseen robots also cluster near morphologically
similar robots, suggesting that PolyMorph encodes structural
features rather than simply memorizing robot identities, and
learns morphology-aware representations that support robust
policy learning across diverse legged robots.

D. Ablation and Baseline Comparison
We evaluate PolyMorph from two perspectives. First, we
examine structured token-wise processing by comparing Poly-
Morph with an MLP variant that flattens the joint-link features
into a single vector instead of processing them as separate
Transformer tokens, while keeping the same training pipeline.
Second, we compare PolyMorph with representative multi-
embodiment baselines, Metamorph [15] and URMA [17],
under comparable training budgets. For controlled comparison,
we preserve the method-specific observations, morphology
representations, and network architectures of Metamorph
and URMA following their original publications and official
implementations, while training and evaluating all methods
under identical training conditions, budget, and evaluation
protocol. Each method is trained with 3 random seeds and
evaluated under the protocol described in Sec. IV-A. In Table V,
”Fail” indicates termination during evaluation (e.g., falling).
As shown in Fig. 6, PolyMorph achieves consistently higher
total reward than the baselines and maintains balanced per-
robot performance across diverse morphologies. The modular
joint-link representation and graph-based Transformer attention
allow the policy to decompose robot morphology into structured
components and model their interactions, leading to more stable
policy learning across heterogeneous robots.
The PolyMorph-MLP variant degrades in performance as
the number and diversity of robots increase. This indicates
that the graph-based Transformer is important for interpreting
morphology and generalizing to new structures. Metamorph
and URMA both rely on attention mechanisms but lack explicit
morphology-aware structural bias and recurrent temporal pro-
cessing of proprioceptive observations. As a result, Metamorph
struggles to track commands and fails on some seen and unseen
robots, while URMA remains competitive on some training
robots but becomes unstable during later curriculum stages and
fails on some unseen robots. These results suggest that attention-

V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this work, we presented PolyMorph, a morphology-
conditioned locomotion framework for controlling legged
robots with diverse morphologies using a single policy. Poly-
Morph represents each robot as a URDF-derived sequence
of joint-link modules and combines a recurrent state encoder,
a graph-based Transformer, and per-joint decoder heads to
generate actions across heterogeneous morphologies. Combined
with morphology-normalized rewards, adaptive robot sampling,
URDF-derived PD-gain initialization, curriculum learning, and
domain randomization, the framework improves training stabil-
ity across robots with different leg counts, configurations, and
mechanical properties. Simulation and real-world experiments
demonstrate robust command tracking on rough terrain across
bipedal, tripedal, and quadrupedal robots, including unseen
configurations of training platforms and unseen quadrupedal
platforms.
The main limitation of the current framework is that
generalization is reliable only within the morphology range
covered by training and degrades under strong extrapolation in
topology. Future work will focus on overcoming this limitation
through robot data augmentation, such as procedurally perturb-
ing URDFs and synthesizing morphology variants along the
topology axis, so that the network is exposed to a broader range
of robots during training and can extrapolate more reliably to
platforms substantially different from the training set.
