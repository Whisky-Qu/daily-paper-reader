Abstract 

Assist-as-needed (AAN) control must distinguish between changes in in-tended motion and changes in effective voluntary torque capability, even though both can produce similar kinematic and interaction responses. Adual-measurand multimodal MHE–MPC architecture is developed as the base framework, in which the intended trajectory and the task-dependent effective voluntary torque capability are estimated online as distinct quanti-ties, and both estimates are used to adapt robotic assistance. Building on this architecture, the ambiguity between the two update channels is formulated as a continuous credit-assignment problem and resolved by an attribution gate. A trajectory-change indicator is obtained from persistent corrective in-teraction, whereas a capability-change indicator is derived from the residual between normalized muscle activation and dynamics-reconstructed voluntary torque. Nine healthy participants completed factorial target-height–load tri-als and a pre–post fatigue cup-transport task. Without target-height input, the gated method generated correctly ordered Low–Middle–High references under every load: target changes were preferentially assigned to trajectory adaptation, whereas added load shifted adaptation toward the capability channel. 1. Introduction 

Upper-limb motor impairment creates demand for intensive and task-specific rehabilitation training [1, 2]. Repeatable movement practice can be delivered and human–robot interaction can be quantified by rehabilitation robots during training [2]. Robot-mediated rehabilitation can be further extended by wearable platforms toward functional tasks and less structured environments [3–6]. Task completion can be facilitated by assist-as-needed (AAN) control while the user’s voluntary contribution is preserved. This balance is par-ticularly difficult in wearable systems, which operate in continuous physical interaction with users whose movement goals, effort, fatigue, and physical limitations may vary across repetitions [7, 8]. A reference trajectory that is inconsistent with the user’s intended movement can produce tracking er-ror and interaction torque, but similar responses can also arise when volun-tary capability is insufficient for the required motion. Active participation may be suppressed by excessive assistance, whereas task completion may be compromised or interaction load may be increased by insufficient assistance. Effective AAN control must therefore address both trajectory compatibility and assistance demand. Trajectory compatibility has been addressed by one line of research through human-intention estimation and reference adaptation. sEMG, kine-matics, and interaction torque are combined with model-based or data-driven inference by multimodal methods [9–14]; the reference is further adapted by iterative learning and optimization [11, 12, 15]. Motion intention and arm stiffness have also been estimated simultaneously for variable-impedance interaction [10]. However, stiffness is an interaction property rather than the voluntary torque effectively available for the current task. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, intended trajectory and task-dependent effective voluntary torque capability have not been jointly estimated as distinct online measurands for AAN. Moreover, trajectory-adaptation signals depend on 2whether the user can execute the intended movement. Assistance is modulated by error-based AAN schemes from kinematic de-viation [16–19], whereas voluntary torque, stiffness, or strength are estimated by capability-centered methods to compensate for an inferred deficit [20– 24]. Physiological effort information is provided by direct sEMG-based as-sistance, although signal variability and session-specific calibration remain practical considerations [23, 24]. How much assistance should be provided is determined by these approaches, but tracking and effort deviations may also reflect a purposeful change in the desired trajectory. Trajectory adaptation and capability-based assistance consequently can-not be treated as unrelated estimation problems. The task demand is modi-fied by a change in intention, while the effective voluntary contribution avail-able in the current task context is modified by fatigue, activation–torque in-consistency, and unmodeled external demand. These effects can occur during the same movement and appear in the same motion, interaction, and effort observations. If the two states are updated from overlapping residuals with-out explicit credit assignment, capability- or demand-related signals can be absorbed into trajectory adaptation, while intention-related signals can be absorbed into assistance. To address this problem, a dual-estimation architecture is established and then extended by automatic attribution. The contributions are as follows: (1) An ungated dual-measurand MHE–MPC architecture is developed to esti-mate intended-trajectory deformation and task-dependent effective voluntary torque capability online, and both estimates are used for AAN. (2) An attribution gate is introduced to compensate cross-sensitivity by al-locating shared observations through a gating variable α ∈ [0 , 1] and its complement 1 − α, thereby limiting leakage between intention and capability estimates. (3) The two layers are evaluated separately: dual estimation is tested by comparison with error-based AAN, whereas credit assignment is tested by gated–ungated comparison. 

2. Measurement System and Estimated Quantities 

2.1. Robot and Sensor Platform 

A lightweight, portable upper-limb wearable robot was developed as the experimental platform for elbow and wrist assistance, as depicted in Fig. 1. The device employs a cable-driven mechanism to deliver assistive torque 3to the elbow and wrist. A Unitree motor (GO-M8010-6, YuShu Technol-ogy Co., Hangzhou, China) actuates the elbow module, while two Damiao motors (DM-J4310-2EC, Damiao Technology Co., Shenzhen, China) actu-ate wrist flexion/extension and pronation/supination. Inertial measurement units (IMUs) (JY-901B, WitMotion, Shenzhen, China) mounted on the robot provide estimates of joint angles. An Arduino Mega 2560 microcontroller (Ar-duino, Ivrea, Italy) interfaces with the motor drivers and IMUs, executing the embedded torque control loop at 500 Hz. A laptop runs the upper-level dual-estimation MPC on ROS 2 at 50 Hz, communicating with the Arduino over a local Wi-Fi link. In this setup, the effective one-way communication latency was approximately 20 ms (about one upper-level sampling interval). Because the high-bandwidth torque loop is closed on the Arduino and the upper-level assistance/trajectory-deformation updates are rate-limited and dominated by slow task dynamics (typically 1–10 Hz), this latency mainly acts as a one-step hold on slowly varying commands. A lithium-ion battery powers the system, enabling untethered operation for extended periods. To improve wearability, the actuators are placed away from the dis-tal joints and the assistive torques are transmitted to the elbow and wrist through cables. The robot has a total mass below 5 kg and a wearable mass of approximately 0.7 kg. Adjustable straps support rapid fitting across participants. IMU

2.3. Motion Intention and Capability Variables 

The user’s intended motion is represented as a smooth deformation of a nominal joint-space task trajectory: 

θint (s) = θnom (s) + Φ(s)w, (4) where s ∈ [0 , 1] is the task phase, θnom (s) is the nominal joint trajectory, 

Φ(s) is a fixed smooth basis, and w is the trajectory-deformation parameter vector estimated online. The vector w encodes the amplitude and direction of spatial path deformation, such as raising or lowering the endpoint relative to the nominal reach. 5The effective voluntary capability is represented by a vector-valued, control-oriented torque envelope τ max . Each component corresponds to one controlled joint direction. This state represents the voluntary torque effectively available under the current task and interaction conditions. It may therefore reflect fatigue, activation–torque consistency, and external demand not represented separately in the prediction model. Given a la-tent task-demand torque τ desire , the available voluntary contribution is approximated by an element-wise saturation abstraction: 

τ vol = S(τ desire ; τ max ),

[S(x; τmax )] i = sign( xi) min {| xi|, τ max,i }. (5) The net voluntary contribution is decomposed as 

τ vol = τ task + τ corr , (6) where τ task supports task progression and τ corr represents corrective inter-action relative to the current reference. These representations provide the state coordinates used by the credit-assignment controller in Section III.

The method comprises three layers (Fig. 2). In the base architecture, ca-pability is estimated by MHE and intended-trajectory deformation by MPC, and both estimates determine assistance. The extension uses αk to gate their updates. Participant-specific quantities are obtained from the calibration in 7Section 4.1.2; synchronized kinematics, sEMG, and robot torque are then used to reconstruct the participant’s mechanical contribution. The central, joint-direction-specific attribution state αk compensates cross-sensitivity between the two measurement channels. Persistent cor-rective interaction supports a change in movement intention and therefore updates the reference trajectory, whereas disagreement between muscle acti-vation and model-implied voluntary torque supports a change in capability. Importantly, αk is a supervisory state, not an MPC decision variable. At each cycle it is computed before the two optimizations: 1 − αk gates the capability indicator in MHE, whereas αk gates the trajectory indicator in MPC. Moving-horizon estimation (MHE) uses a recent window of measurements to update the effective-capability estimate under reliability, physiological, and rate constraints [25]. The accepted capability is then supplied to model predictive control (MPC), which predicts future motion and jointly optimizes reference deformation and the required assistance subject to joint, torque, and slew-rate limits [26]. Thus, MHE estimates what the participant can effectively contribute under the current task context from the past obser-vation horizon, while the attribution-conditioned MPC estimates intended trajectory over the predictive horizon and jointly determines the remaining assistance demand.

4.1.2. Individual Calibration 

Before formal testing, each participant completed three short calibra-tion stages: resting sEMG recording, three EMG-only maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) attempts, and three comfortable unassisted reaches to the middle target. sEMG was recorded from the biceps brachii and triceps brachii using bipolar sensors (Sichiray, Wuxi, China; sampling rate: 1000 Hz) with Ag/AgCl electrodes. The calibration identified the participant-specific EMG baseline and activation scale, nominal middle-target trajectory, initial effective voluntary-torque capability, and associated uncertainty. Neither target-height information nor load data were used in calibration. Elbow dynamic parameters were estimated from participant anthropo-metrics using standard ratios [18]: 

mf ≈ 0.016 mH , lf ≈ 0.682 Lf a (30) where mH is body mass and Lf a is forearm length. These parameters and the calibration-derived quantities initialized the participant-specific model. The assistance-onset factor was fixed at κ = 0 .5 for all reported experiments. 

4.2. Experiment 1: Controlled Variation of Intention and Capability 

To evaluate the separate response to intention and capability changes, target height (Low, Middle, High) was varied to induce intended trajectory 16 deviations, while unmodeled wrist loads (Free, Light, Heavy) were applied to alter the effective voluntary capability–demand balance. All conditions shared a common nominal trajectory derived from the Middle target; the controller had to infer any intended deviation online. Target changes oc-curred only within a fixed load, and load changes occurred only at block boundaries with unchanged target, ensuring that the two perturbation types were temporally decoupled. Three controllers were compared within subjects: (1) error-based AAN (baseline without estimation), (2) ungated dual-estimation MPC (base archi-tecture without credit assignment), and (3) attribution-gated dual-estimation MPC (the proposed method). This progressive comparison isolated the con-tributions of dual estimation and of the attribution gate. Each controller– load block consisted of nine randomized trials (3 targets × 3 repetitions), and the capability estimate was reset to the calibrated prior at each block boundary. The same target sequence was reused across controllers, yielding 

3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81 trials per participant. 

4.3. Experiment 2: Functional Task Under Induced Fatigue 

The gated controller was further evaluated in a cup-transport task (Fig. 4) that combined elbow, wrist, and forearm motion. A functional reference was built from three phase-aligned demonstrations. Nine trials (3 heights × 3repetitions) were performed before and after a fatigue-inducing weight-lifting procedure, with the controller unchanged. This design tested whether the estimator could track a fatigue-induced decline in effective capability and increase assistance accordingly, without explicit fatigue modeling. In this experiment, elbow flexion/extension, wrist flexion/extension, and forearm pronation/supination were assisted, while only the elbow joint as-sistance was adjusted by the proposed method. The other two joints were assisted with a fixed-gain impedance controller to maintain the functional task. 

4.4. Statistical Analysis 

Results are reported as mean ± sample standard deviation across the nine participants after within-participant averaging. Experiment 1 was analyzed using a three-way repeated-measures ANOVA with controller, load, and tar-get height as within-participant factors. Experiment 2 was analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with fatigue state and target height as within-participant factors. Omnibus effects were evaluated first; significant 17 effects were followed by paired comparisons with Holm correction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 .

6. Discussion 

Recent studies have advanced individual components of this problem but do not resolve the ambiguity at the closed-loop control level. Multi-task networks recognize motion intention and fatigue simultaneously from sEMG [29], and separate work detects fatigue by fusing time- and frequency-domain features [30], but these outputs are discrete labels rather than contin-uous control-relevant measurands. Simultaneous intention–stiffness estima-tion has been demonstrated for physical human–robot interaction [10], yet stiffness characterizes mechanical impedance rather than voluntary torque 23 capability. Reference-adaptation methods [11, 12] and capability-centered AAN schemes [21, 22] therefore assign deviations to one prespecified chan-nel. The unresolved issue is structural: when intention and capability share kinematic, interaction, and sEMG observations, parallel estimators cannot determine which latent cause should receive credit for a new residual. Joint estimation is needed to expose both hypotheses to the same evidence, and at-tribution is needed to prevent the same evidence from driving both estimates simultaneously. 

6.1. Simultaneous Estimation of Intention and Capability 

A central finding is that intended trajectory deformation and task-dependent effective voluntary capability can be updated online as distinct, control-relevant quantities with condition-consistent responses. This goes beyond recent simultaneous recognition results [10, 29]: it produces two continuous measurands and explicitly models their competing explanations of the observations. In Experiment 1, the reference endpoints of the gated controller remained correctly ordered (Low, Middle, High) under all three load conditions, despite receiving no target-height information and using the same Middle-target nominal trajectory. The updated first row of Fig. 5 makes this distinction explicit: the dotted original trajectory repeats across the three targets, whereas the online reference deforms downward for Low and upward for High and remains close to the measured path. Thus, the target-specific reference changes cannot be attributed to different original trajectories. At the same time, ˆτmax progressively declined during the Heavy-load block, showing that the capability channel continued to respond to the added demand while the reference followed the cued target transitions. The capability estimator’s response to unmodeled load also explains why tracking RMSE remained low across load-averaged values for the gated controller. Because the MHE continuously updates ˆτmax from the EMG–dynamics residual, the MPC can anticipate the required assistance without relying on a growing tracking error. Recent work has shown that EMG-to-force mappings can be adapted across fatigue states [31], and that physiological and functional measurements capture complementary aspects of fatigue [30, 32]. However, estimating force or detecting fatigue alone cannot tell an AAN controller whether a task mismatch should increase assistance or deform the reference. The proposed paired measurands provide that missing decision context. This predictive compensation decouples assistance from raw kinematic deviation—a property that error-based AAN 24 fundamentally lacks. The significant controller–height interaction in assis-tance torque further confirms that the commanded torque was modulated by task context rather than by a uniform, load-driven offset. 

6.2. Dynamic Credit Assignment Through the Attribution State 

The attribution gate α prevents cross-contamination between the two es-timation channels. This role differs from feature sharing in multitask recog-nition [29] and from cost arbitration in shared-control adaptation [11, 12]: the gate acts on the causal destination of a residual before it changes the reference or assistance demand. Under the nominal Free–Middle condition, 

α remained near neutral (0.489). Adding load at the same target decreased 

α (0.377 Light, 0.402 Heavy), shifting weight toward the capability channel, because the load increased the EMG–dynamics residual without producing a directionally consistent corrective torque. Changing target under Free load shifted α upward (0.573 Low, 0.617 High), as persistent corrective interac-tion provided clear trajectory-change evidence. These shifts were continuous, allowing the gate to express varying confidence and combine evidence when both intention and capability changed simultaneously. The temporal dynamics in Fig. 5 illustrate this arbitration against an unchanged nominal-trajectory baseline. During the displayed Middle and Low Heavy-load trials, decreases in α accompanied downward revisions of 

ˆτmax , placing more weight on capability evidence. In the subsequent High trial, α rose strongly while the adapted reference moved upward away from the repeated nominal trace; capability and assistance continued to change as well. The response therefore reflects continuous assignment when tra-jectory and capability evidence coexist, rather than a sequence of isolated hard classifications. This temporal coordination is consistent with the gate operating as a supervisory scheduler that limits capability deficits from be-ing absorbed as intended deformations and deliberate target changes from being interpreted solely as capability loss. The RMSE improvement of the gated over the independent-estimation variant, although not significant after correction, aligns with this mechanistic interpretation and warrants further investigation with larger sample sizes. 

6.3. Controlled Evidence and Functional Feasibility 

The two experiments provided complementary validation. Experiment 1 employed a full factorial manipulation of target height and load, creating a 3 × 3 grid in which intention and capability perturbations were applied 25 independently and in combination. This allowed controller-state responses to be mapped directly onto known perturbation types, establishing strong internal validity. Experiment 2 extended this validation to an ecologically more relevant setting involving multi-joint coordination and a natural movement sequence under fatigue. This is important because recent high-performing sEMG fa-tigue models have predominantly been validated under structured contrac-tions [29, 30], and a recent review identifies context, sensing modality, and calibration as persistent obstacles to interpreting fatigue during functional performance [32]. Although the pre–post changes in ˆτmax and assistance did not reach significance, their consistent direction across all participants and target heights, together with the significant RMSE increase and sEMG spectral compression, strongly suggests that the controller detected and re-sponded to the fatigue-induced deficit. The continuous traces in Fig. 7 con-firm that the coordinated state adaptations observed in controlled reaching were preserved during functional movement. This transfer to an ADL-like task supports the practical feasibility of the proposed framework. 

6.4. Limitations and Future Work 

The study enrolled healthy participants and used external loads and acute fatigue to simulate capability changes. While this enabled controlled, re-peatable perturbations, it does not reproduce the complex neuromuscular impairments or long-term adaptation of clinical populations. Moreover, no single biosignal fully characterizes central and peripheral fatigue [32]. The results therefore validate algorithmic function rather than therapeutic effi-cacy. Subsequent studies should involve individuals with upper-limb motor deficits to evaluate patient-specific adaptation. Second, adaptive capability estimation and assistance were restricted to elbow flexion. The cup-transport task involved wrist and forearm motion, but capability changes in those degrees of freedom were not estimated or com-pensated. Extending the framework to multiple joints will require managing the increased dimensionality of the MHE–MPC problem and interactions be-tween joint-level attribution states, which is an important direction for future work. 26 7. Conclusion 

This work addresses a fundamental ambiguity in assist-as-needed control—that similar kinematic and interaction responses can arise from changes in movement intention or from reduced voluntary capability—by simultaneously estimating both quantities online and dynamically routing shared sensory evidence through an attribution gate. The key contribu-tion lies in the credit-assignment mechanism that prevents intention- and capability-related signals from contaminating each other, enabling the con-troller to adapt the reference trajectory and adjust assistance appropriately without requiring hard event classification. The attribution-gated controller achieved a condition-level mean tracking RMSE of 0.017 rad across nine load–height conditions, approximately 60% lower than the independent-estimation variant and 87% lower than error-based AAN. The attribution state α varied systematically with load and target height, while the continuous responses showed target-specific refer-ence deformation away from a repeated unupdated nominal trajectory to-gether with coordinated capability and assistance changes. A functional cup-transport task under induced fatigue provided further proof-of-concept evidence in a multi-joint ADL-like context: post-fatigue capability estimates decreased and assistance increased at all target heights, although these two changes were not statistically significant. Together, these findings support the proposed credit-assignment framework while motivating larger studies in clinical populations.
