Abstract — Humanoid behavior foundation models (BFMs) enable diverse whole-body control, but adapting a pretrained controller to a new embodiment remains challenging because of kinematic and dynamic mismatch. We present TRACE, a framework for efficient cross-embodiment adaptation that preserves pretrained motion representations while specializing feedback control to the target robot. TRACE first injects kinematic priors into input and output projection layers and aligns nominal joint dynamics to reduce embodiment mismatch and support stable fine-tuning. It then combines encoder distillation with low-rank reinforcement learning on a compact, quality-screened motion set, using a two-phase curriculum to establish stable tracking before refining difficult motions. We apply TRACE to transfer SONIC from Unitree G1 to Unitree H2 using 10 million motion frames and approxi-mately eight hours of training on a single RTX PRO 6000 GPU, at less than 0.1% of the GPU-hour cost of training SONIC from scratch. * The adapted controller achieves 96.08% success on 67,953 evaluation motions under a height-scaled tracking criterion. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid behavior foundation models (BFMs) aim to learn a single whole-body controller for diverse behaviors rather than behavior-specific policies [1]. Training on large motion datasets with a shared tracking objective makes this approach scalable. Reference motions also provide a com-pact representation and command interface for higher-level models to specify whole-body behavior. SONIC exemplifies this paradigm: trained on over 100 million motion frames, a single Unitree G1 policy supports tracking unseen motions, VR teleoperation, and vision–language–action (VLA) con-trol [2]. Although a BFM trained for a single embodiment can generalize across behaviors, cross-embodiment transfer re-quires addressing two principal mismatches. First, kinematic arXiv:2610.17999v1 [cs.RO] 13 Oct 2026 mismatch stems from differences in the number and arrange-ment of actuators and sensors, as well as in link geometry, altering the dimensionality and physical semantics of the BFM’s observation and action spaces. Second, dynamics mismatch may persist even after kinematic correspondence is established. Differences in link masses and other inertial properties, actuator dynamics, and contact dynamics may induce different state transitions under identical commands and feedback, leading to different closed-loop responses that can affect stability. Neither mismatch is readily resolved by modifying only the BFM’s inputs and outputs. Yet training a dedicated BFM for each embodiment is prohibitively expensive, particularly as robotic platforms evolve rapidly. Recent multi-embodiment approaches jointly train shared controllers across multiple humanoid embodiments or randomized morphologies, often using unified observa-tion–action interfaces and embodiment-aware architec-tures [3]–[5]. Their goal is zero-shot generalization or few-shot adaptation to new embodiments [3], [4]. However, broader embodiment coverage increases training cost, while control performance on out-of-distribution embodiments re-mains uncertain [3], [4]. Shared policies may also fall short of embodiment-specific policies in control accuracy and stability [4], [5]. Recent work also enables efficient trans-fer of pretrained BFMs by combining joint-level kinematic alignment with low-rank adaptation of dynamics-sensitive modules [26]. However, this formulation treats the post-alignment gap as dynamical, without an explicit objective for aligning motion latents across embodiments. It also retains the source motion-sampling and optimization settings, leaving transfer-specific data selection and staged control refinement outside its scope. To this end, we introduce TRACE, an efficient TR ansfer learning framework AC ross humanoid Embodiments for BFMs. TRACE reuses pretrained motion representations while specializing feedback control for the target embodi-ment. We first inject kinematic priors into the BFM’s input and output projection layers for kinematic pre-alignment, and further reduce the initial embodiment mismatch through joint-command normalization and dynamic-response align-ment. We also introduce a targeted dataset construction strategy that selects a compact transfer set from quality-screened motions, balancing motion categories and promot-ing dispersion in the command feature space. Using this set, we separate representation alignment from control opti-mization during transfer training: encoder distillation anchors target motion encodings to the pretrained latent space, while reinforcement learning adapts the control decoder to target dynamics, both through low-rank updates. To accelerate convergence and improve control quality, we organize this training into two phases: first establishing stable tracking across the selected motions, then emphasizing difficult mo-tions to improve tracking and contact accuracy. We use TRACE to transfer the pretrained SONIC con-troller from Unitree G1 to Unitree H2. The two embodiments differ in body scale, mass distribution, joint geometry, and actuation. The transfer training completes in approximately eight hours on a single RTX PRO 6000 GPU, at a fraction of SONIC’s 21k GPU-hour pretraining cost [2] and with improved transfer efficiency over prior work [26]. Quanti-tative evaluation on a common motion-tracking benchmark validates the control quality achieved after transfer, while ab-lation studies quantify the contributions of key components. On the physical Unitree H2, we further demonstrate demand-ing motion tracking and collect whole-body demonstrations through VR teleoperation. These demonstrations are then used to post-train a VLA model for autonomous task execu-tion. These results show that TRACE enables efficient cross-embodiment BFM transfer and provides a reusable controller for real-world tasks and downstream policy learning. The main contributions of this work are threefold:  

> •

We present TRACE, an efficient cross-embodiment transfer learning framework for BFMs that reuses pretrained motion representations while specializing feedback control for a new humanoid. Its core de-sign combines kinematic and dynamic-response pre-alignment with two-phase low-rank training that de-couples reference-encoder distillation from control op-timization.  

> •

We introduce a targeted motion-selection strategy that balances motion categories and promotes dispersion in the command feature space after quality screening. The resulting compact transfer set improves motion-tracking success under a fixed data budget.  

> •

We achieve low-cost transfer of SONIC from Uni-tree G1 to the substantially different Unitree H2, with 96.08% tracking success on the PHUMA benchmark. On the physical H2, the transferred controller supports demanding motion tracking and VR-based whole-body demonstration collection, with the collected data en-abling VLA post-training for autonomous tasks. II. R ELATED WORK 

a) Humanoid Behavior Foundation Model: Humanoid BFMs build on reference-motion tracking in physics-based character control. DeepMimic learned robust, physically plausible policies with dense imitation rewards over pre-scribed clips; later work extended this to diverse motions, dynamic and balance-intensive skills, and adaptation to real humanoids [6]–[9]. Early generalists used broader motion libraries or unified command interfaces within a single policy for universal tracking, teleoperation, and multimode whole-body control [10]–[12], extending beyond individual references or small skill sets without jointly scaling mo-tion data, policy capacity, and distributed compute. Com-plementary unsupervised reinforcement learning prioritizes stability and safety over fine-grained tracking [13]. SONIC established foundation-scale reference-conditioned control by jointly scaling data, policy capacity, and compute; trained on over 100 million motion frames, it generalizes to unseen motions [2]. b) Multi-Embodiment Generalist Control: Multi-embodiment control assumes shared control-relevant physical regularities across robots with different kinematics and dynamics. Early shared policies encoded embodiment and pooled cross-design experience, enabling transfer to unseen structures within a morphology space [16]. Legged-robot methods combine morphology-agnostic diffusion with shared residual adaptation [17], or large-scale training on procedurally generated bodies with long-context test-time adaptation [18]. Recent humanoid systems combine unified state–action semantics, physically consistent morphology variation, embodiment- and topology-aware policies, and generalist–specialist refinement for zero- or few-shot high-dimensional whole-body control and efficient target specialization [3]–[5]. These methods amortize learning across embodiments, but training cost and coverage depend on the training distribution’s breadth and physical validity. Joint tokenization of embodiment, state, and action also supports robot co-design and cross-embodiment control [19], but scalability to humanoid BFM pretraining remains unverified. 

c) Transfer Learning for Humanoid BFMs: BFM trans-fer reuses pretrained motion priors rather than relearning control. Same-embodiment approaches typically add residual or delta policies to pretrained trackers to adapt to new motion and task distributions or improve execution accuracy [20]– [23]. With fixed kinematics and observation–action inter-faces, they do not directly address embodiment transfer. Earlier cross-embodiment studies progressively optimized policies along continuous morphology paths to narrow em-bodiment gaps, but required repeated training and did not address parameter-efficient transfer of large-scale humanoid BFMs [24], [25]. Recent work first validated parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) for cross-embodiment humanoid BFM transfer [26]: kinematic input/output alignment and LoRA [27] fine-tuning of dynamics-related modules enable low-cost transfer to multiple embodiments, indicating that low-rank updates effectively capture this adaptation. How-ever, residual kinematic errors after joint-level alignment shift the command distribution, while overemphasizing dif-ficult motions early in fine-tuning erodes pretrained priors; both slow convergence and reduce final tracking accuracy. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

Figure 2 presents the four-stage TRACE framework. Cross-embodiment pre-adaptation (Stage 1, Sec. III-B) in-jects kinematic priors into reference, observation, and ac-tion projections and aligns action scales and nominal joint responses. Motion dataset curation (Stage 2, Sec. III-C) combines retargeting and quality screening with category balancing and command-space selection. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Stage 3, Sec. III-D) distills command latents and tokens from the frozen source Robot reference encoder into its target counterpart, while PPO adapts the control decoder to target dynamics. The transferred controller is then deployed on the target robot (Stage 4), supporting reference-motion tracking, VR teleoperation, and downstream VLA-based loco-manipulation through a shared command inter-face. 

A. Problem Formulation 

BFMs are pretrained by large-scale motion tracking, for-mulated as a discounted MDP. For embodiment r ∈ { s, t },the policy πr (ur | or , g r ) receives proprioception or and a motion reference gr , and outputs normalized joint commands 

ur . These define position targets for a PD controller; rewards measure motion-tracking quality, and transitions follow the embodiment’s dynamics. We primarily focus on transferring SONIC-based BFMs from a half-scale source humanoid s to a full-scale target humanoid t. Our goal is to accommodate differences in joint layouts and dynamics using limited target-side training while retaining the pretrained motion representation.

C. Motion Dataset Curation 

Efficient transfer requires reliable motion references within a limited data budget. We combine quality screening with category balancing and command-space distribution shaping to construct the transfer set. 

a) Retargeting and quality screening: We retarget the motion corpus to both embodiments, obtaining paired refer-ences for kinematic calibration and policy transfer. Quality screening rejects discontinuities, sustained joint-position or velocity-limit violations, unintended collisions, and implau-sible clip-level acceleration or jerk. Contact checks detect excessive penetration, support-point sliding, and missing intended support while preserving annotated object contacts. 

b) Category balancing: To avoid overrepresenting common motions, we divide a fixed frame budget equally across ten metadata-derived categories (Fig. 6(b)) in a fixed, quality-screened candidate pool. If a category has fewer frames than its quota, we retain all its clips. We redistribute the unused budget equally among categories with more frames than their quotas. 

c) Command-space distribution: To promote command-space dispersion within each category, we summarize frozen SONIC reference-encoder window features by their temporal means and standard deviations, forming a 128-dimensional clip descriptor. D. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning 

SONIC encodes references as Universal Tokens and com-bines them with proprioception in a Robot Control Decoder. We adapt reference encoding and feedback control separately to address residual representation and dynamics mismatch. 

a) Model architecture and LoRA insertion: As shown in Fig. 3, we retain the source reference motion encoders (Robot, Human, and Hybrid) as frozen branches. We initial-ize a single target Robot reference encoder from its source counterpart with the pre-adapted interface. All branches share the fixed finite scalar quantization (FSQ) operator. LoRA [27] augments linear layers in the target Robot reference encoder and Robot Control Decoder:

B = 0 , preserving their pre-adapted mappings at insertion. The critic uses LoRA in every linear layer except its trainable final scalar projection. The Robot Motion Decoder remains frozen and excluded from training because target-motion reconstruction is not required for feedback control. 

b) Command latent alignment: Preserving command-token semantics is a transfer requirement for reusing mul-titask interfaces, including VR teleoperation, motion gen-erators, and planners. Unconstrained end-to-end RL risks semantic drift, whereas freezing the reference encoder leaves residual kinematic-mapping errors that can shift token dis-tributions after nonlinear encoding and FSQ. We therefore fine-tune only the target Robot reference encoder against its frozen source counterpart to maintain command com-patibility and support stable, data-efficient controller fine-tuning. Let zs and zt denote their pre-FSQ command latents, and cr = Q(zr ), r ∈ { s, t }, their quantized token vectors under the fixed FSQ operator Q. We jointly align both representations by minimizing 

Lalign = ℓSL1 (zt, zs) + ℓSL1 (ct, cs) , (7) where the same smooth-L1 loss ℓSL1 measures both latent and token distances. 

c) Controller fine-tuning with PPO: During rollouts, we sample the retained source reference branches and the adapted target Robot branch according to prescribed mixture proportions and use their quantized tokens as commands. Each token remains paired with the corresponding target trajectory for tracking-reward evaluation. PPO updates the Robot Control Decoder’s LoRA parameters and the action-sampling variance, with gradients stopped at the token input; the critic receives the value loss. 

d) Two-phase transfer learning curriculum: Premature concentration of the training distribution on difficult mo-tions can disrupt the BFM’s pretrained motion knowledge. We therefore adopt a two-phase training curriculum within Stage 3. First, we sample from the balanced transfer-set distribution and strengthen existing global-position and end-effector tracking rewards to establish stable target tracking. At the phase transition, we merge LoRA updates into the base weights and reinitialize zero-output branches. Second, we adaptively prioritize motion segments with frequent failures or large tracking errors while retaining baseline sampling for coverage. We further strengthen end-effector tracking rewards and introduce terrain-interaction and ro-bustness regularization to improve precision and disturbance rejection. Final LoRA updates are merged for deployment. IV. E XPERIMENTS

C ONCLUSION 

TRACE demonstrates that a pretrained humanoid BFM can be efficiently adapted to a new embodiment while retaining a shared motion interface for tracking and down-stream tasks. The framework combines kinematic and nom-inal joint-response pre-alignment with a compact motion set selected for quality, category balance, and command-space dispersion. It decouples reference-encoder distillation from feedback-control optimization within a two-phase low-rank curriculum, establishing stable tracking before improving accuracy on difficult motions. TRACE transfers SONIC from Unitree G1 to H2 in approximately eight hours on a single GPU while retaining a broad motion-tracking repertoire. On physical H2, the same adapted controller supports dynamic motion tracking, teleoperated demonstration collection, and autonomous VLA tasks. Our evaluation is limited to one embodiment pair and one pretrained BFM. Future work will extend validation to more embodiments and open-source BFMs and investigate efficient adaptation without a reusable critic. For example, multi-expert distillation may provide no unified critic, whereas changes in training paradigm may make existing value esti-mates unsuitable for the new objective.
