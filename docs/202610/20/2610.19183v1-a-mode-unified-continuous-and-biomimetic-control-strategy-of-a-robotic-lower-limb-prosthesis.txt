# A Mode-Unified, Continuous, and Biomimetic Control Strategy of a Robotic Lower Limb Prosthesis 

Hanjun Kim 1, Sixu Zhou 1,2 , John Shim 1,2 , Xinyuan Guo 1, Kinsey R. Herrin 1,2 , and Aaron J. Young 1,2 , Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Existing biomimetic lower-limb prosthesis controllers are commonly designed for discrete locomotion modes, requiring separate parameterizations for level walking, ramps, and stairs. We present a mode-unified prosthesis control framework that represents knee and ankle biomechanics continuously as functions of terrain slope and gait phase across those locomotion conditions. Locally optimized ascent and descent polynomial models were unified using radial basis function (RBF) interpolation, preserving the accuracy of the partitioned models while outperforming a global polynomial representation. The resulting biomechanics were transformed into continuous and slope-dependent impedance parameters and implemented within a single finite-state-machine (FSM) controller using bilateral sensing for gait-state estimation. Experiments with 10 individuals with transfemoral amputation demonstrated terrain-adaptive locomotion from level walking through ramps and stairs up to ± 35 °, including 13 ° ramp and 15° stair conditions absent from the underlying able-bodied (AB) dataset. Experimental results reproduced the principal slope-dependent biomechanical trends predicted by the unified model, with high agreement for knee-related measures ( 𝒓𝒓 𝟐𝟐 = 0.82 - 0.99) and ankle kinematics ( 𝒓𝒓 𝟐𝟐 =

0.78 - 0.96). These findings demonstrated that unified biomechanical parameterization enables a prosthesis control architecture across diverse terrain conditions while preserving key features of AB biomechanics without requiring independently tuned impedance parameter sets for each locomotion mode.

prosthesis, unified biomechanics modeling, unified control.

These biomechanical data have been widely utilized in developing lower-limb wearable robots because reproducing human dynamics offers nominal control behaviors without costly user-specific tuning [6], [7], [8], [9]. 

Although available datasets encompass level walking, ramps, and stairs, they remain discrete and finite, so prosthesis controllers inherit that same limitation. Accordingly, extending them to new environments needs extra modeling and may produce substantial kinematic and kinetic errors in unseen conditions [10], [11]. This is especially critical for prostheses because users cannot compensate with missing joints. Prior work interpolated biomechanics across adjacent ramp inclines, [12], [13], stair heights [14], or walking speeds [15] improving scalability within a domain. But discontinuities remain between domains such as overground walking and stairs, which are still commonly modeled as separate tasks. Task-agnostic or unified prosthesis controllers have also been proposed to enable multiple ambulation activities without explicit terrain classification [16], [17]. These approaches improve versatility across locomotion tasks but generally do not prescribe healthy individuals’ joint biomechanics as continuous functions of terrain geometry. Existing methods therefore address either biomechanical fidelity within separate domains or unified control across tasks, leaving the simultaneous achievement of biomimicry and task-unified control largely unaddressed. 

Gait phase estimation is another fundamental component of lower-limb prosthesis control because user-environment interaction dynamics vary continuously throughout the gait cycle. Conventional finite-state-machine (FSM) controllers partition the gait cycle into discrete states and assign corresponding control parameters to each state [18], [19], [20], [21]. Among various strategies, impedance control remains widely adopted because of its simplicity, compliance, and ability to reproduce human-like joint mechanics [22], [23]. To replicate human joint behavior, impedance parameters are typically modulated according to the current gait phase [24], [25]. However, these FSM-based control frameworks are commonly designed in a mode-specific manner, where locomotion tasks such as level walking, ramps, and stairs are separately modeled using different gait-state transition conditions. For example, transitions from early to late stance are often triggered when the ankle reaches a predefined dorsiflexion angle, requiring user-specific tuning of the transition threshold. If the angle threshold is not reached during a stride, the controller may fail to progress to the subsequent state as intended. Such threshold-dependent transitions increase tuning complexity and can limit generalizability across environments and prosthesis users. Recent studies proposed continuous gait phase estimation

Nevertheless, these approaches continue to rely on predefined ambulation-mode classifications, particularly for stair locomotion, where distinct control structures or transition conditions remain necessary. Although gait phase evolves continuously, the overall controller remains partitioned across locomotion modes. 

To address these limitations, we propose a mode-unified prosthesis control framework that represents lower-limb biomechanics as continuous functions of terrain slope and gait phase. The proposed framework follows the hierarchical high-, mid-, and low-level control architecture commonly adopted in lower-limb prosthesis control [22], [23], [30]. First, knee and ankle biomechanics spanning level walking, ramps, and stairs were modeled within a continuous terrain slope and gait phase domain (Fig. 1A and Section II). Locally optimized models were developed separately for ascending and descending locomotion and subsequently unified while preserving their local biomechanical characteristics. Stance- and swing-phase representations were also modeled separately to retain phase-dependent locomotion dynamics. These unified biomechanical representations were then transformed into continuously slope-dependent impedance parameters for prosthesis control (Section III). Finally, the proposed framework was experimentally validated in Section IV. The resulting controller employs a bilateral sensing strategy to detect gait events from both-limb forward kinematics while maintaining a single FSM structure across all locomotion conditions. This architecture enables continuous modulation of prosthesis impedance with terrain slope without requiring mode-dependent gait-state definitions or independently tuned parameter sets. The proposed framework was experimentally evaluated across level walking, ramps, and stairs, including intermediate terrain conditions that were not explicitly represented in the underlying able-bodied (AB) biomechanical dataset (Fig. 1C). We tested two main hypotheses: (H1) the unified biomechanics model preserves the fidelity of the local ascent and descent fits and (H2) the proposed unified controller framework preserves the principal slope-dependent trends in healthy individuals’ biomechanics and interpolates these trends to intermediate conditions absent from the reference data. 

II. MODE-UNIFIED LOCOMOTION BIOMECHANICS 

This section develops continuous lower-limb biomechanical models parameterized by terrain slope and gait phase. First, partitioned polynomial models are constructed separately for the ascending and descending terrain domains from open-source locomotion datasets [1], [8]. These locally accurate representations are subsequently unified using radial basis function (RBF) interpolation, a well-established approach for constructing continuous multivariate functions from discrete observations [31], [32]. A. Locomotion Biomechanics Dataset 

We utilized knee and ankle kinematics (joint angles) and kinetics (joint moments) collected from healthy individuals [1], [8]. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, locomotion conditions consisted of level walking (LW, 0 °), ramp ascent/descent (RA / RD, ± 5 °, 7 °, 9 °, and 11 °), and stair ascent/descent (SA / SD, ± 20 °, 24 °, 30 °, 35 °). Four ramp and four stair conditions were selected to balance data distribution across locomotion modes. All kinematic and kinetic trajectories were segmented into stance and swing phases and phase-normalized to 101 samples. The LW trials were selected from the slow-speed condition, up to 0.6 m/s.

D. Validation of the Unified Biomechanics Representation 

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the leave-ramp-out models exhibited moderate degradation but retained reasonable reconstruction of the omitted ramp conditions, whereas the leave-stair-out models failed to extrapolate to stair locomotion. These leave-one-domain analyses were conducted to characterize cross-domain extrapolation rather than to construct the final unified model. The full-ascent and full-descent models, configurations (i) and (ii), achieved the best reconstruction performance across the evaluated configurations and were therefore selected for RBF unification, as described in Section II-C. 

The quantitative comparison focused on the partitioned polynomial, global polynomial, and proposed RBF-unified models using polynomials from (i) and (ii). As shown in Fig. 3, the RBF-unified models consistently achieved lower mean-absolute errors (MAEs) than the global polynomials, while preserving the accuracy of the partitioned polynomials. The only significant difference between the RBF-unified and partitioned models occurred for the ankle swing angle during descent locomotion, where the RBF-unified model achieved a lower MAE. 

The resulting RBF-unified models are shown in Fig. 2B, demonstrating continuous slope-phase trajectories that closely reproduce the underlying AB biomechanics. These unified biomechanics subsequently serve as the foundation for the impedance parameterization in Section III.

A. Biomechanically Inspired Equilibrium Angle Trajectory 

Previous studies identified stiffness, damping, and equilibrium 

angle simultaneously through optimization frameworks [6], [7]. 

In contrast, the proposed controller prescribes biomechanically 

inspired equilibrium angle trajectories and subsequently identifies 

stiffness and damping through constrained optimization. This 

design follows established bio-inspired prosthesis controllers 

[19], [22], [23], [25] while extending their parameterization to 

a continuous terrain slope domain.

biomechanical characteristics of the target moments. The

higher accuracies (0.75 ± 0.21 for the knee and 0.91 ± 0.05 for the

accurately reproduces the support and unloading dynamics 

prioritized by the controller despite the reduction in early stance 

reconstruction performance.

Within the unified FSM, the slope-dependent impedance parameters derived in Section III were executed using the load-reactive impedance strategy proposed by Simon et al. [25]. During level walking and ascending locomotion, stance phase stiffness and damping were continuously interpolated according to the normalized prosthetic side vertical load, while the equilibrium angle followed the prescribed trajectory shown in Fig. 5B. During descending locomotion, the same load-reactive interpolation was applied to stiffness and damping, whereas the equilibrium angle followed the proportional yielding strategy described in Section III. C. Experimental Protocol 

Ten individuals with unilateral transfemoral amputation (eight male and two female; 5 right and 5 left amputations or limb difference; age: 39.7 ± 11 years; body mass: 85.9 ± 13.9 kg; height: 1.74 ± 0.13 m; years since amputation: 17.9 ± 11.7 years) participated in this study. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the approval by the Georgia Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (H21117). 

Before testing, the prosthesis was fit and aligned by acertified prosthetist during quiet standing. Participants then completed a static calibration trial to initialize the wearable IMUs and establish a common global reference frame, followed by an acclimation period consisting of level walking (0 °), ramps (± 9 °), and stairs (± 24 °). After acclimation, swing-phase stiffness and damping were adjusted by up to 10% from their nominal values to achieve comfortable limb advancement. In addition, constant heel-contact angle offsets of up to 10° were introduced during descending locomotion to promote a more extended knee and reduced ankle plantarflexion at initial contact, improving heel-contact stability while preserving the continuous slope-dependent trajectories. This adjustment was introduced because direct reproduction of the AB heel-contact resulted in excessive knee flexion and ankle plantarflexion which was destabilizing in prosthesis users. 

The proposed controller was subsequently evaluated during level walking, ramp walking, and stair ambulation, as illustrated in Fig. 1C. Level walking and ramps (0 ° - 13 °) were performed on an instrumented incline/decline treadmill at 0.4 - 0.5 m/s, whereas stair ascent and descent were evaluated over slopes ranging from 15 ° to 35 °. The intermediate 13 ° ramp and 15 ° stair conditions were not represented in the underlying AB biomechanics dataset and were included to evaluate controller performance in terrain regions absent from the reference data. Due to experimental time constraints, the 13 ° ramp and 15 ° stair conditions were completed by 9 and 6 of the 10 participants, respectively. Participant inclusion in these conditions was determined by available testing time rather than K-level or task performance. Whole-body kinematics and ground reaction forces were recorded using a motion-capture system synchronized with an instrumented treadmill and stair force plates for post-hoc analysis. During stair ascent, participants initiated the first step with the intact limb, whereas the prosthetic limb served as the leading limb during descent in accordance with how stair negotiation is commonly trained in clinical practice. 

D. Statistical Analysis 

The weighted squared Pearson correlation coefficient ( 𝑟𝑟 2)was used to quantify how closely the experimentally measured prosthetic outcomes followed the slope-dependent trends of the corresponding model predictions and AB reference data. To account for the non-uniform spacing of terrain slopes, each slope was weighed according to the interval it represented within the slope domain, based on the spacing to adjacent slope values.

VI. DISCUSSION 

The primary contribution of this work is the demonstration that lower-limb prosthesis control can be formulated using a continuous biomechanical representation spanning level walking, ramps, and stairs, rather than as a collection of independently designed locomotion modes. Existing biomimetic prosthesis controllers generally derive impedance parameters from discrete AB locomotion datasets and therefore inherit the same mode-specific structure, requiring separate controller designs and transition logic for each ambulation task. In contrast, the proposed framework represents joint biomechanics as continuous functions of terrain slope and gait phase, enabling both biomechanical modeling and controller parameterization within a unified domain. This formulation shifts controller design from defining discrete locomotion modes to parameterizing a continuous environmental variable, providing a more scalable foundation for future terrain-adaptive prosthesis control and real-world mobility. The results support two hypotheses introduced in Section I. First, the RBF-unified biomechanics preserved the reconstruction accuracy of the locally optimized ascent and descent models while outperforming the global polynomial representation, supporting 

H1 . Second, experiments with ten individuals with transfemoral amputation demonstrated that the unified controller reproduced the principal slope-dependent biomechanical trends of healthy individuals, including intermediate terrain conditions absent from the reference dataset, supporting H2 . However, H2 was only partially supported for ankle stance kinetics, where the experimental torque trends exhibited lower agreement both with the unified model and AB reference. 

The proposed continuous formulation provides two advantages. First, it enables direct interpolation to intermediate terrain conditions not present in the reference dataset, as demonstrated experimentally for the 13 ° ramp and 15 ° stair conditions. Second, because the controller is parameterized continuously with respect to terrain slope, a single controller architecture can span the entire modeled terrain domain without introducing separate mode-specific parameter sets or additional FSM-based transitions. This reduces controller complexity while preserving continuous scaling in biomechanical behavior across the modeled terrain domain. 

The proposed RBF-unified biomechanics addresses afundamental limitation of polynomial-based locomotion models by reconciling local biomechanical fidelity with global continuity. While partitioned polynomial models accurately reconstructed each locomotion subset, directly concatenating them introduced discontinuities near the shared level-walking condition, whereas a single global polynomial excessively smoothed the local biomechanical characteristics. The RBF blending preserved the locally optimized biomechanics while providing a continuous representation suitable for real-time controller parameterization. Importantly, the objective of the continuous biomechanical representation is not to reconstruct unknown AB biomechanics exactly, but to provide a continuous biomimetic prior for controller design. This distinction is particularly relevant within the transition region between steep ramps and shallow stairs, where changes in foot-ground contact geometry may produce substantially different ankle biomechanics despite similar terrain inclinations. Therefore, the successful implementation at the 13 ° ramp and 15 ° stair conditions should be interpreted as validation of the continuous controller parameterization rather than confirmation of the interpolated AB biomechanics themselves. 

Although unified biomechanics provide continuous reference trajectories, directly reproducing AB joint kinematics and kinetics is neither feasible nor necessarily desirable for powered prostheses. The unified biomechanics served as a design prior rather than a direct control objective, requiring transformation into impedance parameters that balance biomimetic behavior with practical implementation. Rather than optimizing all three impedance parameters simultaneously [6], [7], [39], the proposed framework derived stiffness and damping after designing equilibrium angles (Fig. 5B) based on the bio-inspired support angle �𝜃𝜃𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠� definition (Fig. 4). The 𝜃𝜃𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠 

formulation, constrained optimization, and proportional yielding strategy [22], [23], [25] collectively reduced the controller dimensionality while preserving the biomechanical behaviors most relevant to prosthesis function, including limb support, controlled yielding, and swing clearance. 

Interestingly, the constrained LSQ optimization consistently converged to trapezoid-like stiffness and damping profiles throughout stance (Figs. 6A and 6B). These profiles emerged naturally from the optimization while closely following the prescribed arctangent upper-bound envelopes, indicating that the constraints actively regulated the resulting impedance parameters. An exception was observed for knee stiffness during ascending locomotion, where the optimized values remained below the allowable upper bound despite accurate moment reconstruction (Fig. 6C). This behavior suggests that slope-dependent knee support was achieved primarily through modulation of the equilibrium angle trajectory rather than increasing joint stiffness, highlighting the complementary roles of stiffness and equilibrium angle within the proposed parameterization.

joint dynamics. This was particularly important during descending locomotion, where the initial knee moment peak obtained from inverse dynamics represents the net mechanical moment at the biological joint rather than a direct actuator torque requirement. Such net moments reflect the combined effects of active muscle forces and passive musculoskeletal structures [40] and therefore cannot be interpreted as one-to-one motor commands. Specifically, during stair descent, the biological knee extensors dissipate energy eccentrically while the knee flexes to lower the body [41]. Therefore, directly reproducing the initial moment peak with a large prosthetic support torque would oppose the controlled knee yielding required to “ride” the prosthesis during descent [25]. Beyond this departure from the AB knee moment during descent, several additional design choices intentionally prioritized prosthesis functionality over one-to-one reproduction of natural biomechanics. The ankle support equilibrium angle was bounded to 10 ° dorsiflexion to remain within the prosthesis operating range and preserve sufficient plantarflexion torque. Stance biomechanics were represented through a three-region equilibrium angle structure, with stiffness and damping constrained to physically realizable bounds and progression patterns rather than directly reproducing the full AB moment trajectory. During swing, the controller similarly targeted functional landmarks (e.g. maximum flexion for limb clearance and heel-contact angle for preparation for loading) rather than tracking the complete continuous AB kinematic trajectory. Collectively, these design choices preserved the biomechanical functions considered most relevant to prosthesis control while intentionally relaxing exact replication of natural joint behavior. 

Remaining discrepancies primarily reflect implementation choices rather than limitations of the underlying biomechanics. The largest kinematic differences occurred at heel-contact for both joints, consistent with the constant heel-contact angle offsets introduced to improve prosthetic stability during initial contact (Section IV-C). The ankle kinetic discrepancy highlights a tradeoff introduced by the mode-unified state transition strategy. In conventional mode-specific control, the ES-to-LS transition can incorporate an ankle angle threshold, ensuring that sufficient dorsiflexion develops before entering late stance and thereby preserving the angular displacement �𝜃𝜃 − 𝜃𝜃𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒� 

required for modeled torque. Such thresholds, however, vary substantially across different modes and are difficult to retain within a single mode-independent transition rule. The proposed bilateral limb-progression criterion removes this dependence but decouples the ES-to-LS transition from the desired peak dorsiflexion angle. Consequently, LS may be initiated before sufficient dorsiflexion develops, limiting the angular displacement and the resulting ankle torque. This consideration is particularly relevant during steep stair descent, where the RBF-unified ankle dorsiflexion trajectory can exceed the mechanical range of the current prosthesis (Fig. 9C, Ankle LS Peak Dorsiflexion for -20 °, -24 °, -30 °, and -35 °), making direct reproduction of the reference kinematics infeasible. Accordingly, strategies such as prescribing a more plantarflexed equilibrium angle or accelerating the progression of impedance parameters may help generate greater stance ankle torque without requiring full reproduction of the modeled dorsiflexion. Such modifications may be particularly important during late stance, consistent with prior findings highlighting the biomechanical importance of push-off timing and power generation [42], [43], [44]. 

A further difference was observed during the swing extension, where the experimental trajectories exhibited plateau regions immediately before initial contact (Fig. 8A). This behavior is consistent with terminal-swing stabilization strategies previously reported in unilateral prosthesis users. Prior studies have identified a prolonged latency between full prosthetic knee extension and initial contact, interpreted as a period during which users ensure a stable position before loading [45], and have reported corresponding adaptations in terminal-swing muscle activation [46]. More recent work further demonstrated that when prosthetic knee extension was completed earlier, users tended to preserve or increase this latency rather than initiate ground contact earlier [47]. Thus, the observed plateau may reflect user-selected preparation for stable initial contact rather than a limitation of the continuous biomechanical representation. 

Beyond the impedance parameter formulation, successful controller implementation also relied on the proposed bilateral sensing architecture (Fig. 7). Compared with conventional mode-specific controllers, this architecture eliminates mode-dependent gait state definitions while preserving a single FSM across all modeled terrain conditions. The relative limb geometry was estimated using a simplified sagittal-plane forward kinematics model based on wearable IMUs, without explicitly accounting for out-of-plane pelvic motion or hip tilt. Consequently, the computed geometry should be interpreted as a robust gait state variable rather than an anatomically exact estimate of bilateral limb configuration. Despite this simplification, the experimental results demonstrated reliable gait state transitions across all evaluated terrain conditions, suggesting that the proposed geometry provides sufficient information for unified controller implementation. 

For additional reference, the biomechanical replication results of the proposed controller were examined alongside previously reported results using a mode-specific, hand-tuned controller [24], as detailed in Appendix and Fig. 10. Although both studies evaluated common terrain conditions (± 5 ° ramps and ± 24 ° stairs), they involved different participant groups, experimental protocols, and hardware capabilities. In specific, the previous study employed the first-generation OSL [35], with ± 15 ° of ankle motion, whereas the proposed study used the second-generation OSL [36], with an expanded range of ± 30 °. This hardware difference is particularly relevant to stair locomotion, where the AB-referenced ankle heel-contact and swing clearance angles exceeded the physical limit of the earlier OSL. Therefore, the observed differences in biomechanical replication cannot be attributed solely to the control strategies, and the comparison should be interpreted as descriptive rather than a direct assessment of controller superiority. The present study has several limitations that motivate future research. First, the proposed controller assumed continuous terrain slope information as a high-level input. Although the controller architecture is independent of how this information is obtained, integrating onboard slope estimation methods based on wearable sensing [6], [9], [10] or environment perception [26], [50], [51] will be necessary for fully autonomous operation. 

Second, the proposed continuous biomechanics were derived from AB datasets collected under self-selected or relatively slow walking speeds (up to 0.6m/s). Walking speed was not explicitly included as a model parameter, and the resulting impedance representation is expected to be most applicable within similar speed ranges. Extending the framework to incorporate continuous speed adaptation represents an important direction for future work and could enable unified control across both terrain and walking speed variations [6], [15], [52]. 

Third, the unified biomechanics were constructed by blending independently modeled ramp and stair datasets. The interpolated biomechanics within the transition region between steep ramps and shallow stairs should be interpreted as controller parameterization rather than true AB biomechanics.

VII. CONCLUSION 

This paper presented a mode-unified biomimetic control framework that represents lower-limb biomechanics continuously as functions of terrain slope and gait phase. The RBF-unified models preserved the accuracy of locally optimized biomechanics while enabling continuous impedance parameterization across level walking, ramps, and stairs. Experiments with ten prosthesis users demonstrated continuous terrain adaptation, including 13 ° ramp and 15 ° stair conditions absent from the reference AB dataset. The proposed controller reproduced the principal slope-dependent trends ( 𝑟𝑟 2 = 0.82 -0.99 for all knee-related measures and 0.78 - 0.96 for ankle kinematics), while ankle stance torque remained less accurately reproduced. Together, these findings support our unified biomimetic control framework that reduces mode-specific tuning while enabling users to address varied terrain without relying on separately parameterized controllers for each locomotion modes.
