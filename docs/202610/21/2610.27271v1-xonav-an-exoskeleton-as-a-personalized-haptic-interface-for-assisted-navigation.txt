XoNav: An Exoskeleton as a Personalized Haptic Interface for
Assisted Navigation
Debadrata Sarkar, Soumen Sen, Aman Arora *
Supplementary video: A video illustrating the XoNav hardware, haptic cues, Wizard-of-Oz trials
and autonomous navigation is available at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.34701648
Abstract—Walking without vision requires timely, unambigu-
ous guidance, yet many wearable aids encode directions as audio
or vibrotactile signals that users must translate into movement.
We present XoNav, a four-actuator hip exoskeleton that renders
guidance cue directly at the hip joint. It renders Advance
cue and two-level Braking cues through sagittal-plane actuators
and two-level Steering cues through frontal-plane actuators.
A bilateral LSTM-based gait-state classifier uses two thigh-
mounted IMUs to identify gait states, enabling haptic cues
to be delivered in coordination with the wearer’s gait while
suppressing them during standing. The classifier generalized to
unseen users with recalls of 95.2–97.9%, with mean detection
delays of 38.6 and 63.1 ms for stance-to-swing and swing-to-stance
transitions respectively. Constrained human-in-the-loop Bayesian
optimization personalized Braking for minimum resistance and
Steering for maximum comfort while ensuring detectability and
safety. All participants reliably distinguished between grades of
their personalized cues and the firmest Braking cue remained
below 53% of the safety limit. In blindfolded Wizard-of-Oz
navigation trials along three paths, the participants kept 98.8%
of the walked distance within a 0.75 m wide passage, with a
4.9% increase in mean progression speed per trial. A body-
worn camera, RTAB-Map and Nav2 further enabled a feasibility
study of autonomous navigation and detours around previously
unmapped obstacles.
Index Terms—Wearable robotics, haptics and haptic interfaces,
prosthetics and exoskeletons, physical human–robot interaction,
robot navigation.

Body-worn haptic interfaces offer hands-free guidance, al-
though a survey found that many walking aids still depend on
Wizard-of-Oz operators and are not ready for daily use [7].
Vibrotactile belts reduce collisions during training [8], instru-
mented walker handles can improve confidence [9], and textile
interfaces distribute haptic feedback across the body [10], [11].
Vibrotactile directions nevertheless require interpretation as
locomotor actions, while audio competes with ambient cues.
Kinesthetic interfaces instead communicate movement through
force, as demonstrated by the resistance and pulling rendered
by the Kinethreads exosuit [12].
Lower-limb exoskeletons can convey guidance at the
joints responsible for walking. Recent works include multi-
activity [13] as well as task-agnostic [14] joint-moment as-
sistance, data-driven community ambulation [15] and online
personalization [16], frontal-plane control of step width and
balance [17], [18], and improved haptic transparency [19].
Variability in individual responses have motivated human-in-
the-loop optimization (HILO) [20], [21], including Bayesian
optimization [22] and preference-based methods [23], [24].
Adding vibrotactile signals to exoskeleton torques can modu-
late walking speed but may increase cognitive load [25].
Closest to our work, Carrasquillo et al. [26] used artificial
potential fields to modulate lower-limb exoskeleton impedance
for obstacle avoidance. In virtual reality with occluded vision,
this increased clearance and reduced collisions, demonstrating
the potential of exoskeleton force feedback for human navi-
gation. However, four gaps remain for real-world wayfinding.
First, directional cueing through the hip’s frontal-plane degrees
of freedom, which govern mediolateral foot placement and
heading, remains unexplored. Second, guidance has not been
phase-locked to gait events, potentially displacing an unloaded
swing limb and overriding, rather than informing, motor intent.
Third, navigation cues have used fixed parameters despite
individual differences in perception and comfort, while HILO
has not been formulated to jointly satisfy detectability, safety
and comfort. Finally, reliance on external tracking leaves
untested whether egocentric localization from a body-worn
camera, despite gait-induced sway, can couple a navigation
stack [27] with a human walker.
Here, we present XoNav, a hip exoskeleton serving as a
personalized haptic interface for assisted navigation (Fig. 1).
XoNav renders Advance and two-level Braking cues in the
sagittal plane and two-level Steering cues in the frontal plane.
Each cue is gait-informed, being released at its gait event

II. XONAV HARDWARE
XoNav is a bilateral, four-degree-of-freedom hip exoskel-
eton (Fig. 1). Each side has a coaxial flexion–extension (FE)
actuator and a posteriorly mounted abduction–adduction (AA)
actuator that drives the thigh through waist and thigh links.
All four are quasi-direct-drive AK80-9 actuators (CubeMars,
China) with 9:1 reduction and MD80 drivers (MAB Robotics,
Poland). The low reduction ratio limits backdrive torque,
allowing unimpeded walking without cues. Adjustable waist
and thigh links and padded cuffs accommodate users and
transfer torques (Fig. 1(c)). Two BNO085 IMUs (Adafruit
Industries, USA) are mounted on the thigh cuffs and a third
on the torso. A chest-mounted RealSense D435i (Intel, USA)
provides stereo and inertial measurements for navigation. Total
system mass is 4.3 kg.
The backpack (Fig. 1(b)) contains a 6S 13.5 Ah Li-ion
battery, power distribution, an emergency cutoff switch and
an event marking trigger switch for data collection. A Jetson
Orin Nano (NVIDIA, USA) running ROS 2 Humble performs
gait inference, cue generation and navigation. It communicates
with the motor drivers over USB-to-CAN (CANdle, MAB
Robotics) and receives IMU measurements through an ESP32
(Espressif Systems).
Each joint is driven in impedance mode (Fig. 1(d)),

III. GAIT-INFORMED HAPTIC CUES
XoNav renders four everyday navigation commands as hap-
tic cues at the hips, namely Advance (keep walking), Braking
(slow down or stop), Steering (turn) and Neutral (no guidance)
(Fig. 2). Their mechanical effects and gait-dependent timing
are described below.

nal hip extension, identified by the extension-to-flexion zero
crossing of thigh angular velocity, the FE actuator produces
a brief trapezoidal flexion torque τff with KP = KD = 0
(Fig. 2(a)). The perceived gentle forward pull accompanies
the wearer’s ensuing step rather than initiating one.
The Braking cue asks the wearer to slow down or to stop.
The FE actuator renders a virtual spring–damper about the
neutral hip angle (qd = ˙qd = 0) whose gains are switched by

B. Gait-Informed Rendering
XoNav times its cues to accompany wearer-initiated move-
ment rather than initiating it. Steering begins immediately
during turning-side stance or waits until the next heel strike if
commanded during swing, after which the full profile executes.
With the foot loaded, the abduction cue is conveyed through
the pelvis and trunk rather than displacing the swing foot and
pilot wearers found it more perceptible than during swing. All
cues are suppressed during standing.
A bilateral classifier estimates Standing, Stance and Swing
for each leg using only two thigh-IMU pitch signals sampled
at 100 Hz. Every 50 ms, seven causal features (mean, RMS,
maximum, minimum, range, net change and standard devia-
tion) are computed for each signal over the preceding 300 ms.
Six consecutive feature vectors (550 ms total history) feed
a shared long short-term memory (LSTM) layer with sepa-
rate classification heads, requiring two consecutive identical
predictions to confirm a state transition. Heel and toe force-
sensitive resistors provide stance and swing labels, while event
triggers mark standing intervals, both used only for training.
The classifier was trained on recordings from ten par-
ticipants. The deployed model achieved recalls of 99.7%,
95.8% and 98.4% for Standing, Stance and Swing, respectively
(Fig. 3(a)). Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) validation, avoiding
leakage from overlapping windows and assessing unseen-user
generalization, yielded bilateral recalls of 97.9±2.5%, 95.2±
1.3% and 97.1 ± 1.7%, respectively, with comparable perfor-
mance across legs (Fig. 3(b)). On the Jetson, the classifier ran
at 20 Hz with an inference latency of 18.2 ± 4.1 ms. Before
two-prediction confirmation, transition detection delays were
38.6 ± 35.3 ms (stance-to-swing) and 63.1 ± 31.8 ms (swing-
to-stance). Standing-to-swing transitions took 37.5 ± 25.0 ms,
while transitions to Standing took 41.4±117.3 ms from Stance
and 90.0±108.4 ms from Swing. These delays permit prompt

IV. HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP CUE PERSONALIZATION
Haptic cues must be reliably distinguishable without unnec-
essarily interfering with natural walking, yet wearers differ in
their perception of hip forces and motions and in comfort
preferences. XoNav therefore personalizes its graded cues
through constrained human-in-the-loop Bayesian optimization,
using wearer feedback to balance detectability, comfort and
safety (Fig. 4). Both loops share the evaluation and constraint-
handling procedures described below. Sessions lasted 30–
50 min, with rest breaks on request.

B. Braking Cue Personalization
Braking personalization seeks the lowest resistance that
allows wearers to reliably distinguish the Slow-down and Stop
cues, thereby minimizing interference with natural walking.
The parameter set X = (K0, ∆K), with K0 ∈[10, 150] and
∆K ∈[10, 190] N·m/rad, minimizes the mean commanded

their application to the wearer. The penalties use β0 = 175
and β1 = 160, with 20 evaluations per session (five random,
15 guided). Two Braking levels were adopted after pilot trials
showed that an intermediate third level could not be reliably
distinguished from its neighbors.

D. Results
In representative sessions (Fig. 5(a), (b)), infeasible Braking
candidates were penalized either before rendering for exceed-
ing the safety limit or afterward for poor detectabiility. The
optimizer then moved toward low base stiffness, selecting the
best candidate at evaluation 18. For Steering, a small amplitude
increment failed the detection criterion and was penalized,
while the best candidate was selected at evaluation 10. Clip-
ping the chained comfort score to [0, 1] prevents unbounded
accumulation across comparisons and keeps its cost below the
penalty offset. Although the score thereby saturates, ties at the
ceiling retain the current best, deliberately favoring a stable
selection over differences that pilot wearers could not reliably
perceive between similarly comfortable profiles.
Across ten participants (Fig. 5(c), (d)), Braking converged to
low Slow-down stiffness (K0 = 10.7–25.7 N·m/rad) and sub-
stantially higher Stop stiffness (∆K = 55.5–80.1 N·m/rad),
a Stop-to-Slow-down ratio of 5.2 ± 1.1. Relative contrast
mattered more than absolute stiffness. In one representative
session, a base near 85 N·m/rad failed detection even though
a similar increment passed at a base near 10 N·m/rad. This
agrees with Weber’s law: discriminable increments rise with
baseline stimulus intensity. The resulting Stop stiffness was
72–105 N·m/rad, no more than 53% of the safety limit. The
consistent optima suggest K0 ≈17 and ∆K ≈67 N·m/rad as
starting values for shorter future sessions.
Steering preferences varied more widely, with ∆θ = 6.5–
15.7◦(Sharp amplitudes 1.4–2.0 times Gentle) and Ts = 0.34–
0.63 s. Preferred profiles were brief enough for single-stance

larger amplitudes also favored longer durations, suggesting
sensitivity to abduction rate. Frontal-plane displacement was
discriminated at smaller relative contrasts than sagittal-plane
resistance. Thus, Braking showed a shared low-intensity pat-
tern, whereas Steering required individual comfort tuning; all
selected settings met the detection criterion ˆa ≥0.85.
V. WIZARD-OF-OZ NAVIGATION STUDY
To evaluate navigation using the personalized cues inde-
pendently of perception, localization and planning errors, we
adopted a Wizard-of-Oz protocol [7]. A trained operator issued
navigation commands, while the untethered exoskeleton ren-
dered the corresponding gait-informed cues as in autonomous
operation.
A. Participants and Protocol
Ten healthy male participants (age 28.1 ± 4.9 years; height
166.3±5.9 cm; mass 67.3±8.7 kg; eight right-leg dominant)
navigated blindfolded using their personalized cues. U-, N-
and 8-shaped paths of 10.5, 12.5 and 17 m were marked as
0.75 m-wide passages, approximately 1.5 times the combined
hip width of the wearer and exoskeleton (Fig. 6). An operator
in the arena issued Advance, Braking and Steering commands
at predefined cue zones using a Bluetooth keyboard connected
to the onboard computer, avoiding network-based ROS 2
teleoperation delays. Each participant completed one sighted
baseline per path to characterize natural head sway, followed
by five blindfolded trials per path (150 guided trials). A
motion-capture system (PhaseSpace, USA) tracked a head-
mounted LED marker throughout.
B. Metrics and Analysis
Trajectories were referenced to each path’s centerline. Pro-
gression speed is centerline advancement divided by time;
corridor adherence is the fraction of walked distance inside the
passage. Lateral wander accumulates head displacement side
to side per metre of progress, capturing weaving even when
mean offset is small. Corrective reversals count per-metre
lateral-drift changes exceeding 50 mm, and RMS heading error
measures deviation from path heading. To remove baseline
head sway and marker variability, each participant’s same-path
sighted value was subtracted from wander and, in quadrature,
from heading error.
Adaptation across trials was assessed using a linear mixed-
effects model of log-transformed metrics, with trial number
and path as fixed effects and a participant-by-path random
intercept. The trial coefficient estimated percentage change per
repetition, accounting for individual and path differences.

maintaining high corridor adherence.
VI. AUTONOMOUS NAVIGATION FEASIBILITY
A chest-mounted camera sways and changes tilt and height
with the wearer’s gait, complicating localization compared
with wheeled platforms. Since prior exoskeleton navigation
was evaluated with external tracking in virtual environ-
ments [26], we tested whether onboard sensing alone could
support closed-loop XoNav guidance.
A. Localization and Navigation Stack
The furnished laboratory arena was mapped using RTAB-
Map with the chest-mounted D435i in stereo-inertial mode.
Infrared stereo images (848×480 px, 30 Hz; emitter off) pro-
vided visual odometry, while the camera IMU, fused through a
Madgwick filter, provided gravity-aligned orientation. During
navigation, RTAB-Map localized against the saved map, using
the camera frame as the wearer reference so that heading and
look-ahead were evaluated relative to the trunk.
Nav2 [27] used 0.05 m-resolution costmaps combining the
static map, live depth-point-cloud obstacles (marking 0.3–
3.0 m, clearing to 4.0 m) and inflation (0.45 m radius,
scaling factor 5.0) around a 0.30 m-radius circular footprint. A
Dijkstra-based NavFn global planner generated paths, while a
Dynamic Window Approach-based (DWB) controller tracked
them at 10 Hz within an 8 × 8 m rolling window. Its
configuration included human-compatible acceleration limits
of 0.5 m/s2 and 1.0 rad/s2, a 3 s trajectory simulation horizon
and a 1.2 m/s speed ceiling.
To avoid unsafe maneuvers during vision-occluded walking,
recovery was limited to costmap clearing and waiting rather
than rotation or reversing. Goal arrival required only a position

B. From Velocity Commands to Haptic Cues
A ROS 2 node converts smoothed Nav2 velocity commands
(vx, ωz) into discrete haptic cues. Steering takes priority
when |ωz| > 0.25 rad/s and persists until it falls below
0.15 rad/s, with direction set by the sign of ωz. Otherwise,
vx ≥0.55 m/s triggers Advance, retained until vx < 0.45 m/s,
while lower forward velocities trigger Slow-down. Hysteresis
and a minimum cue duration of 0.8 s prevent chattering, with
Braking and Neutral exempted for prompt response. Unlike
velocity-derived Slow-down, Stop overrides other cues upon
goal arrival or obstacle entry into the Nav2 collision monitor’s
stop zone. It remains active until the classifier detects standing,
after which Neutral is activated. When Stop is inactive, Neutral
is also activated if no velocity commands are received for
0.75 s. The velocities convey direction and urgency rather than
impose walking speeds.
C. Trials and Outcomes
Three participants each completed five nominal start-to-
goal trials and three trials with an obstacle placed along the
planned route but absent from the saved map, totaling 24
trials. Fig. 8(b) shows representative nominal paths of one
participant, while Fig. 8(c) illustrates an obstacle trial in which
the live point cloud registered the added obstacle and Nav2
replanned around the central island, allowing the participant
to follow the cues safely to the goal. The cue sequence of a
nominal trial (Fig. 8(d)) shows Advance predominating along
straight segments, Steering at turns and Slow-down during the
final approach.
Fourteen of 15 nominal trials reached the goal, although
two involved light furniture contact followed by recovery
without assistance. The remaining trial failed when sudden
localization drift disrupted planning. Similarly, eight of nine
obstacle trials successfully followed replanned detours, while
delayed obstacle registration in the remaining trial prevented
replanning and resulted in a collision. Across all trials, walking

VII. USER FEEDBACK
Participants rated nine aspects of XoNav after the experi-
ments (Fig. 9). High ratings for cue clarity, distinguishability
and control over walking supported the gait-informed guidance
approach, while cue timing and comfort also scored above
neutral. Wearability showed the greatest variation, suggesting
scope for reducing device mass and improving fit. Trust and
perceived safety remained favorable despite blindfolding. The
three autonomous-navigation participants rated independent
wayfinding positively, with overall acceptance receiving the
highest rating.

VIII. CONCLUSION
XoNav demonstrates how a hip exoskeleton can serve as
a personalized, gait-informed haptic interface that conveys
navigation guidance directly through joint motion rather than
symbolic audio or vibration. Its four actuators render Advance,
two-level Braking and Steering cues across the sagittal and
frontal hip planes, coordinated with wearer-initiated movement
through a classifier using only two thigh IMUs. The classifier
achieved 95.2–97.9% leave-one-subject-out recall, with gait
transitions detected within tens of milliseconds, enabling gait-
dependent cue delivery and suppression during standing.
Constrained human-in-the-loop optimization yielded reli-
ably distinguishable Braking cues with minimal resistance and
individually comfortable Steering cues across ten participants,
with maximum Stop stiffness remaining below 53% of the
safety bound. This personalization allowed guidance without
unnecessarily restraining natural walking. In 150 blindfolded
Wizard-of-Oz trials, participants maintained 98.8% corridor
adherence within a 0.75 m passage while improving progres-
sion speed by an estimated 4.9% per trial. The same cues also
supported autonomous navigation using a body-worn camera,
RTAB-Map and Nav2, including detours around unmapped
obstacles. Together, these findings demonstrate the potential
of personalized kinesthetic guidance for assisted navigation,
although validation remains limited to blindfolded sighted
participants in controlled indoor environments. Future work
will evaluate XoNav with people with visual impairment
against white-cane navigation, improve device wearability and
extend autonomy to less structured environments.
