Humanoid robots are envisioned to perform diverse human-level tasks through
their anthropomorphic morphology. Their dual-arm configuration enables a
broad range of everyday actions. Yet, as in humans, this morphology can con-
strain loco-manipulation when both arms are occupied or additional contacts are
required beyond their reach. Supernumerary robotic limbs (SRLs) offer a way
to extend these capabilities, but their integration requires the added limb to be
coordinated with whole-body motion and balance. In this study, we develop and
integrate a supernumerary arm into a humanoid platform to investigate how mor-
phological augmentation reshapes its feasible action space. Through quantitative
analysis and hardware experiments across two configurations, we show how the
additional limb expands whole-body interaction capabilities by enabling manipu-
lation and support to be coordinated beyond the constraints of the native dual-arm
configuration. To control this augmented morphology, we leverage model-based
trajectory optimization (TO) to generate dynamically feasible motions for goal-
conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) controllers. The controllers are trained
in simulation and transferred to hardware in a zero-shot manner, enabling coor-
dinated whole-body skills for contact-rich tri-arm loco-manipulation. We further
show vision-guided autonomous execution on selected tasks using diffusion poli-

Introduction
Humans evolved a pair of versatile forelimbs adapted for skilled interaction with tools and complex
environments. This bilateral arm configuration provides an effective combination of dexterity and
redundancy, supporting a wide range of bimanual actions and fine control across diverse scenarios.
Yet this morphology also imposes inherent limitations on reachable workspace and the availability
of arms for additional interaction. In tasks such as complex assembly and cooperative lifting,
maintaining existing contacts can restrict access to other task locations, even with adaptive whole-
body coordination. When both arms are occupied, additional manipulation or support may require
reorganizing the task or seeking assistance. Humanoid robots inherit this anthropomorphic dual-arm
design, enabling them to operate in human-centered environments while exhibiting increasingly
capable whole-body coordination across locomotion and manipulation (1–4). Although whole-body
strategies broaden the ways these robots interact with their surroundings, the availability and reach
of the native arms continue to constrain their loco-manipulation capabilities. These constraints
become particularly consequential when both arms are committed to an ongoing task or when
additional contacts are required across spatially separated regions.
Supernumerary robotic limbs (SRLs) for humans have demonstrated how independently ac-
tuated appendages can expand the available action space (5–7). Users can learn to coordinate
additional degrees of freedom (DoFs) alongside their natural limbs, enabling concurrent interac-
tions beyond those supported by the native body alone (8, 9). SRLs have also been explored for
load sharing and physical support during manipulation, including overhead tasks and assembly,
allowing the added limb to assist while the natural arms remain engaged (10–13). These studies
motivate morphological augmentation as a means of extending physical interaction capabilities
through additional functional contacts.
Motivated by these findings, we investigate SRLs as a morphological augmentation for hu-
manoid robots. We develop a lightweight four-DoF supernumerary arm that can be mounted on
the humanoid’s neck or back, with one additional arm used in each configuration. The added limb
functions as a complement to the native arms, enabling new modes of multi-contact interaction
and parallel manipulation. Through quantitative analysis and hardware experiments, we investigate
2


how this augmentation reshapes the feasible action space and expands whole-body interaction ca-
pabilities. In particular, we study how loco-manipulation and support can be coordinated across the
augmented body beyond the constraints of its native dual-arm configuration. Realizing this augmen-
tation requires more than controlling an additional manipulator. The arm’s kinematic structure and
attachment must provide useful access while remaining compatible with the existing body’s motion.
Its added mass and movement couple to whole-body dynamics, while environmental contacts may
provide support or introduce disturbances. These couplings require the added limb’s motion and
contact forces to be coordinated with the rest of the body to maintain balance.
Model-based control methods have been fundamental to advances in humanoid whole-body
behaviors (2,14), enabling coordinated loco-manipulation through explicit consideration of robot
dynamics and contact constraints (15,16). Their real-time implementation requires balancing model
fidelity against computational cost. Simplified models enable more tractable online optimiza-
tion (17,18), whereas full-body (15,16,19,20) or kino-dynamic (21,22) formulations capture more
detailed system dynamics but place greater demands on computation and contact-state estimation.
Control performance can also be affected by uncertainty in robot dynamics and deviations from the
assumed contact conditions, particularly during complex physical interactions. For SRL-augmented
humanoids, the additional DoFs and broader range of contact configurations can further increase
these demands, especially when the native limbs and SRL must coordinate different interaction
roles while maintaining whole-body balance.
Learning-based approaches offer a complementary approach to humanoid whole-body control.
Reinforcement learning (RL), in particular, has shown hardware-deployable behaviors by leveraging
large-scale simulation and training across variations in robot dynamics and environmental condi-
tions (1). However, learning complex whole-body behaviors from scratch can require substantial
interaction data and extensive reward design. RL-based motion imitation has been widely explored
for humanoid whole-body control, using reference motions to provide structured guidance for skill
acquisition (23–26). A common approach retargets human motion to the robot’s morphology, pro-
viding supervision for learning versatile and human-like whole-body behaviors (27–29). However,
differences in embodiment mean that retargeted trajectories do not necessarily satisfy the robot’s
kinematic and dynamic constraints, particularly during contact-rich interaction. Consequently, this
embodiment gap can limit motion-tracking fidelity and the range of demonstrated behaviors that
the robot can reliably reproduce. For humanoids augmented with SRLs, human demonstrations
introduce a further challenge, as they do not directly specify the additional limb’s motion or its
coordination with native structures. Human motion data can inform the existing body’s behav-
ior, but additional motion synthesis is needed to provide complete references for the augmented
morphology.
Model-based contorl and optimization has also been integrated with RL to provide physically
grounded guidance for whole-body control (26,30–33). In particular, optimized motion references
3


can guide policy learning while accounting for robot dynamics and physical constraints. This
integration has been explored in quadrupedal control (30, 34, 35) and humanoid locomotion (33,
36–38). Within this line of work, Opt2Skill (26) combines full-order-dynamics-based TO with
RL for humanoid whole-body loco-manipulation. Dynamically feasible motion references improve
tracking and task performance, while the associated joint-torque information provides additional
supervision for contact-rich skills. This establishes a foundation for learning whole-body skills with
physically consistent guidance tailored to the robot’s embodiment.
Building on this foundation, we adapt the TO-RL framework for whole-body control of the
SRL-augmented humanoid. The framework combines a TO-generated library of whole-body loco-
manipulation motions with a set of goal-conditioned RL controllers. TO incorporates the augmented
robot’s full-body dynamics and prescribed contact constraints together with joint-torque limits,
providing physically consistent supervision for coordinated motion of the native limbs and SRL. The
optimized trajectories span single-arm, dual-arm and tri-arm interaction modes and guide controller
training in simulation. To improve training efficiency and learn deployable controllers under partial
observability, we adopt the Learn-to-Teach (L2T) privileged-learning framework (1, 39, 40). A
teacher policy learns to track task-conditioned end-effector references using privileged simulator
information, while a student policy learns to reproduce the teacher’s behavior from proprioceptive
observations and the same task references. The two policies are trained concurrently, reusing
simulation experience without requiring a separate student-distillation stage. The resulting student
controllers are transferred to hardware in a zero-shot manner to execute coordinated whole-body
behaviors with the augmented morphology.
To demonstrate that the learned whole-body controllers can also be driven by perception, we
integrate a vision-based diffusion policy (DP) (41) for selected autonomous tasks. The DP serves
as a high-level perceptual planner that conditions on RGB-D observations and robot proprioception
to predict short-horizon end-effector targets. These targets are executed by the task-specific RL
controllers, allowing the augmented humanoid to perform closed-loop tri-arm behaviors from
visual observations. This extension provides a vision-guided interface to the whole-body control
framework without changing the underlying low-level controllers.
We evaluate the proposed system through quantitative analysis and hardware experiments on
both SRL configurations (Fig. 1). The results show that the additional limb reshapes the humanoid’s
feasible action space and enables whole-body interaction beyond the constraints of the native dual-
arm morphology. The TO-RL controllers transfer from simulation to hardware in a zero-shot manner
and support coordinated tri-arm behaviors across manipulation, support, and dynamic whole-body
interaction. We further demonstrate closed-loop autonomous execution on selected tasks using the
vision-based diffusion policy. These results establish supernumerary limbs as a viable means of
extending humanoid morphology and broadening its physical interaction capabilities.

Results
Morphology and Workspace Augmentation
We evaluate how the added limb reshapes the humanoid’s feasible action space through two
mounting configurations: a neck-mounted embodiment that extends frontal and overhead reach, and
a back-mounted embodiment that enlargers rearward and downward access (Fig. 2A; Movie S1).
Both configurations use the same 4-DoF arm module, but produce distinct augmentation patterns
due to differences in attachment kinematics and whole-body coupling. These embodiments allow
us to characterize how supernumerary morphology expands reachable workspace, modifies local
dexterity, and influences whole-body support feasibility across spatial configurations.
Fig. 2B summarizes the reachable-space augmentation introduced by the added limb. The 3D
workspace overlays in Fig. 2B(i) show that the neck-mounted configuration preferentially expands
frontal and overhead access, whereas the back-mounted configuration extends the robot’s reachable
set into rearward and lower regions. The directional asymmetry is quantified in Fig. 2B(ii). Relative
to the native arms, the neck-mounted SRL increases maximum forward reach from 0.66 m to 0.87
m and maximum upward reach from 1.16 m to 1.53 m in the body frame, corresponding to 2.53 m
in the global standing pose. The back-mounted SRL increases maximum rearward reach from 0.77
m to 1.05 m and maximum downward reach from 0.30 m to 0.68 m.
Sampling-based kinematic analysis further quantifies how the added limb alters the structure of
the reachable workspace. As shown in Fig. 2B(iii), the neck-mounted and back-mounted configu-
rations increase total reachable volume from 1.95 m3 to 2.94 m3 (+50.3%) and 2.78 m3 (+42.3%),
respectively. The workspace reachable by at least two arms expands from 0.52 m3 to 1.43 m3
(neck) and 1.26 m3 (back). Furthermore, Both embodiments introduce a tri-arm co-reach region
of 0.24 m3. These results show that SRL augmentation expands not only total reach but also the
shared workspace available to multiple arms, providing a geometric basis for coordinated multi-arm
interaction.
We characterize local kinematic conditioning using translational manipulability isotropy, de-
fined in Materials and Methods. Fig. 2C shows the best available sampled isotropy score across
workspace slices expressed in the nominal standing frame. The neck-mounted SRL expands re-
gions of favorable kinematic conditioning in the frontal and overhead workspace, whereas the
back-mounted SRL extends these regions rearward and downward. In both embodiments, the added
limb increases the spatial extent over which well-conditioned translational motion is available, with
the direction of augmentation determined by the mounting configuration.
We finally examine whether these geometric gains are achieved without compromising static
support. Fig. 2D shows CoM distributions for the neck-mounted and back-mounted embodiments
across all reachable SRL configurations and payload from 0 to 4 kg. The distributions are overlaid
with support polygons corresponding to a set of static standing configurations in which the feet were

Support and Stability Augmentation
Beyond extending manipulation reach, the supernumerary arm can establish auxiliary environmen-
tal contacts that contribute to whole-body support while the native arms remain occupied. We
examine this role through disturbance-rejection experiments using a Computer-Aided Rehabilita-
tion Environment (CAREN) system and a support-assisted forward-leaning task during payload
handling (Movie S2).
To assess the contribution of the additional SRL contact to disturbance tolerance, we conducted
CAREN perturbation experiments with and without SRL support (Figs. 3A and B). In the supported
condition, the back-mounted SRL braces against the external frame, creating an additional contact
that enlarges the whole-body support region beyond the feet alone. With SRL support, the robot
tolerated repeated forward and backward perturbations at the maximum clip amplitude permitted
by the experimental safety protocol (10 cm), as shown in Fig. 3D. Without SRL support, the robot
tolerated smaller perturbations but lost balance at 7.5 cm when the CoM moved beyond the feet-only
support polygon (Fig. 3E). These results show that the auxiliary SRL contact increases disturbance
tolerance by providing an additional whole-body support mode.
Beyond disturbance rejection, we investigate how SRL-assisted environmental bracing enables
whole-body postures beyond feet-only support during manipulation (Fig. 3C). The native arms carry
a payload while the SRL braces against a wall to support the leaning posture. The global-frame
trajectory in Fig. 3F(ii) shows the CoM reaches a maximum forward displacement of 0.28 m and
extends 0.16 m beyond the feet-only support boundary. Figure 3F(i) shows the projected contact
hull with conditional static-equilibrium regions computed assuming unilateral frictional contact
(𝜇= 1 for both wall and floor). Evaluating wall-normal-force caps of 5%, 10%, and 20% of the
total body weight 𝑊, we observe that the admissible CoM region expands alongside the allowable
force limit. This demonstrates that environmental bracing broadens static support through contact
force capability rather than contact geometry alone.

Triadic Coordination
Beyond extending reach, the SRL changes how manipulation and support roles can be distributed
across the humanoid body. The additional limb provides access to spatial regions beyond the
native-arm workspace while allowing the native arms to maintain existing contacts or continue
separate actions. We examine these capabilities through two interaction regimes: reach-augmented
manipulation, where the SRL operates in workspace regions that are difficult or inaccessible
for the native arms, and coordinated augmentation, where all three arms contribute to a shared
manipulation objective.
Reach-Augmented Manipulation
In reach-augmentedscenarios,theSRL operatesinregionsoutsideornearthenative-armworkspace,
while the native arms maintain other manipulation or support roles (Movie S3). Across the four tasks,
Figs. 4E–H(i) compare the SRL end-effector trajectories with the sampled native-arm workspace
in a common body frame. Positive signed distance indicates that the SRL reference lies outside
the sampled native-arm reachable set, whereas negative values indicate overlap. The shaded bands
indicate sensitivity to workspace discretization. Overall, substantial portions of the SRL references
extend beyond the native-arm workspace, showing how the added limb provides access to spatial
regions not readily available to the native arms.
The neck-mounted SRL retrieves an object from the top layer of a tall shelf and hands it to
a human seated across the table (Fig. 4A). The corresponding vertical trajectory in Fig. 4E(ii)
reaches a maximum world-frame height of 2.34 m and descends to 1.31 m during the handoff.
The accompanying base-height trace shows how whole-body motion contributes to the extended
vertical interaction range.
We next evaluate object transfer between spatially separated shelves under constrained body
motion (Fig. 4B). The neck-mounted SRL moves an object from the upper front shelf to the rear
workspace while the robot maintains an approximately fixed heading. Fig. 4F(ii) shows the SRL
end-effector path in a frame fixed at the initial base pose, together with schematic shelf boundaries
separated by 0.50 m. This narrow arrangement restricts body reorientation and limits the clearance
available for large native-arm movements, such as raising or sweeping the arms between the shelves.
The task demonstrates how the added limb enables object transfer across spatially separated regions
when both body motion and native-arm access are constrained by the environment.

Coordinated Augmentation
In coordinated augmentation scenarios, the SRL works together with the native arms toward a shared
manipulation objective (Movie S4). Rather than acting independently in an extended workspace,
the SRL participates through relay, object-relative manipulation, or added contact support. These
tasks show how tri-arm coordination enable continuous manipulation across multiple workspace
regimes and improves stability when an additional contact is required.
In the shelf-transfer task (Fig. 5A), the native arms retrieve a box from a lower shelf and lift it
upward, but the final placement lies beyond their practical range for stable insertion. Once the box
reaches the upper transfer region, the SRL engages and guides the final placement onto the high
shelf. The vertical motion traces in Fig. 5D(i) show the coordinated vertical motions of the SRL and
native left hand in the body frame, while Fig. 5D(ii) captures the full motion of the object from the
initial pose to the final placement at 1.8 m height and 0.8 m forward relative to the torso. This task
illustrates a workspace relay in which the object moves continuously from native-arm acquisition
to SRL-assisted placement without intermediate drops or body repositioning.
A different coordination pattern appears in the loading task (Fig. 5B). The native arms first
grasp and lift a container from a desk, establishing a stable support frame in front of the torso. The
SRL then reaches to a top shelf, retrieves a second object, and places it into the held container.
The corresponding body-frame traces in Fig. 5E show the coordinated vertical motions of the SRL

Dynamic and Collaborative Whole-Body Augmentation
The supernumerary limb extends tri-arm coordination from stationary manipulation to tasks per-
formed during whole-body motion. In these settings, the added limb allows an ongoing manipulation
or support role to be maintained while the humanoid walks, transports objects, or interacts with
a human partner. We evaluate three tasks that illustrate how the SRL enables different types of
interaction to proceed concurrently with locomotion (Movie S5).
During collaborative table transport, the humanoid lifts and carries a table with a human partner
while the SRL stabilizes an object on the tabletop (Fig. 6A). The native arms remain engaged with
the table throughout the task, while the added limb maintains contact with the yoga ball to prevent
rolling during lifting and forward walking. This behavior shows how the third arm can assume a
secondary manipulation role without interrupting collaborative transport.
Box transport through a door illustrates how object carrying and environmental manipulation
can proceed concurrently (Fig. 6B). The robot first squats to pick up a box and then walks forward.
During transport, the neck-mounted SRL extends ahead to push a weighted door panel, allowing
the robot to walk through the door without stopping. The task therefore distributes object transport
and environmental manipulation across different limbs.
In a rearward door-opening task, the back-mounted SRL reaches behind the torso to engage
the door handle and initiate opening while the native arms continue to hold the box (Fig. 6C).
The added limb accesses the door before the body reorients toward the doorway, after which the
humanoid proceeds through the opened passage. This configuration enables interaction from a
rearward approach direction while preserving the primary transport task.
Across these scenarios, the SRL allows manipulation roles to remain active during locomotion
and collaborative interaction. Rather than requiring the humanoid to complete each interaction
sequentially, the additional limb enables tasks to be distributed across the body so that transport,

Vision-Guided Autonomous Control
To demonstrate closed-loop task execution beyond predefined reference sequences, we integrate a
vision-based diffusion policy (DP) as a high-level perceptual planner. The task-specific RL con-
trollers provide feedback control for whole-body execution, but their reference targets are otherwise
prescribed in advance and are not updated from visual feedback. The diffusion planner instead pre-
dicts short-horizon reference targets from updated visual and proprioceptive observations, providing
a perception-driven interface to the low-level controllers.
We evaluate this extension on three tasks spanning reach-augmented manipulation, coordi-
nated augmentation, and dynamic whole-body interaction: top-shelf retrieval and human delivery,
stacked-box stabilization, and object transport during door interaction. Task-specific diffusion poli-
cies are trained from hardware demonstrations collected with the corresponding RL controllers.
As demonstrated in Movie S6, the resulting system executes all three tasks autonomously using
visual feedback to update task-level references throughout execution. These experiments show that
the tri-arm whole-body controllers can be coupled with a perceptual planning layer for closed-loop
autonomous operation, while the underlying whole-body execution remains handled by the same
RL controllers used in the preceding experiments.
Discussion
Our results show that equipping a humanoid with a supernumerary arm changes not only how far
the robot can reach, but also the structure of its feasible whole-body interactions. The additional
limb creates new workspaces of multi-arm co-reach and introduces environmental contacts that
can provide extra manipulation or support capabilities. These changes allow interaction roles
to be redistributed across the body, enabling the robot to maintain ongoing tasks while accessing
otherwise difficult workspace regions and to sustain whole-body postures beyond feet-only support.
In this sense, morphological augmentation changes both where the humanoid can interact and how
different parts of the body can contribute to a task.
The two mounting configurations illustrate complementary ways in which the same SRL module
can reshape this interaction space. The neck-mounted embodiment emphasizes frontal and overhead
access, whereas the back-mounted embodiment extends rearward and downward interaction. These
differences are reflected in the workspace, manipulability, and coordinated behaviors observed
across the experiments. In our system, these morphological capabilities are realized through unified
whole-body coordination rather than by treating the added limb as an independent manipulator. The
16


TO-RL framework provides physically consistent motion guidance together with feedback control,
allowing the SRL and native limbs to play different roles as task demands change. The resulting
behaviors show that tri-arm coordination introduces new ways of distributing manipulation and
support across the body rather than simply adding another redundant end-effector.
The vision-guided experiments provide an initial demonstration that these capabilities can
also be connected to perception for closed-loop autonomous execution. Task-specific diffusion
policies were evaluated on selected behaviors rather than across the full task suite, and broader
generalization to new environments and objects, as well as longer-horizon interactions, remains to
be studied. The present hardware study also considers two mounting configurations of a single SRL
design rather than a systematic optimization over possible morphologies. In addition, the grasp-
support analysis relies on simplified frictional contact assumptions. Future work could jointly
optimize limb geometry and attachment for different task families. Extending this approach to
multiple SRLs would require selecting their optimal number and arrangement to broaden functional
capability while respecting the humanoid’s payload limits and avoiding self-collision. Richer contact
estimation could further support whole-body interaction, alongside advances in perception-driven
control for less structured environments and longer-horizon human–robot collaboration.
Taken together, these findings support a broader view of humanoid robots as extensible mor-
phologies rather than fixed replicas of the human body. A supernumerary limb need not serve only
as additional reach or payload capacity; it can change how physical interaction roles are distributed
across the robot and its environment. This perspective highlights a design and control space for
humanoids in which morphological augmentation and whole-body coordination jointly expand
physical interaction capabilities.
Materials and Methods
Humanoid and SRL Hardware
Humanoid platform.
Experiments were conducted using a Digit humanoid robot from Agility
Robotics. This robot has a 30-DoF platform with 20 actuated joints, including two 4-DoF arms
and two 6-DoF leg modules. The ankle and shin joints incorporate passive compliance using
leaf-spring four-bar linkages. Toe pitch and roll are actuated indirectly via pushrod-coupled dual
motors. The platform weighs approximately 48 kg, and is designed for dynamic locomotion and
mobile manipulation. Custom communication and control interfaces were implemented to enable
synchronized multi-arm coordination and real-time integration of SRL-mounted perception.
SRL mechanical design and actuation.
To enable morphological augmentation, we designed a
modular 4-DoF supernumerary robotic limb (SRL) that can be mounted either at the neck or at the
17


back of the humanoid. The SRL consists of a 3-DoF shoulder assembly (roll, pitch, yaw), a remotely
actuated elbow joint driven by a 3:1 belt transmission, and a wrist interface for interchangeable
end-effectors. The arm is designed for low distal inertia through a gimbal-style shoulder structure
and by relocating the elbow drive to the joint base using remote actuation. The SRL has a total
length of 83.0 cm and a total mass of 4.3 kg, including drivers, structural linkage, and wiring.
We used a compact rack-and-pinion parallel gripper for loco-manipulation tasks. The end-effector
supports up to 2 kg continuous payload and 4 kg short-duration peak payload.
The primary elbow link (30.8 cm) was machined from Al-6061 to withstand transmission
loads, whereas the wrist assembly used a high-strength Al7075 shaft coupled to the drive pulley
through a keyed interface. Structural adapters, including the neck and back mounting brackets,
were fabricated in PLA. Shoulder roll, yaw, and elbow joints were actuated using RobStride
RS-02 motors, while the shoulder pitch used a RS-03 motor. Actuator selection was guided by
inverse dynamics simulation to ensure torque-speed feasibility over representative SRL trajectories.
Additional implementation details, including battery and communication architecture, are provided
in Supplementary Materials.

Workspace and Contact Analysis
Workspace and manipulability
The workspace illustrations and volume summary in Fig. 2B use the original analysis, which
specifies 5, 000, 000 random joint configurations per arm and a 20 mm voxel grid. Arms were
sampled within joint limits with the torso and lower body in a nominal standing pose; configurations
with detected self-collisions were rejected, and environmental obstacles were excluded. Occupied
voxels were classified by single-arm reachability: “exactly-one”, “exactly-two”, and “exactly-three”
arm volumes sum to the total workspace union, while the “at-least-two” volume combines the latter
two. These overlaps classifications describe reachability by individual arms and do not guarantee
collision-free simultaneous multi-arm configurations. Additional views are provided in Fig. S3.
The heatmaps use the velocity-scaled translational manipulability isotropy (defined in Materials
and Methods), evaluated using the full base-to-end-effector Jacobian at the nominal base pose. Each
voxel records the maximum score sampled across all accessible arms. Zero-padding and Gaussian
smoothing were applied solely for visual presentation and were excluded from the underlying
distance-analysis workspace.
Workspace distance and contact metrics
For Fig. 4E-H, signed distance is measured in the instantaneous body frame from the SRL reference
to the native-arm voxel-union boundary (positive outside, negative inside). The workspace union is
discretized into 20 mm voxels using 222 Sobol samples per arm, with shaded bands spanning 15, 20,
and 25 mm voxel estimates to illustrate resolution sensitivity. Wall-support boundaries satisfy static
force and moment equilibrium under fixed, unilateral contacts with a friction coefficient of 𝜇= 1
for both wall and floor, a 16-ray friction cone linearization, and maximum total wall-normal force
capped at 5/10/20% of the modeled weight (shared between the two native hands). For Fig. 5F, the
Ferrari– Canny-type score 𝜖(45) is the radius of the largest origin-centered ball contained within
the convex hull of unit-normalized contact wrenches; 𝜖= 0 if the origin lies outside or on the
hull boundary. Torque components were scaled by the modeled stack’s bounding-box diagonal,
ℓ= 1.75 m, before unit-wrench normalization. Four ray directions approximate the friction cone
at each contact of the idealized rigid stack. Note that this normalized geometric metric evaluates
wrench-space coverage rather than physical force capacity.

Vision-guided autonomous control implementation
Data collection and synchronization
Demonstrations were collected on hardware using trained task-specific RL student controllers to
execute reference motions in predefined scenes. RGB-D streams from the torso-mounted and SRL
end-effector cameras were recorded at 15 Hz, while proprioceptive states and reference commands
were recorded at 200 Hz. Each control sample was paired with the latest available image from
each camera. The synchronized trajectories were subsampled to 50 Hz for diffusion-policy training,
retaining only successful runs.
Observation and action specification
The diffusion policy uses a single observation and predicts an action sequence of length 𝑙𝑝= 64,
executing the first 𝑙𝑎= 32 actions before the next inference. The five controlled end-effectors are
the two native hands, two feet, and the SRL end-effector, with their reference positions expressed
in the robot body frame. The policy-mode variable enables transitions between task-specific RL
controllers when required by the task. Table S10 summarizes the implementation details.
Diffusion-policy implementation and online inference

timesteps. Training follows the conditional DDPM objective outlined in the main text, with the
