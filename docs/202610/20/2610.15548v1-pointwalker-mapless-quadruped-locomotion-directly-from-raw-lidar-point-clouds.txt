PointWalker: Mapless Quadruped Locomotion
Directly From Raw LiDAR Point Clouds
Sicen Li1,2, Zhen Chu1,2, Chao Li2, Jun Zheng4, Qiuguo Zhu1, and Jun Wu1,3,*
Abstract—Quadruped robots deployed for inspection and field
missions must cross heterogeneous terrain, from stairs, slopes,
and gaps to high platforms, stepping stones, and balance beams,
where safe support is sparse or see-through. Existing methods
interpose a terrain map or a depth image between sensing and
control. Maps are costly to maintain, corrupted by odometry
drift and outliers, and misled by see-through gratings, while
depth cameras degrade with lighting and surface material. In
this paper, we present PointWalker, a mapless framework whose
single reinforcement learning policy directly consumes raw point
clouds from four light detection and ranging (LiDAR) sensors.
Voxelized points are encoded by linear self-attention and fused
with proprioception through cross-attention, so terrain geometry
reaches the policy without maps or foothold estimation. One
proximal policy optimization (PPO) run, with terrain-aware
rewards and dual-channel simulation of see-through gratings,
yields one policy for all seven terrain classes with a 90.1%
mean success rate in simulation. Deployed zero-shot on a Deep
Robotics X30 at 50 Hz onboard, it crosses 0.8-m gaps, climbs 0.8-
m platforms and 45◦steel-grating stairs, carries a 50-kg payload,
and completes a 2.5-km mountain route.
Index Terms—Legged locomotion, legged robots, LiDAR, point
clouds, reinforcement learning.
I. INTRODUCTION
Quadruped robots are entering industrial inspection, payload
delivery, and field exploration, where one platform must
cross heterogeneous terrain: stairs, steep slopes, gaps, high
platforms, stepping stones, and balance beams (Fig. 1). Safe
traversal depends on perceiving support geometry before con-
tact (see-through grating treads, the far edge of a gap, the next
stone in a sparse field), onboard in real time.
Reinforcement learning (RL) dominates quadrupedal loco-
motion: massively parallel simulation [1] yields robust pro-
prioceptive policies [2], [3], and teacher–student distillation
injects terrain awareness from privileged height or environ-
ment observations [4], [5]. The resulting policies are reactive
to what the feet touch: on gaps, high platforms, and sparse
footholds (whose decisive geometry lies ahead of and beneath
the body), blind performance collapses.
Perceptive methods therefore insert a terrain representa-
tion between sensing and control. Elevation maps [6], [7]

II. RELATED WORK
A. Learning-Based Legged Locomotion
Deep RL is the workhorse of quadrupedal control: Hwangbo
et al. [2] learned actuator models for real ANYmals, mas-
sively parallel simulation [1] cut training to minutes, and
DreamWaQ [3] inferred terrain implicitly from proprioception
alone. Teacher–student pipelines instead grant the teacher
privileged observations and distill a proprioceptive student [4],
[5]; Miki et al. [9] refined the recipe with a belief state over
elevation maps whose trust is gated against sensor failure,
and Ji et al. [18] trained the state estimator concurrently with
the policy. For terrain whose support must be seen before it
is stepped on, distillation cannot add information the student
never senses; PointWalker trains a directly perceiving policy
in one stage, with privilege confined to the critic.
B. Perception Interfaces for Agile Locomotion
Elevation maps [6], [7] fuse depth and point-cloud mea-
surements with odometry into a robot-centric 2.5-D surface,
on which foothold optimization [8] and attention-based map
encoders [19] build. Depth images and maps reconstructed
from multi-sensor point clouds instead feed parkour policies
end-to-end [10]–[12]. Single-stage frameworks estimate terrain
implicitly [13], reconstruct local height maps [20], or predict
foothold priors [21] from vision, with depth-only perception
extending to humanoids [22]. Specialized lines target hollow
see-through stairs [23], platform jumps of varying distance and
height [24], and jumping over gaps and high platforms [25].
Across these systems, the interface rather than the task bounds
performance, and forward depth limits perception to a cone
that excludes the area beneath the body.
C. LiDAR-Based Locomotion
In terrain-aware locomotion, LiDAR reaches the policy
in raw form only for collision avoidance among dynamic
obstacles [16]. BeamDojo [14] trains humanoid locomotion
on sparse footholds but feeds the policy an elevation map
reconstructed from LiDAR scans; JEPLO [15] learns mapping-
free terrain representations with a joint-embedding predictive
world model, projecting accumulated scans onto a spherical
depth image and acting on the learned latents. PointWalker re-
moves the last intermediate form: one policy attends directly to
raw voxelized LiDAR points cross-fused with proprioception
(gratings and sparse footholds stay points) and spans stairs to
balance beams in one training run.
III. METHOD
Fig. 2 illustrates PointWalker: one PPO run in massively

A. Point Cloud Perception from Onboard LiDAR
LiDAR configuration. Four Livox Mid-360 LiDARs are
mounted at the corners of the body, two at the front and two
at the rear, with interleaved pitch, for a combined 360◦hor-
izontal field of view and −7◦–52◦vertical range. Simulation
reproduces this geometry by casting multiple rays that also
hit the perception-only structural layer (Section III-D) and the
thigh and shank meshes to model leg self-occlusion. The rays
are randomly permuted into five groups emitted in rotation
(an effective 10-Hz scan rate), mimicking the Mid-360’s non-
repetitive scanning. At 50 Hz, one group is read per step and
the latest five retained: 5 × 70 = 350 points spanning 0.1 s.
Preprocessing and degradation. Each step, the hits are
transformed to the base frame, and points within 0.15 m of a
sensor origin or inside the body bounding box are removed.
Gaussian noise (σ = 0.02 m) is added; the rest is voxelized at
0.1-m resolution over the perception region x ∈[−1.2, 1.2],
y ∈[−0.6, 0.6], z ∈[−1.0, 0.5] m (the tops of the tallest
curriculum platforms rise above this bound; their front faces
remain in view) with per-voxel averaging; and 70 points per
group are sampled from occupied voxels into the fixed-size
tensor Pt ∈R5×70×3. Training also injects sensor degra-
dation (2% invalidated points, 2% random outliers, 40–60-
ms latency), so the policy trains against an already-degraded
sensing chain. Invalid points are masked, not removed, in the
attention below. Fixed-size sampling keeps onboard compute
independent of scene complexity.
Linear-attention encoding. Each slot, base-frame coordi-
nates plus a temporal scan-group encoding (4-D input), is
linearly embedded to 32 dimensions and contextualized by two
levels of linear self-attention [27], yielding 32-D point tokens

Both are estimated online from the privileged height samples
(gap width as the width of support-free columns, platform
height as the difference between the means of the highest and
lowest point sets). The incentive scales with difficulty.
Termination. Episodes terminate on persisting undesirable
body contact (≈1 s), tipping over, sinking below the support
surface, falling into a gap, leaving the terrain, or timeout (20 s);
failures and timeouts enter the advantage estimation differently.

D. Dual-Channel Simulation and One-Stage Training
Physics and perception channels. The physics channel
holds the collidable height field and meshes; privileged height
sampling and the foot rays of Section III-C cast only against
it. The perception channel is a structural layer that enters
LiDAR casting but never in collision: steel-grating treads and
risers (5-mm flat bars at 40-mm pitch), stair handrails, random
posts, hanging bars, and platform edge beams and posts. The
privileged side sees solid support, while the policy’s point
cloud sees the sparse grating, handrails, and clutter of a real
industrial stair, flat bars and spacing preserved as points. The
policy learns to land on solid support while perceiving the
see-through structure.
Terrain curriculum. The seven terrains (Table II) form
a curriculum of 10 × 10-m tiles, robots starting on random
difficulty rows; discrete obstacles are used in simulation only.
Following legged_gym [1], traveling beyond half the tile
length promotes a robot to a harder row, falling short of half
the commanded distance demotes it. Balance beams are narrow
beams arranged in a star.
Domain randomization. To bridge the reality gap, train-
ing randomizes contact, mass, actuation, and sensing [32];
sensing-side degradation follows Section III-A. Half of the
environments apply left–right mirror augmentation (joint re-
ordering with matching reflections of observations, commands,
actions, and points). Training runs in Isaac Lab with massive
parallelism; the full configuration is in Table III.
Deployment. Only the policy network is exported to ONNX
(Fig. 2(c)) and runs deterministically (action mean) at 50 Hz
on the X30’s onboard RK3588. The cross-attention weights
are read out with the action and projected onto the points as
per-point importance for interpreting foothold decisions.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We report the experimental setup (Section IV-A), simulation
results (Section IV-B), and hardware results (Section IV-C);

A. Experimental Setup
Platform and deployment. The robot is the Deep Robotics
X30 quadruped (55 kg, 0.9-m body, 12 actuated joints); sens-
ing and deployment follow Sections III-A and III-D. Table IV

B. Simulation Results
Overall traversal and baselines. PointWalker averages
90.1% across the seven terrains and never drops below 83.8%,
with one policy and no per-terrain switching (Table V).
Height-map encoding trails on stairs and stepping stones
(86.7% and 75.8% versus 93.2% and 83.8%): on the see-
through grating (Fig. 3(b)), the 2.5-D aggregation yields spuri-
ous heights that are neither safe support nor true geometry, and
rasterization erases the geometry of sparse footholds. Slopes
and platforms score slightly higher, yet the mean trails by 2.7
points. Blind keeps 82.6% and 84.1% on stairs and slopes,
terrain the feet can probe, but collapses below 30% on gaps,
platforms, stones, and beams, where support must be seen
before contact. Depth-image encoding averages 84.8%: gaps
and stones fall well short (71.5% and 68.4% versus 86.1% and
83.8%) and platforms and beams slightly short, as the forward
camera cone, an interface property rather than an encoding
deficit, misses the area beneath and beside the body and forces
reliance on network memory. On stairs, slopes, and discrete
obstacles the two trade places (94.6%, 94.9%, 91.7%). Image
space keeps the grating’s 2-D bars that the height map loses
on stairs, at 2.2× the latency (Table IV).

and largest on stepping stones; w/o Foot-edge penalty reaches

without fine-tuning. The operator issues only a constant for-
ward command (≈1.0 m/s) with no per-terrain switching or
adjustment. Indoor tests run under overhead fall protection.

Payload. With rigid backpack loads, the X30 mounts the

Occlusion robustness. We block one front and one rear

still passes the combined scene of stairs, platform, and slope

so the challenge is sensing degradation, met by the LiDARs’
field-of-view redundancy and the injected degradation.
Long-range and natural terrain. We also drive the policy,
carrying 25 kg (45%), through a 2.5-km mountain route
of 200 m elevation gain in about 46 min at 0.9 m/s. The
route crosses rock slopes, leaf litter, and stone steps, and
the policy runs unadjusted throughout. Gravel, sand, and
cobblestone (Fig. 15) lie outside the seven training classes
and differ in contact behavior (loose, sinking, rounded). The
same policy walks stably on all three without targeted training,
so its foothold decisions transfer beyond the training domain.

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In this paper, we presented PointWalker, a mapless frame-
work in which one PPO run trains a single policy that attends
directly to raw LiDAR points across seven terrain classes. In
simulation, intermediate representations degraded on sparse
and see-through support: height maps aggregated the grating
into spurious heights, and depth images never saw beneath or
beside the body. The raw points preserved that geometry. Only
a small fraction of them are decisive footholds, so replacing
cross-attention with mean pooling cost 10 points of success
rate; linear attention held the perception pathway at 2.01 ms, a
tenth of the control period. On hardware, each ablated training-
only design was essential: without dual-channel simulation,
the policy stalled at the stair base, and without the foot-
edge penalty, it slipped off the stones. Without the traversal
incentives, it reached the gap short of momentum and the
platform without a leap. Zero-shot on the X30, the same
policy crossed 0.8-m gaps, mounted 0.8-m platforms, walked
on with two of four LiDARs blocked, and completed a 2.5-km
mountain route, all without a terrain map.
Perception is local and momentary, so the policy traverses
only what the current points show; porting beyond the X30’s
four-LiDAR layout is untested. Within that scope, it gener-
alized to natural terrain beyond the training classes; global
path planning and moving support remain open. The first
extension embeds PointWalker in a navigation stack with a
global planner. Another is dynamic environments, extending
the interface to moving obstacles and support.
