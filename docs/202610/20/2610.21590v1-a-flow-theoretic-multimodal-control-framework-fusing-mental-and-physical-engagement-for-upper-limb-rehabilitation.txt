# A Flow-Theoretic Multimodal Control Framework Fusing Mental and Physical Engagement for Upper-Limb Rehabilitation 

Jun Leng, Student Member, IEEE , Pengcheng Li, Senior Member, IEEE , Hanze Wang, Student Member, IEEE ,Shuxiang Guo, Fellow, IEEE , and Junlin Jiang, Student Member, IEEE 

Abstract —While Assist-as-Needed (AAN) control aims to max-imize user participation in robot-assisted training, existing meth-ods relying solely on physical metrics cannot distinguish muscular inability from mental disengagement, leading to suboptimal interventions. To address this ambiguity, a Flow-theoretic mul-timodal AAN framework is proposed, fusing EEG-based mental engagement and sEMG-based physical engagement into an adap-tive impedance control strategy. A four-quadrant fuzzy decision engine translates these dual physiological metrics into continuous control policies. This enables the upper-limb exoskeleton to inject feedforward assistance during intent-execution mismatch (e.g., motor output attenuation despite mental focus) and escalate guiding stiffness to penalize mental distraction. The framework was validated with healthy participants against a state-of-practice sEMG-only AAN baseline. Kinematically and mechanically, the proposed framework reduced robotic energy expenditure by 71.9% while maintaining precise trajectory tracking, confirming it elicited greater voluntary human effort. Physiologically, the system increased the time spent in the neuroplasticity zone (Flow State Ratio) from 4.6% to 42.8%. These results validate that decoupling and jointly regulating mental and physical states enhances the selectivity of robotic assistance, mitigating user passivity and sustaining the engagement crucial for motor learning. 

Index Terms —flow theory, multimodal physiological sensing, Assist-as-needed (AAN), upper limb rehabilitation.

These systems offer precise physical guid-ance while reducing the labor burden on clinicians, making them essential for delivering the high-dosage therapy required for motor learning. To maximize therapeutic outcomes, the field has widely adopted the “Assist-as-Needed” (AAN) paradigm. The core principle of AAN is to minimize robotic intervention, pro-viding assistance only when the user cannot complete the movement independently. This approach aims to ensure max-imal active effort [5]. However, accurately inferring the user’s true “need” remains a critical challenge. Traditional AAN strategies rely heavily on physical performance metrics, such as kinematic error or interaction force. For instance, Han et al. [6] proposed an adaptive control method that infers movement intention by decomposing real-time interaction forces. The system then adjusts the robot’s impedance parameters to pro-mote active participation. More recently, researchers have inte-grated surface Electromyography (sEMG) to incorporate phys-iological effort into the control loop. Xu et al. [7], for example, developed a framework that adapts training trajectories and robotic assistance based on sEMG-derived muscle activation. This system encourages patient participation by modulating feedback gains. However, while such approaches successfully measure mechanical or muscular execution, they still fail to capture user’s mental focus. Although high-amplitude sEMG quantifies physical exertion, it cannot differentiate genuine task engagement from pathological co-contraction or abnormal compensatory synergies. Execution-level metrics alone fall short in revealing the user’s mental intent. Without monitoring mental focus, purely physical assistance strategies often fail to maintain an optimal training challenge, eventually leading to user passivity. To resolve this ambiguity, integrating electroencephalogra-phy (EEG) into the control loop is essential for providing a direct measure of task-related attentional focus [8]. While EEG is widely applied in robotic rehabilitation, current paradigms mostly use it within Motor Imagery-based Brain-Computer Interfaces (MI-BCI) to decode discrete movement intentions [9], [10]. This paper introduces an adaptive control framework grounded in Flow Theory to map these multidimensional phys-iological signals into actionable robotic interventions. Classic Flow Theory defines an optimal mental state as a balance between external task challenge and intrinsic user skill [11], [12]. The modern four-quadrant extension categorizes user experience into Flow, Anxiety, Boredom, and Apathy based on these misalignments [13]. In robot-assisted training, keeping users within this flow state mitigates both task-induced anxiety arXiv:2610.21590v1 [cs.RO] 15 Oct 2026 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON HUMAN-MACHINE SYSTEMS (UNDER REVIEW) 2

and under-stimulated boredom [14], [15]. This equilibrium sustains intrinsic motivation and facilitates motor learning [16]. Most existing flow-aware systems rely on predefined task difficulties or intermittent self-reporting, limiting their real-time adaptability. Directly quantifying the abstract Challenge and Skill axes in real-time is also highly impractical during dynamic robot-assisted training, as the user’s motor capabili-ties fluctuate continuously due to fatigue and varying physical demands. To address this gap, the proposed framework by-passes the direct measurement of classic Flow coordinates. By estimating the user’s Flow state, the system dynamically adjusts guiding stiffness and feedforward assistance to mitigate physical passivity, address the mismatch between mental intent and physical execution, and optimize the AAN interaction. B. Flow-Grounded Theoretical Mapping 

Because quantifying absolute “Challenge” and “Skill” in real-time remains impractical, the decision-making en-gine translates the four-quadrant Flow model into a two-dimensional physiological diagnostic space (Fig. 2(a)). Neuroergonomic and cognitive workload literature demon-strates that shifts in the Challenge-Skill balance elicit dis-tinct physiological manifestations across central and peripheral pathways. For example, concurrent high cognitive demand dur-ing physical exertion induces prefrontal neuronal interference, which increases neuromotor noise and impairs joint steadiness [20]. This provides a quantifiable basis for task overload (Challenge ≫ Skill). Conversely, resource theory suggests highly overlearned, automated motor executions demand min-imal attentional resources; prolonged exposure to such task underload (Challenge ≪ Skill) diminishes cognitive vigilance while preserving physical task-tracking output [20]. When both task-directed cognitive drive and volitional motor execu-tion are deficient, the neuromuscular system exhibits concur-rent hypo-arousal and dropping active torque, characterizing complete task disengagement [21]. Finally, neurophysiological evidence shows active volitional intention coupled with effec-tive peripheral execution induces the early cortical activation and somatosensory synchronization vital for Hebbian-based neural plasticity [22]. Maintaining the task-skill equilibrium (Challenge ≈ Skill) thus ensures the synchronous central and peripheral activation necessary for optimal motor learning. A. Experimental Participants and Setup 

Ten healthy, right-handed volunteers (age 23 .3 ± 1.8 years; 8 males, 2 females) participated in the study under institu-tional ethics approval (no. 20250066). The CURE upper-limb exoskeleton was utilized for 1-DoF elbow flexion/extension. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), seated participants interacted with a custom visual feedback interface on a monitor. Through this interface (Fig. 4(b)), participants tracked a continuous sinusoidal target (0.2 Hz, range 0◦–90 ◦) by controlling a real-time cursor that reflected their actual elbow joint angle. The interface also delivered synchronized on-screen text and audio cues to guide subjects through specific behavioral phases. To emulate the elevated physical demand and intent-execution mismatch often encountered in robot-assisted training, a 1.2 kg weight was affixed to the user’s wrist. This added inertial load ensures sustained muscular effort and natural fatigue during prolonged tracking tasks. As depicted in Fig. 5(a), an individualized calibration ses-sion was conducted prior to the main trials. The normalization bounds ( [𝑠 min , 𝑠 max ]) for the EEG decoder were established through a 60-second resting baseline, a passive following task (eyes closed, mental relaxation), and an active tracking task (maximal visual focus). Concurrently, physical engagement for the sEMG decoder was normalized against peak values obtained from a 5-second Maximum Voluntary Contraction (MVC) test.

A. Resolving Intent-Execution Ambiguity in Multimodal AAN Control 

While recent advancements in robot-assisted rehabilitation have driven significant progress in personalized therapy, sus-taining optimal neuroplastic engagement remains a formidable challenge. Contextualizing the proposed architecture within the landscape of contemporary AAN paradigms elucidates the necessity of its underlying design and its fundamental functional departures.

Unimodal sEMG- or kinematic-driven AAN controllers [28] adapt support via peripheral effort but remain blind to cogni-tive states. Failing to distinguish motor fatigue from mental distraction, they inadvertently reward rote repetitions or with-hold assistance from exhausted users. Conversely, EEG-driven systems [14] monitor mental engagement but lack peripheral feedback to assess execution capability, unable to discern whether a disengaged user requires feedforward assistance or a haptic penalty. To address this, recent multimodal systems [29], [30] fuse EEG and sEMG, yet typically implement decoupled integrations (e.g., using EEG as a discrete switch while sEMG drives admittance). Such rigid state-machine designs fail to continuously diagnose intent-execution congru-ence, while their discrete thresholds induce mechanical chat-tering during physiological fluctuations, disrupting immersion. The design philosophy of the proposed framework shifts from discrete, symptom-based triggering to a continuous physiological-mechanical closed loop grounded in Flow the-ory. Rather than layering multimodal signals, it evaluates the cognitive-motor balance via a continuously differentiable ( 𝐶 1)fuzzy manifold. By decoding intent-execution mismatches, it delivers chatter-free haptic interventions, specifically assist-ing during motor output attenuation ( Anxiety ) and penalizing slacking ( Boredom ). B. Multimodal Integration and Functional Implications 

Integrating hybrid physiological smoothing with the macro-scopic Dwell-Time Stabilizer eliminates standard BCI “chat-tering,” yielding safe impedance modulation despite biosignal non-stationarity. The Flow-grounded strategy penalizes antag-onist co-activation within the physical metric ( 𝑠 ∗

Unlike sim-ple force estimation that misinterprets excessive co-contraction as high effort, the proposed metric identifies it as low effective engagement, triggering an Assist & Guide strategy to prevent reinforcing maladaptive patterns. “Physiological Clamping” ensures that insufficient challenge triggers Boredom haptic penalties, compelling users to train within target physiological zones. The comparative efficacy results demonstrate the transi-tion from execution-aware to intent-aware training. Common EMG-driven baselines [26], [27] assume muscular output mirrors active participation, rewarding distracted repetitions or wrongly withholding assistance during motor output attenua-tion. The proposed Fusion Mode resolves this ambiguity. By coupling bidirectional haptic adaptability, specifically penaliz-ing distraction and assisting during motor output attenuation, the system drove a 72.4% reduction in robotic energy and a nearly tenfold increase in Flow State Ratio, proving that sus-taining neuroplasticity demands synchronized central neural drive and peripheral motor effort. 

C. Limitations and Future Work 

Validating on healthy young adults limits generalizability to elderly stroke populations, as healthy intent and execu-tion are naturally coupled. The induced mismatches represent controlled approximations. Future longitudinal trials enrolling age-matched stroke survivors will validate parameter general-izability and therapeutic robustness against severe, neurologi-cally derived intent-execution mismatches. While the Apathy quadrant detects complete disengagement, sustained physical exertion may induce muscle fatigue prior to total mental drop-off. Future deployments will integrate an auxiliary safety layer analyzing real-time sEMG spectral shifts (e.g., median frequency decline) to monitor fatigue directly, preemptively modulating robotic impedance or ini-tiating mandatory rest to ensure patient safety. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a multimodal AAN framework for upper-limb rehabilitation that integrates Flow Theory with adaptive variable impedance control. By fusing real-time EEG and sEMG signals to simultaneously quantify mental and physical engagement, a four-quadrant fuzzy decision engine was developed to mitigate intent-execution mismatch and passive user disengagement. Compared to widely adopted unimodal sEMG-driven baselines, the proposed framework achieved tighter trajectory tracking while reducing robotic energy expenditure by 71.9%, confirming the elicitation of greater voluntary human effort. Physiologically, it increased the time spent in the optimal neuroplasticity zone (Flow State Ratio) nearly tenfold (from 4.6% to 42.8%). Continuous dynamic testing verified the system’s stability, demonstrating its ability to safely inject feedforward assistance during motor output attenuation and escalate guiding stiffness during mental distraction.
