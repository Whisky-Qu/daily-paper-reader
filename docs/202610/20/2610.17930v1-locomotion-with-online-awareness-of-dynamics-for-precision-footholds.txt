# LOAD: Locomotion with Online Awareness of Dynamics for Precision Footholds 

Kunzhao Ren 1, Zhishuo Zhu 2, and Xiaobin Xiong 1,†

Abstract — Unknown payloads alter robot dynamics and make precise contact execution difficult when foothold margins are small. We present LOAD, a perceptive locomotion frame-work that combines explicit inertial estimates with implicit dy-namics features for narrow-beam traversal. Separate recurrent modules infer torso inertial changes and task-relevant history from proprioception, while a height-map teacher is distilled into a depth-based student. We extensively evaluate LOAD and its variants in simulation and on hardware across beam widths and payload configurations. The results show that implicit dynamics features accelerate training, while explicit inertial estimation further improves traversal success; combining both enables LOAD to approach the performance of a privileged policy supplied with ground-truth inertial parameters. Hardware experiments demonstrate traversal with unilateral payloads of 6 kg on a 10 cm beam and 9 kg on a 15 cm beam, and an evenly distributed 11 kg payload on a 15 cm beam. These results support combining physical estimates and history features for precision locomotion under unknown loads. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Legged robots have demonstrated increasingly agile loco-motion over rough and unstructured environments. Learning-based controllers have enabled quadrupeds to traverse chal-lenging terrain with dynamic and robust motions [1], [2], [3]. The integration of exteroceptive sensing has further im-proved locomotion over stairs, gaps, obstacles, and parkour-like environments [4], [5], [6]. Recent studies have also moved toward sparse and risky footholds through terrain-aware learning, terrain reconstruction, and foothold-aware perception [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Complementary work in-corporates explicit constraints into locomotion learning [12]. These developments highlight the importance of determining where and how each contact should be established. Perceptive locomotion methods emphasize learning terrain representations to guide contact placement. Robustness to variations in robot dynamics is commonly promoted through domain randomization [13], [14], while Rapid Motor Adapta-tion (RMA) and related history-based estimation methods use proprioceptive history to adapt to changing conditions [15], [16], [17]. Building on these advances, we investigate how implicit dynamics features and explicit inertial estimates jointly support precise contact execution on narrow beams under unknown payloads. Existing narrow-beam studies report real-world traversal of an 18 cm-wide single-plank bridge with Go2 [18] and 20 cm-wide beams separated by gaps with ANYmal-D [7]. More recently, START demonstrated traversal of 20 cm-wide indoor balance beams and 15 cm-wide outdoor flowerbed edges with Lite3 using onboard depth sensing and terrain reconstruction [10]. Complementary progress has been made in payload-adaptive locomotion. Rapid Em-bodiment Adaptation infers physical parameters from short interaction histories and conditions a locomotion policy on these estimates, enabling Go2 to adapt to a 5 kg payload [20]. FLAP combines physics-informed dynamics prediction with adaptive control to compensate for disturbances from shifting payloads, demonstrating Go1 locomotion with 5 L of water across varied outdoor terrains [21]. These results motivate studying payload adaptation under the restricted support geometry of narrow beams. Unknown payloads change mass, center-of-mass location, and rotational inertia, so the same contact forces can produce different body motions. The beam simultaneously limits lateral foot placement and corrective stepping, leaving little room to compensate for these changes. Reliable traversal therefore requires adapting contact behav-ior and body motion to the current dynamics within the available support geometry. To address this challenge, we present LOAD, a loco-motion framework that integrates terrain perception with implicit dynamics features and explicit inertial estimates for narrow-beam traversal under unknown payloads. Two sepa-rate branches process proprioceptive history: one learns task-relevant dynamics features through reinforcement learning, while the other is supervised to estimate payload-induced changes in the torso’s inertial properties relative to a nom-inal model. This pairing provides physically interpretable information about the current dynamics while retaining arXiv:2610.17930v1 [cs.RO] 13 Oct 2026 task-relevant history that does not need to be expressed through inertial parameters. The policy uses both outputs together with terrain features to adapt whole-body motion and foothold execution. A teacher trained with privileged height maps is distilled into a student using onboard depth images for deployment. We evaluate LOAD in simulation and on hardware across beam widths and payload configurations. In the reported training runs, policies with implicit dynamics features reach high traversal success earlier than their counterparts without these features. These features also improve evaluated traver-sal performance, with benefits persisting even when ground-truth inertial parameters are available. Combining these features with explicit inertial estimates brings the LOAD teacher closer to its privileged counterpart, LOAD-GT, over the tested conditions. The benefit of combining explicit and implicit dynamics information persists after visual distillation on narrow beams within the training payload range. Tests with heavier, unseen payloads reveal limitations in both inertial identification and policy generalization, indicating that accurate dynamics information alone does not ensure successful extrapolation. The contributions of this work are threefold:  

> •

We develop LOAD, a perceptive locomotion framework that combines online inertial estimation with implicit dynamics features to adapt whole-body motion and foothold execution to unknown torso payloads.  

> •

We evaluate six policy variants to characterize the separate and combined benefits of explicit inertial in-formation and implicit history. The comparisons show improved narrow-beam traversal with the combined rep-resentation and identify limitations beyond the training payload range.  

> •

A. Perceptive and Precision Locomotion 

Proprioceptive reinforcement learning has enabled robust quadrupedal locomotion over challenging terrain [1], [2]. Perceptive approaches incorporate elevation maps or depth observations to anticipate terrain geometry [3], [4], [5], [6]. For sparse footholds, recent methods use terrain-aware learning [7], [8], terrain reconstruction [9], [10], and foothold priors [11]. Complementary work incorporates explicit con-straints into policy optimization to reduce reward engi-neering and guide locomotion behavior [12]. Model-based approaches address contact feasibility through centroidal planning, trajectory optimization, and multi-contact optimal control [22], [23], [24]. These studies motivate our focus on how payload-induced dynamics changes affect the execution of contacts on narrow support surfaces. 

B. Robust and Adaptive Locomotion 

Dynamics randomization improves robustness by exposing policies to variations in physical parameters during training [13], [14]. RMA infers a latent dynamics context from recent observations and actions to condition a policy trained with privileged information [15]. Concurrent estimation and control [16] and learned terrain and state representations [17] further demonstrate the value of history-based inference. Such representations can retain control-relevant information without explicitly identifying inertial parameters. LOAD preserves this implicit pathway while adding supervised inertial estimates. Our comparisons examine the separate and combined contributions of these two information sources. 

C. Physical Parameter Estimation 

System identification and adaptive control use observed responses to recover uncertain physical parameters [25], [26]. For legged robots, kinematics and contact constraints sup-port state estimation [27], [28], while constrained moving-horizon estimation jointly recovers states and ground-reaction forces [29]. SPI-Active combines sampling-based parameter identification with active exploration for sim-to-real transfer [30]. PRIME jointly estimates motion, contact forces, and inertial parameters through physically consistent optimization [31]. LOAD uses a learned recurrent identifier to supply online inertial estimates directly to the locomotion policy. Its evaluation centers on how these estimates com-plement implicit history in precision foothold control. III. LOAD: L OCOMOTION WITH ONLINE AWARENESS OF

LOAD integrates dynamics perception and terrain per-ception for precision foothold locomotion under unknown payloads. Two separate gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks process proprioceptive history to extract an implicit dynamics feature and estimate payload-induced changes in the torso’s inertial properties relative to a nominal model. A teacher policy uses terrain features encoded from privileged height maps and is distilled into a student policy that uses on-board depth images for deployment. The locomotion policy combines the implicit dynamics feature, inertial estimates, and terrain features with current proprioceptive observations. Fig. 2 provides an overview of LOAD.

Termination : Episodes terminate upon any of four failure conditions: body tilt exceeding 70 ◦ from upright, non-foot terrain contact, self-collision, or a fall from the beam. Terrain curriculum : We use a terrain curriculum with 15 width levels. All environments begin on a 3.20 m-wide sup-port surface. The nominal beam width decreases to 0.50 m, followed by 0.20 m, and subsequently decreases in 0.01 m increments to 0.08 m. Each environment advances one level after a successful crossing without failure and retreats one level after an unsuccessful episode, including a timeout, with the level bounded below by the initial level. After a successful crossing at the narrowest level, a new level is sampled uniformly from all levels. Beam lengths are sampled from 0.75–3.00 m, and beam widths beyond the initial level are perturbed uniformly by up to ±0.005 m. Beam surface heights are sampled uniformly from 0.20–0.60 m. D. Terrain Perception 

Terrain perception encodes local support geometry into the terrain feature supplied to the locomotion policy. 

Height-map encoding : The teacher receives a yaw-aligned height map mt covering 0.80 × 0.40 m, centered 0.12 m forward of the torso origin. Longitudinal and lateral sampling intervals are 0.04 m and 0.02 m, respectively, yielding a 21 ×

21 grid supplied at each policy step. An MLP-based height-map encoder compresses the map into the terrain feature zTt .

Depth-based encoding : For deployment, the student instead uses a CNN to extract spatial features from the current 

424 × 240 depth image dt from a forward-facing camera. Depth is represented as distance along the camera optical axis, clipped to [0 .3, 3.0] m, and normalized to [0 , 1] before encoding. The terrain memory uses a GRU to integrate these features with normalized proprioceptive observations, gait phases, and commands to form the student terrain feature 

zSt . Proprioception provides motion cues, while the recurrent memory retains terrain information after the corresponding region leaves the camera’s field of view. 

E. Policy Distillation 

We train the depth-based student to imitate the height-map teacher using DAgger-style online imitation learning [36], which is common practice in perceptive locomotion [37]. 

Imitation objective : The student minimizes the action im-itation loss Ldist = 1

The action regularization in Sec. III-B discourages but does not strictly prevent teacher action means from exceeding the clipping bounds. Imitating raw teacher actions could therefore cause the student to reproduce excessively large outputs, even though the excess beyond the clipping bounds is discarded before execution. 

Training procedure : During rollout collection, the proba-bility of executing teacher actions decreases linearly from one to zero, while the teacher provides supervision at every visited state. Student training uses the same beam-width curriculum, physical randomization and proprioceptive noise as teacher training. To account for onboard depth sensing, we ad-ditionally apply the camera pose perturbations and depth corruption listed in Table II. Camera position bounds apply to each axis, and angle bounds follow roll, pitch, and yaw. Depth corruption includes spatially and temporally correlated missing regions and simulated stereo occlusions. To retain the dynamics representations learned during teacher training, we freeze the teacher and the inherited dynamics encoder and inertial estimator, while training the student depth encoder, terrain memory, and actor. Jointly up-dating the two dynamics modules with the depth encoder and terrain memory led to distillation failure in our experiments. IV. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

We vary the payload configuration by changing the number and placement of the sandbags. All variants use payload randomization during training, with payload masses ranging from 0 to 8 kg. Unless otherwise stated, tests use left-side payloads, a 3 m-long beam, and a forward velocity command of 0.6 m/s. D. Student Policy Comparison 

To assess deployment performance, we compare the depth-based students obtained by distilling Explicit , Implicit , and LOAD (Fig. 6). We exclude Baseline due to its low teacher-policy success rate, and Explicit -GT and LOAD-GT because they require ground-truth inertial parameters unavailable at deployment. The comparison evaluates whether combining implicit dynamics features with explicit inertial estimates remains beneficial when terrain observations come from depth images. Physical domain randomization and propri-oceptive noise are enabled, while depth randomization is disabled during evaluation. During distillation, we freeze the inherited dynamics encoder in Implicit , the inertial estimator in Explicit , and both modules in LOAD, as described in Sec. III-C. LOAD retains a clear advantage on narrow beams within the training mass range. At 8 kg on a 10 cm beam, its student achieves approximately 90% success, compared with 70% for Implicit and 12% for Explicit . On an 8 cm beam with a 6 kg payload, the corresponding rates are 63%, 23%, and 2%. These results support the complementary roles of implicit and explicit dynamics information after visual dis-tillation. Generalization to heavier payloads remains limited: at 12 kg, LOAD stays below 50% success at every tested beam width, and Explicit performs better on the wider beams. Thus, LOAD’s advantage after distillation does not extend uniformly beyond the training mass range.

C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented LOAD, a locomotion approach that com-bines terrain perception, explicit inertial estimation, and implicit dynamics features for narrow-beam traversal un-der unknown payloads. Separate recurrent modules recover physically interpretable inertial changes and task-relevant history, while a privileged teacher using height maps is distilled into a deployable student using onboard depth images. Comparisons across six policy variants demonstrate the complementary value of explicit and implicit dynamics information: history remains beneficial even with ground-truth inertial parameters, and the LOAD teacher achieves an overall success rate close to that of LOAD-GT over the tested conditions. The benefit of the combined representation persists after visual distillation, particularly on narrow beams within the training payload range. Hardware experiments demonstrate traversal with unilateral payloads of 9 kg on a 15 cm beam and 6 kg on a 10 cm beam. Generalization beyond the training payload range remains limited. Inertial estimates exhibit increased errors for heav-ier loads, while performance degradation with ground-truth parameters indicates that accurate identification alone does not ensure successful extrapolation. Future work will inves-tigate uncertainty-aware conditioning and training strategies that improve both identification and control under unseen dynamics. We will also extend the evaluation to payload changes during locomotion and more varied support geome-tries, examining how terrain and dynamics perception jointly support reliable contact execution.
