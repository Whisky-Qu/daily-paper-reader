This makes the representa-tion between high-level planning and whole-body control criti-cal: kinematic or contact targets provide compact abstractions but do not directly encode interaction-specific control responses, while directly predicting low-level actions requires the planner to model high-frequency closed-loop feedback. Our Latent Policy Projection (LPP) maps independently acquired task-specific policies into this representation by identifying latent commands whose induced actions match their local control responses, enabling lower-rate planning while retaining the BFM’s high-frequency state feedback. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid loco-manipulation requires both deciding what 

interaction to perform from visual and semantic context and determining how to realize it through balance, contact, and coordinated whole-body control. Recent work has achieved strong task-specific interaction skills, including object ma-nipulation, articulated-object interaction, and object trans-port [1, 2], often using task-specific objectives, observations, arXiv:2610.24675v2 [cs.RO] 20 Oct 2026 and training procedures. Scaling these successes to a versatile policy requires unifying diverse task-level decisions without losing the physical responses that make each skill succeed. A common strategy separates task-level decision making from whole-body control. A high-level policy predicts struc-tured targets such as body motion, end-effector poses, or con-tacts, while a low-level controller realizes them on the robot. However, the same target may require different actions under different contacts, object states, or disturbances, so such representations do not directly capture the state-dependent control responses required for interaction. Predicting low-level actions avoids this abstraction, but offline learning then requires broad coverage of closed-loop corrective responses, which is difficult for contact-rich interaction. This exposes a central challenge for humanoid planning: how can we abstract low-level control complexity without abstracting away the physical responses that make interaction succeed? 

A learned behavioral representation offers a way to resolve this tension. We build on a Behavior Foundation Model (BFM) that exposes an executable behavioral latent space. Given the current robot state and a latent command, BFM produces low-level actions at high frequency. A latent there-fore indexes a state-dependent control policy rather than merely a desired motion, allowing high-level planning to operate over compact behavioral commands while the BFM retains high-frequency closed-loop physical feedback. Using such an interface for versatile loco-manipulation still requires acquiring the interaction capabilities that the planner should invoke. One approach is to collect demon-strations for the desired interactions through a common data-acquisition pipeline [3, 4], but obtaining broad coverage of contact-rich and corrective behavior is expensive. Another approach is to first train task-specific interaction policies and distill them into a generalist. On-policy multi-expert distillation can improve closed-loop coverage by querying these policies at states visited by the generalist, but requires all policies and their environments to remain available during integration. We instead consider a decoupled setting: each interaction policy can be acquired independently, and only afterward incorporated into the shared behavioral interface. Under this decoupled setting, different interaction policies need not share their observations, objectives, or task repre-sentations. They nevertheless share one quantity that directly describes their physical realization: the actions applied to the robot. A latent command from the frozen BFM is grounded in the same quantity, because it also produces robot actions conditioned on the current robot state. This common action space provides a direct way to relate an independently acquired interaction policy to the BFM latent representation. Based on this insight, we propose FIBER , which repre-sents independently acquired interaction policies, or experts, in the executable latent space of a frozen BFM. At the core of FIBER is Latent Policy Projection (LPP), which identifies latent commands by matching the actions induced by the BFM to an expert’s local state-dependent control responses. The resulting projected policy executes through the frozen BFM, preserving high-frequency state feedback while expressing heterogeneous interaction skills through a common lower-rate representation. Because each expert is projected independently, the ex-perts need not share an acquisition procedure or remain jointly available during subsequent training. After projection, we roll out the projected policies and collect their latent commands together with the perceptive and proprioceptive observations available to the final policy. These closed-loop latent rollouts are aggregated across skills to train a single perceptive planner entirely offline. At deployment, the planner predicts future latent commands from onboard observations and a high-level steering condition, while the frozen BFM provides the state-dependent whole-body re-sponses required to execute them. Finally, projection and planning impose different temporal requirements on the latent representation. Matching an ex-pert’s control response favors precise short-horizon behavior, whereas lower-rate planning benefits from stable commands that preserve behavior over longer horizons. We therefore introduce AH-BFM , an adaptive-horizon BFM trained with multi-γ Forward-Backward (FB) learning to support both control fidelity and temporally extended behavioral structure. We evaluate FIBER on three heterogeneous interaction tasks—box pickup, door opening, and object grasping-in simulation and on a real Unitree G1. In simulation, LPP largely preserves expert performance on box pickup and door opening near the interaction regime. The unified perceptive planner achieves 42 .0% mean success, compared with 11 .6% 

for fixed projected latent replay and 3.5% for kinematic planning. Real-world experiments further demonstrate di-rect sim-to-real transfer, interaction chaining, and waypoint-conditioned steering through the same planner. Our contributions are threefold:

A shared executable representation for humanoid interaction planning. We use the latent space of a frozen BFM as a behavioral interface that supports lower-rate high-level planning while retaining state-dependent interaction realization responses.  

> •

A. Humanoid Controller and Behavioral Foundation Models 

Humanoid control has increasingly moved from skill-specific policies toward shared controllers that support broad motor repertoires through a common conditioning interface. Motion-conditioned approaches unify locomotion and whole-body behaviors through dense or sparse motion specifi-cations [5, 6], while recent systems extend unified control toward language-conditioned behaviors and humanoid loco-manipulation [7, 8]. These works demonstrate that diverse behaviors can be consolidated within a single controller, but the supported behaviors are typically acquired through the controller’s predefined training and conditioning interface. Our work instead considers a complementary setting: inte-grating interaction behaviors acquired independently of this interface into an already-trained controller. BFMs expose a learned behavioral latent space as such a shared control interface. BFM-Zero [9] uses FB to learn a promptable humanoid behavioral space, while subsequent behavioral inference methods show that expert behaviors can be recovered by optimizing within a frozen FB latent space [10]. These results establish latent inference as an effective mechanism for behavior reproduction. We build on this capability but study its use as an interface for interac-tion representation, where the latent sequence must capture temporally varying local control responses rather than only recover a stationary behavior or desired state trajectory. This setting further exposes a trade-off in conventional FB learning: the successor horizon favorable for precise local tracking differs from that required to preserve long-term behavior. Our adaptive-horizon BFM is designed to retain both properties within the same behavioral space. 

B. Humanoid Loco-Manipulation and Representation 

Humanoid loco-manipulation requires specifying not only the desired motion of the robot, but also how it should respond to physical interaction with objects and the en-vironment. A broad family of methods makes interaction explicit through structured task representations, including contact sequences, end-effector constraints, task-motion ob-jectives, distance fields, and interaction-preserving references [11–17]. Such representations provide effective and often interpretable interfaces for learning diverse skills. However, they specify interaction through variables chosen in advance; the resulting task description does not directly encode the state-dependent control response induced by contact and environment dynamics. Alternatively, interaction responses can be learned di-rectly through task-specific reinforcement learning (RL) or demonstrations [18–25]. Teleoperation and human-video-based pipelines further broaden the sources from which such behaviors can be acquired [26–32]. These approaches can capture interaction-specific control responses through RL, but the acquired behavior generally remains tied to the specialist policy or downstream training process. In contrast, we decouple interaction acquisition from execution: demonstrated state-action responses are projected into the native latent interface of a frozen behavioral foundation controller, allowing independently acquired interactions to share a common executable substrate. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Overview 

Our goal is to express independently acquired interaction skills in a shared behavioral interface that preserves their state-dependent physical realization and supports unified perceptive planning. FIBER uses the latent space of a frozen BFM as this interface and maps each interaction expert into it by aligning the expert’s local control responses with the actions induced by BFM latent commands. As illustrated in Fig. 2, FIBER proceeds in three stages. First, interaction skills are acquired independently with task-appropriate supervision. Second, LPP maps each expert into the frozen BFM latent space by matching the actions induced by latent commands to the expert’s local control responses. The resulting projected policies are rolled out directly through the shared behavioral interface, after which the original experts are no longer required. Finally, their closed-loop latent rollouts are aggregated to train a single perceptive flow planner entirely offline, conditioned on on-board observations and high-level steering commands. This latent space must support both precise local inter-action and temporally extended whole-body behavior. We therefore introduce AH-BFM , an adaptive-horizon BFM trained with multi-γ FB learning. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate FIBER through simulation and real-world experiments to answer four questions: 

Q1: How faithfully can heterogeneous interaction experts be projected into the shared latent space of a frozen BFM? 

Q2: Can these projected skills be jointly learned by a single planner for versatile humanoid loco-manipulation? 

Q3: Can the unified planner chain projected skills and flexibly steer their execution through various conditions? 

Q4: Which components of FIBER are critical for reliable latent projection and downstream planning? For each projected skill, we collect 20K episodes, and merge them across tasks for FIBER training. Language con-ditions are assigned according to the underlying task, while waypoint conditions are labeled from the robot trajectories in the collected rollouts. 

A. Simulation Evaluation 

We evaluate in IsaacSim, with 128 variations per task :box size and initial placement for pickup, door geometry for opening, and object identity for grasping. We do not report on-policy multi-expert distillation because the experts use different environments, observation spaces, and codebases, and therefore do not share the online rollout interface re-quired for joint distillation. 

a) Action-aligned latent projection: To answer Q1 , we compare each task-specific expert with its LPP projector in Tab. I. LPP largely preserves expert performance on box pickup and door opening, while fine-grained grasping is more challenging, dropping from 97 .0% to 52 .9% .We further compare representations using targets derived from the same successful expert trajectories. Reference-tracking baselines, including the BFM’s own backward rep-resentation B(s), perform poorly, whereas action-aligned projected replay achieves substantially higher success on box pickup and door opening. Notably, FALCON [21], despite learning force-adaptive end-effector tracking under randomized external forces, achieves only 0.4% /2.7% on door opening. These results show that kinematic, behavioral, or generic force-adaptive tracking alone does not recover the task-specific control responses required for interaction, motivating action-aligned latent projection. 

b) Unified perceptive planning: To answer Q2 , we compare replaying LPP-produced latent trajectories with the unified perceptive planner. FIBER improves mean success from 11 .6% to 42 .0% , showing that high-frequency BFM feedback alone is insufficient and that task-level latent replanning remains important. Under the same perceptive planning setup, the Action Planner fails on all tasks, while the Kinematic Planner reaches only 3.5% mean success. Directly planning low-level actions removes high-frequency state feedback between planner updates, whereas predicting kinematic targets does not specify the interaction-specific control response, which leads to failure on contact-rich tasks; 

FIBER retains both through latent planning over BFM. 

c) Skill chaining and conditional steering: To answer 

Q3 , we further test whether the unified planner can compose projected skills under changing high-level commands. As shown in Fig. 3, the same policy sequentially opens the door, picks up the box, and carries it back through the doorway within a single rollout, demonstrating closed-loop chaining of heterogeneous interaction skills.

Together, these comparisons separate the roles of action-aligned representation, latent feedback, and perceptive replanning in FIBER .V. C ONCLUSIONS AND LIMITATIONS 

We presented FIBER , an action-aligned latent planning framework for versatile humanoid loco-manipulation. LPP projects heterogeneous interaction experts into a frozen BFM by matching their short-horizon control responses, producing executable latent trajectories for unified offline perceptive planning. Adaptive multi-γ FB learning supports both precise control and temporally extended behavior, while successor-feature-guided learning enables interaction-preserving behav-ior reuse. Our simulation and real-world experiments demon-strate effective expert projection and perception-conditioned execution across diverse interaction tasks. While our approach enables versatile humanoid loco-manipulation through action-aligned latent planning, extend-ing the underlying BFM toward more dynamic whole-body behaviors, such as stair traversal, remains worth exploring. Moreover, incorporating richer visual and tactile feedback together with online latent adaptation could support inter-action under more diverse contact conditions and facilitate the continual integration of new skills. We hope our work provides insights into scalable humanoid skill acquisition and opens new directions for unified whole-body planning. REFERENCES [1] T. He, Z. Wang, H. Xue, Q. Ben, Z. Luo, W. As in the main paper, skill acquisition is deliberately decoupled from the shared representation. Experts may use different supervision, observations, and training procedures; projection only requires access to the resulting robot actions. We use motion-based and reward-shaped experts for the base interactions, and SF-guided behavior reuse when we specifically want to create diverse compositions between an interaction and native BFM behaviors. 

A. Task-Specific Interaction Experts 

Motion-based experts combine humanoid motion tracking with object and contact objectives. For box pickup and object grasping, the tracked quantities include both body motion and manipulated-object pose, while contact rewards encour-age the demonstrated interaction to be realized physically. The door expert is trained with stage-specific objectives [2] for approach, contact establishment, handle manipulation, and opening. These experts may use different privileged observations and rewards; none of those task-specific inputs are required by the unified planner after projection. For reproducibility, the HDMI-style motion expert uses a privileged observation encoder with a 256-dimensional embedding and a reference-action residual actor. Its actor hidden widths are [512 , 256 , 256] , critic hidden widths are 

[512 , 256 , 128] , and the activation is Mish. The PPO settings for this route are summarized in Tab. VI.

The third acquisition route targets interaction–behavior composition. Given an acquired interaction, we use behav-iors already represented by the BFM to create additional physically valid variants: the interaction reward preserves the task-specific physical requirements, while a native BFM latent specifies the whole-body behavior to be expressed simultaneously. This expands one interaction into a family of compositions without requiring a separate motion reference or task-specific expert for every combination.

D. On-Policy Projection and Data Aggregation 

Projection is trained with single-skill on-policy data ag-gregation. The current projector is executed through the frozen BFM under randomized task initializations. At the states it visits, we query the corresponding expert for body actions and record the BFM inputs, expert observations, and reset masks. Short execution windows are then replayed through the frozen BFM actor to evaluate (56) and (57). The projector is updated and new rollouts are collected with the updated policy. Gradients pass through the frozen BFM actor to the predicted latent, but no BFM parameter is changed and no simulator gradient is required. This DAgger-style procedure [35] exposes the projector to corrective states that are absent from nominal expert trajectories. Exploration noise is applied to the executed latent before projection back to Z and is never added to the expert action label. Once projection is complete, the original expert is no longer required for projected execution or planner-data collection. The projected latent should be interpreted locally: it is identified by the control response induced by the frozen BFM on states visited during projection, rather than by assuming that the expert and BFM policies are globally identical. This is why projected closed-loop rollouts, rather than nominal expert trajectories alone, are used as the supervision for the unified planner. APPENDIX IV UNIFIED LATENT FLOW PLANNING

A. Simulation Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate each task over diverse object or environment variations in IsaacSim. For pickup and door opening, the 

w/ appr. setting initializes the humanoid approximately 0.5–

1.5 m from the interaction target and requires it to approach before execution, whereas w/o appr. initializes the humanoid directly in front of the target. Pickup succeeds if the box is lifted by at least 0.3 m and maintained for at least 1 s; grasping succeeds if the object is lifted by at least 0.1 mand maintained for at least 1 s. Door opening succeeds when the door is opened and the humanoid passes through the doorway. Reported ± values are computed from repeated evaluations with different random seeds to account for sim-ulation stochasticity. Detailed object variation configurations used in evaluation are shown in Tab. X. 

B. Real-World Execution 

The real-world system uses onboard RealSense depth and proprioception. Both the unified planner and the frozen BFM run on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 5000 Blackwell laptop. The TensorRT planner replans at 5 Hz using 10 

Heun integration steps, with approximately 20 ms inference time per planner call, while the BFM runs at 50 Hz with approximately 1 ms per forward pass. We use RTC during deployment to avoid discontinuities between consecutively sampled latent trajectories. At each replanning step, the flow planner predicts a chunk of future latent commands while the robot continues executing the current chunk. The newly generated chunk overlaps tempo-rally with the remaining portion of the previous prediction, and RTC conditions the overlapping prefix of the new sample toward the previously committed trajectory during flow integration. This preserves continuity across replanning boundaries while still allowing later latent commands in the chunk to adapt to the latest onboard observation. In this way, RTC decouples the planner inference latency from continuous robot execution and avoids abrupt latent changes caused by independently sampling each plan.
