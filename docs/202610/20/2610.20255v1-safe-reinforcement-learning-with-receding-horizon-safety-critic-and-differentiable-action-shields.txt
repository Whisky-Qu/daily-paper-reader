# Safe Reinforcement Learning with Receding-Horizon Safety Critic and Differentiable Action Shields 

Siwei Ju 1,∗, Jan Peters 1,2,3,4, and Oleg Arenz 1

Abstract —Despite the Markov assumption, safety failures in robot reinforcement learning rarely occur instantaneously; they typically emerge over several steps as the policy enters unfamiliar states under disturbances and domain shift. We address this limitation by learning a receding-horizon safety critic 

that predicts whether a state will remain non-terminating over the next receding horizon, enabling anticipatory detection of impending locomotion failures. The critic is trained from rollout-derived horizon labels with a composite objective that combines horizon classification, control-barrier-function-inspired temporal consistency, and smoothness regularization, and we aggregate an ensemble of critics with a conservative Conditional Value at Risk estimate to account for uncertainty. Building on the learned critic, we derive a closed-form, differentiable CBF-style safety filter that minimally modifies the policy action to improve predicted safety, and integrate it into PPO with an anticipation loss to reduce policy–filter mismatch during training. In simulated quadruped locomotion, our method improves robustness under action noise, external perturbations, and terrain shifts, reducing catastrophic failures while maintaining competitive returns. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning (RL) has enabled increasingly agile legged locomotion, yet safety remains a central obstacle to de-ploying learned controllers beyond narrow training conditions. In practice, many locomotion failures are delayed: a destabi-lizing contact, a slight foot scuff, or a suboptimal recovery step may not cause an immediate violation, but can drive the robot into an unrecoverable state several steps later. This makes safety inherently horizon-dependent in locomotion, especially under disturbances and terrain-induced contact variability. A common approach to safety in continuous control is to enforce state constraints using control barrier functions (CBFs) [3] and differentiable safety layers that project actions through constrained optimization [1, 31]. Data-driven CBF variants often learn uncertainty or residual terms to com-pensate modeling error [27, 9], while neural CBFs (NCBFs) parameterize the certificate itself to reduce manual design effort [37, 24, 20, 25, 36, 17, 2]. In safe RL, CBF-style shields are frequently combined with actor–critic training [10, 7, 8, 32, 34] or treated as part of the environment dynamics [6]. Despite

from one-step constraints on the safety layer that may not anticipate multi-step failures, and (iii) policy–filter mismatch ,where the executed (filtered) actions induce a distribution shift that is not always accounted for during policy optimization. Predictive safety filters address myopia by enforcing safety over a horizon, typically relying on known or nominal dynam-ics models and uncertainty representations [28, 29, 30]. In par-allel, safe RL has explored learning feasibility or long-horizon safety signals that resemble critics/value functions [13, 26] and structured safety enforcement that avoids generic QPs when dynamics are known [15]. For robot control, complementary approaches predict failure and switch to recovery behaviors (e.g., safe-fall policies) [19, 14], or deploy CBF shields on complex platforms such as humanoids [33, 34]. These strands collectively indicate the need for safety mechanisms that are horizon-aware, computationally lightweight, and compatible with end-to-end policy learning under distribution shift. We propose a receding-horizon safety critic that directly predicts whether the system will remain non-terminated over the next H steps from a state. The critic is trained from rollouts using horizon-wise binary labels, shaped by a temporal consistency prior inspired by discrete-time CBF conditions, and regularized for smoothness to improve robustness under distribution shift. To make the safety signal actionable for control, we derive a differentiable safety layer that minimally corrects the policy action via a single-constraint projection with a closed-form solution, and we use a slack variable scaled by the ensemble uncertainty. Because the safety layer is differentiable, we integrate it into PPO and introduce a safety anticipation loss so the policy learns to reduce interventions and align with the horizon-safety objective. We evaluate the approach on quadruped locomotion under controlled domain shifts, including stochastic action noise and external perturbations on both flat and heightfield terrains. Across disturbance intensities, the proposed method substan-tially improves success rates while maintaining competitive returns compared to PPO with domain randomization and PPO augmented with an NCBF-style safety layer. An ablation on the horizon length reveals a clear trade-off: longer horizons better mitigate myopic failures but are harder to train and can become over-conservative under shift. This work intersects barrier-function-based safety, predic-tive safety filtering, and safe reinforcement learning for legged robots. We review these areas and highlight the gap our approach targets: horizon-aware safety enforcement that is differentiable, model-free at deployment, and compatible with end-to-end policy optimization under distribution shift. 

A. Control Barrier Functions, Predictive Safety Layers, and Neural CBFs 

Control Barrier Function (CBF)s enforce forward invariance of a safe set by constraining control inputs and are a standard tool for safety-critical control [3]. To handle uncertainty and disturbances, data-driven CBF methods often assume a nom-inal model and learn learn the residual and uncertainty from data, e.g., reducing model error in the CBF derivative [27] or enabling long-term safe operation under unknown distur-bances [9]. For neural policies and end-to-end training, CBF constraints are frequently implemented as differentiable optimization lay-ers (typically QPs) so gradients can pass through the safety filter [3, 1, 31]. In safe RL, such filters are sometimes treated as part of the environment dynamics [6]; examples include CBF layers added to SAC [10], model-based filtering with learned uncertainty (e.g., GP) combined with RL [7], and methods that integrate CBF/CLF constraints into actor–critic updates or reward design [8, 32, 34]. A recurring issue is policy–filter mismatch : the executed (filtered) action can deviate from the policy output, yet many approaches do not explicitly optimize under the induced distribution shift. Beyond one-step constraints, predictive safety filters enforce safety over a future horizon, typically requiring nominal or learned dynamics and optional uncertainty models [28, 29, 30]. Finally, Neural Control Barrier Function (NCBF)s param-eterize certificates with neural networks to avoid manual barrier design; recent work studies verification and training of NCBFs [37, 24], learning certificates from demonstrations [20, 25, 36, 17], incorporating physics/PDE structure [2], and appli-cations under input limits or from sensory predictors [16, 12]. These tools are increasingly used in robotics, including legged locomotion and manipulation [4, 11, 21, 34, 18], motivating safety mechanisms that remain effective under domain shifts. 

B. Relationship to Safe Reinforcement Learning 

Safe RL methods can be broadly grouped into approaches that (i) learn a safety/feasibility signal analogous to a value function, and (ii) enforce constraints via projection or struc-tured action correction. The former methods learns a scalar signal predictive of future constraint satisfaction to guide exploration or policy improvement. [13] explicitly targets long-horizon safety under uncertainty, emphasizing that safety is a temporally extended property rather than a purely local certificate. In parallel, [26] connects RL value functions to the barrier-function viewpoint, bridging critics and formal safety reasoning. These perspec-tives motivate learning safety critics that encode horizon-aware feasibility. Another line avoids per-step QPs by exploiting constraint structure. ATACOM provides a closed-form mechanism to enforce multiple constraints for known dynamics by operating on the constraint manifold, reducing computational overhead relative to generic QP-based filters [15]. Such methods are efficient when modeling assumptions hold, but do not directly address end-to-end integration with policy learning under domain shift, which is central in robotics. 

C. Safe Reinforcement Learning for Robot Control 

In robot control, safe RL commonly follows two directions. (i) Risk prediction with recovery policies. These approaches train predictors for failure events and switch to a recovery behavior when risk is high. SafeFall trains a falling predictor paired with a safe-fall policy to mitigate catastrophic failures at deployment [19]. Related work emphasizes high-performance locomotion with safety-aware objectives (e.g., collision-free high-speed behaviors) [14]. While effective, recovery pipelines introduce additional controllers and switching logic, and typi-cally do not provide a single differentiable safety mechanism jointly optimized with the primary policy. (ii) Safety layers / shields based on barrier functions. A second family uses CBF-based filtering to minimally mod-ify policy actions to satisfy constraints. Robot-focused work demonstrates CBF-based shields on complex platforms such as humanoids [33] and shows that incorporating CBF structure can also benefit learning via reward shaping or auxiliary objec-tives [34]. Nevertheless, three practical limitations remain: (1) 

training–deployment mismatch when applying the filter only at deployment, it may result in distribution shift; instead using safety filtering also during training can be important [5, 34]; (2) computational overhead from solving optimization prob-lems online, which hinders real-time deployment and large-scale RL training [33]; and (3) local (one-step) constraints and delayed failures: standard CBF/NCBF conditions constrain the instantaneous change of a barrier value, which can be too local in domains where failures emerge over multiple steps (e.g., loss of balance after a disturbance). Predictive safety filters address this by enforcing constraints over a horizon, but they typically lead to more involved multi-step optimization and stronger modeling assumptions. We address these issues by learning a receding-horizon safety critic and using it as a differentiable safety layer that does not require an explicit dynamics model. This enables end-to-end safety-shielded training to reduce distribution mis-match, avoids per-step QP solves through learned differen-tiable action correction, and mitigates myopia by optimizing multi-step safety through the horizon-aware critic. f) Data buffer and sampling.: To stabilize safety-critic learning under a non-stationary policy and severe class imbal-ance, we maintain two separate replay buffers: a safe buffer 

D+ that stores horizon-safe transitions and an unsafe buffer 

D− that stores transitions that lead to (or contain) safety violations. Using two buffers, rather than a single mixed replay, follows common practice in safety learning and risk-aware RL when negative events can be rare and their frequency changes over training [13, 35]. This design is motivated by the fact that as the policy improves, unsafe events become increasingly infrequent. With-out explicit control of negative replay, the training distribu-tion for the safety predictor can drift toward predominantly safe samples, causing the predictor to overfit to the current training stage, forget previously observed failure modes, and degrade generalization under distribution shift. By preserving a dedicated set of unsafe samples and continuing to replay them, D− acts as a long-term memory of rare but safety-critical behaviors, helping maintain calibration and reduce false negatives. During training, each minibatch is constructed by sampling a fixed ratio from the two buffers. Concretely, given minibatch size B and positive sampling ratio ρ+ ∈ [0 , 1] , we draw 

B+ = ⌊ρ+B⌋ samples from D+ and B− = B − B+ samples from D−, and then merge them into a single minibatch. The ratio ρ+ provides direct control over the effective class balance seen by the safety predictor, allowing us to maintain sufficient exposure to unsafe samples throughout training even when violations become rare. We presented a safe locomotion framework that replaces myopic one-step safety constraints with a receding-horizon safety critic and a closed-form, differentiable safety shield. The critic predicts horizon-wise feasibility from rollout-derived labels and is stabilized through temporal-consistency and smoothness regularization, while an ensemble with CVaR aggregation provides conservative safety estimates under dis-tribution shift. The resulting safety layer performs minimal action corrections without requiring a dynamics model at deployment and can be trained end-to-end with PPO via a safety-anticipation loss, reducing policy–filter mismatch. Across simulated quadruped locomotion under controlled domain shifts (action noise, external perturbations, and terrain changes), our method improves success rates while preserving competitive returns relative to PPO with domain randomization and an NCBF-based shield. An ablation over the horizon length highlights a practical trade-off between anticipatory safety and critic trainability/conservatism. Because of the better generalizability and robustness of the state-action model, we choose to present it as our main method for the rest of the evaluations. C. Safety Critics vs. SafeFall Baseline 

A key contribution of our approach is a structured training pipeline for the safety critic, formulated as an unsafe-event predictor. In humanoid locomotion, falling is one of the most critical safety events and has been extensively studied in recent work. In particular, SafeFall [19] is closely related in spirit: it trains a predictor for the probability of falling, but differs in how supervision is constructed and how the loss is defined. Our method trains the critic in a receding-horizon manner with temporally discounted labels, which yields dense supervi-sion at every timestep and supports flexible horizon selection at both training and deployment. In contrast, SafeFall discretizes each trajectory into three regions—safe ( t < T 1), ambiguous (T1 < t < T 2), and unsafe ( t > T − T2, where T denotes the trajectory length)—using hand-crafted triggers tied to a fixed number of steps. This design encodes a similar intuition (early states are safer; states close to failure are riskier), but it imposes several practical limitations. First, because the labeling is defined relative to a fixed tra-jectory length, it implicitly restricts training and data collection to rollouts with a pre-specified horizon. This can reduce label coverage when episodes terminate early or vary in length. Additionally, by construction, each trajectory is anchored around a single terminal event, which limits the representation of repeated near-failure episodes or multiple failure modes within the same rollout (e.g., stumble–recovery–fall). Second, the supervision is highly sensitive to the threshold choices 

(T1, T 2): shifting either boundary changes which states are treated as safe versus ambiguous/unsafe, directly affecting both class balance and the sharpness of the decision boundary learned by the predictor. As a result, the predictor quality can degrade under distribution shift if the step-based segmentation no longer aligns with the true time-to-failure statistics. Third, our safety predictor leverages an ensemble of models to better capture uncertainty. The ensemble approach allows the safety critic to provide more accurate risk estimates and provide more conservative predictions, which is particularly valuable when faced with the complexities of real-world environments. However, the ability to predict failures robustly and as early as possible is crucial for a safety layer. By identifying the risk of failure sooner, the system can take corrective actions proactively, preventing the system from reaching an unrecoverable state. Furthermore, the softer gradients in the safety prediction lead to more gradual adjustments in the action, preventing large, abrupt changes that could exceed action limits or cause instability in the system. This smoother action modification is essential for maintaining continuous and safe operation, as sudden jumps in action could result in undesirable system behavior or damage. Therefore, our approach not only offers earlier failure detection but also produces safer, more controlled interventions compared to the binary predictions of the SafeFall-style predictor. D. RL-PPO with Domain Randomization 

We employ a set of domain randomizations to improve robustness and expose the safety critic to diverse dynam-ics during training. The randomizations are implemented as modular components and can be grouped into the following categories:  

> •

MuJoCo model randomization: randomizes physical pa-rameters of the simulator model (e.g., masses, inertias, joint/actuator properties, and contact-related parameters) to induce dynamics variability.  

> •

Observation noise: injects noise and/or bias into propri-oceptive observations to emulate sensor corruption and partial observability.  

> •

Action delay: applies stochastic control latency to account for delays between policy output and actuation.  

> •

Joint dropout: randomly masks (drops) a subset of joint signals to simulate intermittent sensing/estimation fail-ures.  

> •

Initial-state randomization: perturbs the initial base pose/velocity and joint configuration at reset to diversify starting conditions.  

> •

External perturbations: applies impulse-like disturbances directly to the base and joints. This is the primary shift we sweep at evaluation time and is detailed next. Among the different domain randomization aspects, we elaborate in detail on how we do the evaluations with the controlled sim2sim shift on the external perturbations. The implementation details can be found in our codebase.
