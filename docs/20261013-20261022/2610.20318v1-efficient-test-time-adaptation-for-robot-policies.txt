# Abstract 

Reinforcement-learning policies for robot locomotion can fail at deployment when operating conditions shift (e.g., rough or slippery terrain, payload changes, sens-ing degradation), and retraining or manual recalibration is often infeasible under on-device latency and memory constraints. We study test-time adaptation (TTA) for robotic control: adapting a pretrained policy online and self-supervised during execution, without access to rewards. Our starting point is a lightweight baseline that updates only the actor using a frozen pretrained critic as a deployment-time per-formance surrogate. Building on this, we introduce TEMPO, a test-time entropy-regularized, memory-guided policy optimization method that stabilizes critic-driven updates under severe shift. TEMPO combines (i) an entropy-promoting regularizer on the critic’s predictive distribution to mitigate overconfident extrapolation, and (ii) a score-based memory, inspired by prioritized replay, that prioritizes low-value (“hard”) observations and applies importance sampling to focus limited compu-tation on the most informative deployment conditions. Across Go1, Go2, and G1 in MuJoCo Playground under four representative shifts and multiple archi-tectures, TEMPO yields consistent gains, reaching up to 80% improvement in a single-device setting. We further validate on a physical Unitree Go2 with an added calf payload, where fully on-device adaptation improves performance over the non-adapted baseline across real-world episodes. 

# 1 Introduction 

Robots deployed in the real world must contend with operating conditions that can diverge sharply from those encountered during training [Kumar et al., 2021]. These discrepancies arise from multiple sources: terrain variability (e.g., rocks, low-friction surfaces) [Sun et al., 2025], hardware changes (e.g., localized payloads or partial actuator degradation) [Pouroullis et al., 2025], emergent situations (e.g., a weight stuck on one arm/calf) [Zhang et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2023], and the broader sim-to-real gap between simulated and physical environments [Peng et al., 2018]. In practice, such factors rarely occur in isolation: a deployed robot may simultaneously experience altered contact dynamics, sensor noise, actuator wear, and previously unseen environmental configurations. These distribution shifts can substantially degrade policies that perform well under nominal conditions (refer to Figure 1 left), making online adaptation central to robust autonomy. A common response to combat this problem is to apply large-scale domain randomization during reinforcement learning to improve zero-shot generalization [Tobin et al., 2017, Peng et al., 2018]. While effective in some cases, this approach is fundamentally constrained by what can be specified in advance: it presumes one can enumerate relevant sources of variability, model them with sufficient fidelity in simulation, and collect enough data to cover their interactions. When robots encounter truly novel conditions, adaptation is performed offline. This interrupts operation to gather new data and fine-tune from scratch, which is misaligned with real-time, on-device compute and reliability

Architectural add-ons for adaptation can help but often increase system complexity and reduce portability across tasks, embodiments, and deployment settings [Kumar et al., 2021]. We study Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) [Wang et al., 2020, Sun et al., 2020] as a practical alternative for robotic control under distribution shift. TTA is well-suited for tackling this problem as it adapts the pretrained model at test time in an online and self-supervised manner, alleviating the need to disrupt the inference stream for retraining and requiring no labeled data. Thus, our starting point is a simple, general baseline: we update only the actor (policy) parameters at deployment while freezing the critic, using a compact on-device memory of recent observations. At periodic intervals during execution, the actor is adapted by maximizing the critic’s estimated action value on stored observations—treating the critic as a learned performance surrogate and requiring no online rewards. Unlike standard deployments where the critic is used primarily for training and often discarded at inference, here we actively leverage the pretrained critic during runtime. While distribution shift also affects the critic, we find it still provides a sufficiently informative signal to guide test-time actor updates [Chen et al., 2023]; nonetheless, naive value maximization can overfit to spurious, overconfident estimates [Kumar et al., 2020] and under-explore the loss landscape. In this work, and to address these limitations, we introduce an enhanced framework with two com-plementary elements. First, we add an entropy-promoting regularizer on the critic’s predictive distribution during adaptation, which tempers overconfidence under shift and biases updates toward regions that are simultaneously high-value and high-uncertainty. This yields more informative gradi-ents due to the stability and exploration benefits of maximum-entropy objectives in RL [Eysenbach and Levine, 2021], which we adapt to the critic ’s uncertainty at test time. Second, and in the spirit of prioritized experience replay [Schaul et al., 2016], we employ a score-based memory that prioritizes observations inversely proportional to the critic’s predicted value, coupled with importance sampling to focus updates on the most informative deployment conditions under tight memory and latency budgets. We evaluate on Go1, Go2, and G1 in Mujoco Playground simulation [Zakka et al., 2025], spanning quadruped and humanoid morphologies under four representative shifts (mass on torso, mass on calf, rocky terrain, slippery terrain) and three different architectures, and observe up to 50% performance gains very efficiently in a single-device setting. We further validate our approach on a physical Unitree G O2 robot by attaching a {3, 2} kg calf payload and performing fully on-device TTA , observing trends consistent with simulation. We can summarize our contributions in four folds: • A lightweight, on-device TTA framework for RL control. We adapt only the actor parameters while freezing the critic, maintain a compact memory of recent observations, and perform periodic, self-supervised updates that fit strict control-loop latency and compute budgets. • Entropy-regularized critic-driven adaptation. We introduce an objective that maximizes the critic’s estimated action value while promoting higher-entropy critic outputs during test-time updates—mitigating overconfident value extrapolation and preserving local action coverage; the procedure is architecture-agnostic and supervision-free. • Score-based memory with importance sampling. We propose a memory mechanism that assigns each observation a score inversely proportional to the critic’s predicted value, stores harder samples, and employs importance sampling to focus on more informative samples under memory constraints. • Comprehensive validation in simulation and on-device. We show consistent gains on GO1/G O2/G1 embodiments and on four deployment shifts, and confirm the same trends on a physical Unitree G O2 via fully on-device adaptation with a {3, 2} kg calf payload. 22 Robot Adaptation as Test Time Adaptation 

2.1 Problem Formulation and Evaluation Protocol RL setting and distribution shift. We consider locomotion as a Markov decision process (MDP)

At deployment, the robot operates under a shifted test-time distribution (e.g., altered friction, payload changes, rocky terrain), which can be viewed as changes in the observations (i.e from o to ˆo) encountered by the agent and in the induced observation-transition process (i.e. from P to ˆP ). We assume the deployed policy is pretrained using an actor–critic framework [Haarnoja et al., 2018, Fujimoto et al., 2018]. In particular, alongside the pretrained policy πθ0 , training produces a critic Qψ0 (o, a ) that estimates the action-value of executing action a when observing o. In contrast with the common asymmetric setup [Nahrendra et al., 2023] where the critic is fed privileged information from the underlying simulator state, we consider a critic that consumes the same set of observations as the actor. 

Test-time adaptation as an online stream problem. At test time, the robot receives a stream of unlabeled observations {ot}t≥0 governed by the actions taken by the policy πθt and the transition dynamics ˆP . A Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) method is an update rule g that uses only quantities available online at inference time to update either the model parameters, the received observation, or both. This formulation is intentionally general: g may adapt the policy parameters θ, the critic parameters ψ, both, or a subset of either. 

Online evaluation protocol. Following the online/streaming evaluation paradigm [Cai et al., 2021, Ghunaim et al., 2023, Alfarra et al., 2024], we simulate the interaction between the deployed agent and the test stream at each time step t as: 1. The environment reveals an observation ot ∼ ˆP (ot−1, a t−1).2. The agent selects and executes an action at ∼ πθt (ot).3. The adaptation rule updates parameters: (θt+1 , ψ t+1 ) ← g(θt, ψ t).Notably, this protocol does not assume access to rewards or labels at test time. Although rewards are not used by the adaptation mechanism, performance is evaluated using task-level metrics computed offline or by the benchmark (e.g., episodic return in simulation, distance traveled, tracking error, stability/fall rate, task success). These metrics are used solely for reporting and comparison, not as supervision for adaptation. 

2.2 Constructing a Baseline 

Test-time adaptation for robot control is challenging for two reasons. (i) As opposed to massively par-allel simulation-based training [Li et al., 2023], deployment typically occurs in a single environment with limited diversity over short time horizons. Consecutive observations are strongly correlated and provide a narrow view of the shifted dynamics. (ii) The distribution shift can be abrupt (e.g., payload changes or slippery terrain), yet the adaptation budget is small and must fit within on-device latency constraints. These constraints make standard TTA baselines (e.g., Tent [Wang et al., 2020]) as well as RL-based alternatives (e.g., OPEX [Park et al., 2024]) ineffective in our setting (see Fig. 1, middle) For simplicity, we assume throughout that the policy is deterministic ( i.e. ai = πθt (oi)).

# 3 Test-time Entropy regularized, Memory guided Policy Optimization 

In the previous section, we formulated online test-time adaptation for a pretrained policy under distribution shift and introduced QM AX , which adapts the policy by maximizing a pretrained critic on a small buffer of recent observations. As we demonstrate empirically in Section 5.1, QM AX 

can be effective, but it may become unstable under severe shifts: when the critic is queried out of distribution, directly ascending Qψ0 (o, a t) may yield overly sharp updates, reduced action coverage, and brittle closed-loop behavior. We propose TEMPO (Test-time Entropy-regularized, Memory-guided Policy Optimization ), which improves stability and sample-efficiency by combining two lightweight modifications: (i) An entropy-regularized critic objective, and (ii) a score-based memory that prioritizes informative test-time observations. As described in Section 2.1, TEMPO updates only the policy parameters θ at test time. 

3.1 Entropy Maximization

# 4 Related Work 

Domain Shifts on Robots Robots deployed outside controlled training conditions face domain shifts such as changes in contact dynamics, inertial properties, and sensing, which can substantially degrade locomotion policies. A common response is to harden policies in simulation via accurate actuator modeling [Tan et al., 2018, Hwangbo et al., 2019, Zhu et al., 2026, Shin et al., 2025], domain randomization [Peng et al., 2018, Lee et al., 2020] and massively parallel training [Rudin et al., 2022, Zakka et al., 2025]. This improves zero-shot robustness within the training envelope. To better handle unstructured environments, many approaches additionally incorporate exteroception (e.g., elevation maps or images) to anticipate terrain-induced shifts [Miki et al., 2022, Lai et al., 2023, He et al., 2025, Yu et al., 2024, Dong et al., 2025], or auxiliary mechanisms (e.g., asymmetric training, distillation, learned estimators) that infer latent factors [Chen et al., 2020, Kumar et al., 2021, Ji et al., 2022, Choi et al., 2023]. However, when deployment conditions drift beyond the training distribution, these approaches often still require retraining or additional structure, motivating lightweight deployment-time adaptation under strict hardware constraints. Adapting at deployment time has been explored in adjacent settings: Park et al. [2024] analyze offline RL bottlenecks and propose simple test-time policy improvement methods, while Chen et al. [2023] adapt on-the-fly in single-life deployment by selecting and modulating a repertoire of pretrained behaviors using value estimates. In contrast, we target lightweight on-device test-time adaptation for locomotion under strict latency and memory budgets, by adapting a single deployed actor via critic-guided updates. 

Test-Time Adaptation Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) mitigates the performance drop of pre-trained models under test-time distribution shift by adapting at inference using only unlabeled test data [Mancini et al., 2018, Kojima et al., 2022]. Methods either update model parameters online [Liang et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2020] or adapt the test input to better match the training distribution [Gao et al., 2022]. Early approaches focused on updating Batch Normalization statis-tics [Li et al., 2016], while later work introduced stronger self-supervised objectives, including entropy minimization and stabilized variants [Wang et al., 2020, Niu et al., 2022, 2023], information maximization [Liang et al., 2020], and consistency/teacher–student updates [Yuan et al., 2023], as well as auxiliary-task-driven adaptation [Alfarra et al., 2025]. Translating TTA to robotics is challenging because adaptation must preserve stable closed-loop control under strict latency/memory budgets and highly correlated on-policy streams; our work addresses this by tailoring lightweight, critic-guided TTA to on-device locomotion. 

# 5 Experiments 

Environments and pretrained policies. We evaluate TTA on three simulated locomotion tasks from MuJoCo Playground [Zakka et al., 2025]: G O1-JOYSTICK (quadruped), G O2-JOYSTICK (quadruped), and G1-JOYSTICK (humanoid). Since Section 2 places no assumptions on the policy architecture, we consider three representative pretrained policy families: F AST -TD3 [Seo et al., 2025], S IMBA V2 [Lee et al., 2025], and Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) [Kumar et al., 2021]. 

Training protocol and distribution shifts. To disentangle test-time robustness from training-time regularization, we train πθ on a flat terrain without domain randomization, and evaluate (and adapt) under a single fixed test environment that emulates a realistic deployment condition. We consider four shift types parameterized by a severity scalar δ: (i) Rough terrain , with height variation δ; (ii) 

Calf mass perturbation , attaching a mass of δ kg to one calf; (iii) Torso mass perturbation , attaching a mass of δ kg to the torso; and (iv) Slippery terrain , setting the ground friction coefficient to 1 − δ.Unless otherwise stated, each test episode contains a single shift type (i.e., no mixed corruptions). 

Adaptation protocol. Across all methods, we use Adam with learning rate 5 × 10 −6. We adapt all learnable parameters of πθ while freezing the critic(s). We maintain a replay buffer of size |B| = 16 kand use batches of size B = 2 k. Note that with 49-dimensional observations and |B| = 16 k, the FIFO buffer footprint is merely ≈ 3 MiB; score-based variants require ≈ 3.05 MiB. Adaptation is performed online with period K = 128 : every K environment steps, we sample a batch from 

B, compute gradients, and take a single optimizer step. For TEMPO we set the entropy weight to 

λ = 0 .5. In the following sections, we will provide a comprehensive evaluation demonstrating the benefits of TTA under distribution shift and the consistent performance gains of TEMPO. Unless stated otherwise, we fix the policy architecture to Fast-TD3 and the environment to Go2 on Flat terrain without domain randomization. Further experimental details can be found in Appendix B.1.

5.4 Continual TTA 

So far, we evaluated adaptation under a fixed deployment condition. In practice, however, distribution shift is often non-stationary : terrain properties can change gradually, payloads can vary over time, and contact dynamics can drift during prolonged operation. We therefore consider a continual TTA [Wang et al., 2022] setting in which the deployed policy experiences a sequence of increasingly difficult conditions, implemented by continually increasing the severity of the domain shift during evaluation. Figure 4 reports average return as adaptation progresses in this non-stationary stream. The results highlight a clear separation between methods: TEMPO exhibits faster recovery after the shift intensifies and reaches a higher performance plateau than QMAX. This gap is most evident as the stream becomes more challenging, where naive critic maximization improves initially but saturates earlier, while TEMPO continues to make progress and maintains stronger steady-state performance. Overall, these results indicate that TEMPO is not only effective under a fixed test condition, but also well-suited to continual deployment where the test distribution evolves over time and adaptation must remain stable under compounding shift severity. 

Performance on Source Domain. A key concern in continual test-time adaptation is forgetting :gains under shifted conditions should not degrade behavior when the environment returns to nominal. To evaluate this, we append the source domain (flat terrain without domain shift) at the end of the continual sequence and directly assess the adapted policies (Appendix B.4, Fig. 9). We observe that both TEMPO and QMAX recover source-domain performance instantly , matching the original policy without any transient degradation or additional adaptation, indicating no observable forgetting.

# 6 Conclusions 

In this work, we studied reward-free, on-device test-time adaptation for robot locomotion under deployment-time distribution shift. Starting from a lightweight baseline that updates only the actor using a frozen pretrained critic, we introduced TEMPO, which stabilizes critic-guided adaptation through entropy regularization and a score-based memory that prioritizes informative test-time observations. Across simulated locomotion benchmarks and multiple shift types, architectures, and environments, TEMPO consistently improves over no adaptation and naive critic maximization, with the clearest gains under severe shifts. 12 A Test-time Entropy regularized, Memory guided Policy Optimization 

In Section 3, we proposed TEMPO, an efficient, on-device friendly TTA method for adapting robotic policies. In the construction of TEMPO, we followed Seo et al. [2025] in accessing the distributional critic. We note that the output of the distributional critic is nothing but the output of the penultimate layer of the critic network. Refer to the works of Seo et al. [2025], Lee et al. [2025] for further details. Due to the space constraints, we did not provide a full explicit algorithm to describe how one can implement TEMPO in practice. For completeness, we provide these algorithms in this Section. Algorithm 1 summarizes the high level interaction between the test stream and the pretrained policy equipped with our proposed TEMPO. Given an observation vector ot, the policy decides on the action 

at. TEMPO leverages this observation vector and its corresponding action in updating its memory bank B according to Algorithm 2. When there are enough samples in the memory to conduct an adaptation step, TEMPO samples a batch with importance sampling according to Eq. 4 and runs a single update step with the loss in Eq 2. We note that for efficiency purposes, TEMPO conducts a single update step every K received observations to keep the additional compute requirement light. Algorithm 2 describes the score based memory update that we proposed in section 3.2. In a nutshell, as long as the memory bank not full, we store all received observation accompanied by their corresponding score. When hitting the capacity limit, we check if the newly received observation has a better score than the sample with the lowest score in memory, then we evict that sample and store the newly received one. We note that for additional implementation efficiency purposes, one can leverage directly the action from step 4 in Algorithm 1 instead of recomputing it in step 1 in Algorithm 2 (one less forward pass).

B.4 Performance on Source Domain 

A key concern in continual test-time adaptation is catastrophic forgetting: parameters adapted to severe deployment shifts may degrade performance once the environment returns to nominal conditions. This risk is particularly acute in on-device, online settings, where updates are applied continuously without explicit supervision, replay from the source domain, or reset mechanisms. To assess whether critic-guided test-time adaptation induces such forgetting, we evaluate both QMAX and TEMPO after prolonged exposure to increasingly severe distribution shifts, followed by a return to the source domain (flat terrain without perturbations). 16 .8 at β = 0 (QMAX) to 16 .3, 16 .1, 15 .7, and 15 .6 for β = {0.02 , 0.04 , 0.06 , 0.08 }, respectively. Similarly, for MC=5, performance decreases from 14 .7 at β = 0 to 14 .4, 13 .7, 13 .6, and 13 .3. Thus, the strongest TTT configuration corresponds to β = 0 , which exactly recovers QMAX. 

# C Compute Requirements, Limitations, and Broader Impact 

Compute Requirements. All our experiments that were conducted on simulation were run on NVIDIA V100 GPUs including training, evaluation, and TTA (each experiment was run on a single GPU). Regarding the real world experiments, we ran our TTA pipeline on the Unitree Go2 robot leveraging four CPU cores (2 for inference stream and 1 for adaptation and 1 for odometry). 

Limitations. Our study focuses on reward-free, critic-guided test-time adaptation for locomotion policies under deployment-time distribution shifts, and some limitations remain. First, while TEMPO is designed to be lightweight and on-device friendly, it still relies on access to a pretrained critic that is reasonably informative under shift; if the critic is severely miscalibrated or trained on a highly mismatched domain, adaptation quality may degrade. Second, TEMPO induces an additional, albeit small, memory and compute cost during inference. 

Broader Impact. This work aims to improve the robustness of robotic systems under deployment-time distribution shifts, reducing the need for manual recalibration and improving autonomy. However, adaptive systems may introduce risks if deployed without adequate testing and oversight, especially in safety-critical environments. We emphasize that our method is intended to complement, not replace, careful system design, and we encourage responsible use in real-world settings. 17
