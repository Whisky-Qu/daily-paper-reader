Abstract
World models enable embodied agents to anticipate action consequences, yet jointly
modeling locomotion and manipulation across diverse embodiments remains chal-
lenging due to heterogeneous action spaces and limited multimodal supervision. We
present Ropedia W0, the first egocentric loco-manipulation world foundation model
spanning humans and robots with diverse embodiments. We curate Xp-Data, com-
prising 10k hours of egocentric human data and 3k hours of robot data with unified
multimodal annotations and systematic quality control. A unified kinematic represen-
tation encodes locomotion through camera poses and manipulation through projected
skeletons, with robot skeletons combining embodiment-specific forward kinematics
with extensive manual keypoint annotation and correction. We also introduce Xp-
Teleop, a long-horizon loco-manipulation benchmark based on high-quality dexterous
humanoid teleoperation data. Evaluations demonstrate state-of-the-art performance
across five cross-embodiment benchmarks (DreamDojo, Ψ0, ω-HOME, Xp-Teleop, and
AgiBot) and on held-out human egocentric video, from zero-shot inference to few-shot
and full-data post-training. Scaling human pre-training data improves performance
on both human and robot evaluations. After distillation, our model supports real-time,
minute-long rollouts, enabling live VR interaction. Applications in policy evaluation,
model-based planning, and world–action modeling further demonstrate the model’s
utility for embodied decision-making.
1. Introduction
World models predict how environments evolve in response to actions [42, 14] and support
decision-making by imagining future outcomes [43], giving them considerable potential in
embodied intelligence. However, most existing models [119, 122, 4] focus on fixed-base, gripper-
based manipulation, leaving locomotion-induced viewpoint changes and dexterous hand–object
interactions largely underexplored. Moreover, generalizing across embodiments poses a further
challenge. DreamDojo [34] replaces latent-action conditioning with robot-specific actions during
post-training, and Cosmos3 [1] relies on domain-specific action projections despite a shared
backbone; both therefore require a dedicated control interface or additional adaptation for each
new embodiment. Therefore, jointly modeling locomotion and manipulation while seamlessly
generalizing across diverse embodiments remains an open problem.
Learning a generalizable world model hinges on large-scale, high-quality egocentric video
paired with rich multimodal annotations, such as camera trajectories, hand motion, and task
descriptions. While robot demonstrations across diverse tasks and embodiments remain ex-
pensive to collect, egocentric human video offers an abundant and complementary source of
experience on how humans navigate and interact with their surroundings. However, existing
public egocentric datasets [39, 40, 46, 113, 27, 2, 78] frequently suffer from incomplete or unreli-
able annotations. Specifically, many lack metric camera trajectories, provide noisy or missing
hand poses, and adopt heterogeneous formats that hinder joint training. Furthermore, the scale
of these datasets remains limited, falling short of the data required to train a generalizable world
model.
To address these challenges, we present Ropedia W0, a unified egocentric world model that
scales learning from human and robot videos for cross-embodiment loco-manipulation. We
2


curate Xp-Data, comprising 10k hours of egocentric human data and 3k hours of robot data,
with unified geometric, kinematic, and semantic annotations produced through automated
processing, manual robot-keypoint annotation, and quality checks. To connect human mo-
tion and robot actions, we introduce a shared explicit kinematic representation that combines
metric camera poses for locomotion-induced viewpoint changes with projected skeletons for
manipulation. The skeletons combine human pose annotations with robot forward kinematics
(FK) and manually annotated or corrected robot keypoints, preserving embodiment-specific
structure in an image-aligned format. This conditioning interface remains consistent across hu-
man pre-training, human–robot mid-training, and optional post-training. We further introduce
Xp-Teleop, a humanoid teleoperation benchmark for evaluating long-horizon prediction during
locomotion and dexterous manipulation.
We evaluate Ropedia W0 on five cross-embodiment benchmarks (DreamDojo, Ψ0, ω-HOME,
Xp-Teleop, and AgiBot), alongside held-out human egocentric video. Results demonstrate strong
predictive performance across embodiments and post-training budgets, including zero-shot
transfer and data-efficient adaptation. Scaling analyses show that increasing the amount of
human pre-training data improves both prediction on human videos and transfer to robots.
Ablations establish the complementary roles of camera and skeleton conditioning, while dis-
tillation enables real-time, minute-long rollouts and live VR interaction across embodiments.
Downstream experiments further demonstrate accurate policy ranking, improved trajectory
planning, and more robust action learning in simulation and on real robots.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce Ropedia W0, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the first unified egocentric
world model for cross-embodiment loco-manipulation. A shared explicit kinematic represen-
tation of camera poses and projected skeletons enables scalable learning from human and
robot videos.
• We design a human-in-the-loop pipeline that unifies collection, annotation, and quality
checks, including extensive manual robot-keypoint annotation and correction of inaccurate
FK projections. Consequently, we build Xp-Data, comprising over 10k hours of human data
and 3k hours of robot data in a shared schema.
• Extensive experiments show that Ropedia W0 achieves state-of-the-art cross-embodiment
prediction, including on our long-horizon loco-manipulation Xp-Teleop benchmark. We
further show that Ropedia W0’s performance scales with human data and demonstrate
applications in real-time interaction, policy evaluation, model-based planning, and world–
action modeling.
2. Related Work
Robotic video world models. Early world models learned compact latent dynamics for
control [42, 43]; large video generators yield interactive worlds [14, 88, 89, 75, 24, 74] and
ego-motion-controlled driving simulators [47, 98, 35]. Distillation and self-rollout training
convert video generators into causal few-step models [130, 50, 26, 72], augmented with long-
horizon memory [45, 100]. Robotic world models learn video dynamics from robot trajec-
tories [124, 142, 55, 67, 41, 139, 122, 44], often adapting a video foundation model [3, 108].
Numerical action tokens require embodiment-specific encoding [111, 41]; latent actions inferred
from video [14, 128, 36, 38, 34] make unlabeled footage usable, but may entangle actions with
other visual changes [133]; spatially aligned renderings provide explicit geometric condition-
ing [55, 122, 126, 115, 119]. Cross-embodiment models use embodiment-specific action heads,
3


projections, or adaptation [111, 34, 1], a single rendered interface [115, 119], or factorized human-
to-robot recomposition [22]. The last, AnyWorld [22], recomposes human interactions into robot
experiences but conditions on source human skeletons rather than target-robot kinematics and is
evaluated mainly on manipulation, leaving sustained loco-manipulation untested. Ego-motion
is also studied in navigation and driving [8, 47] and in egocentric predictors conditioned on or
jointly modeling full-body pose [7, 87, 121]. Ropedia W0 uses an explicit kinematic interface to
unify loco-manipulation across humans, gripper robots, and dexterous humanoids.
Human and robot datasets. Large egocentric human collections cover daily and skilled
activity [39, 27, 40]; pose-annotated collections add body or hand pose [82, 78, 46, 92], and
portable rigs bring hand pose into everyday scenes [109, 76]. Aggregated and in-the-wild
demonstrations [86, 58, 107, 30, 118] and large teleoperation sets [15] provide robot actions,
calibrated views and task captions in varying combinations, with substantial coverage of tabletop
arm and gripper manipulation; humanoid sets include locomotion or dexterous hands [138,
101, 94, 117, 65]. Evaluation data include held-out robot video clips [142, 34, 119], multimodal
world-model benchmarks [1], and benchmarks measuring visual quality, physical consistency
or embodied utility [132, 84]. Xp-Data unifies annotations for public human and robot data
and self-collected egocentric video; Xp-Teleop benchmarks long-horizon dexterous humanoid
loco-manipulation with robot-native kinematics.
Policy evaluation, planning, and learning. Policy evaluation extends real-to-sim proto-
cols [62] to video world models through recorded-action rollouts [142, 64, 119] or closed-loop
policy interaction [95, 41, 103, 102]. For planning, world models score or refine action proposals
using predicted outcomes [93, 28, 53, 34], in pixel or learned latent spaces [140, 5], and support
reinforcement fine-tuning and synthetic data generation [143, 56, 61, 120, 54]. Vision-language-
action models map observations and language to actions using pretrained vision-language
models [13, 59, 12, 52, 11], increasingly trained with human video [128, 16, 15, 123, 80, 57, 117],
whereas world–action models couple future video or latent-state prediction with action learn-
ing [21, 141, 48, 111, 67, 127, 1, 9, 81, 65, 112, 131]. Ropedia W0 supports these applications in
Sec. 6; for world–action modeling, we transfer its pretrained video backbone to initialize such a
model rather than adding a dedicated action head.
3. Data
Learning cross-embodiment loco-manipulation requires data that captures both viewpoint
changes and manipulator motion under a shared annotation scheme.
However, existing
datasets [39, 40, 46, 113, 27, 2, 78, 31, 29, 60, 77, 37, 92, 97, 15, 58, 30, 135, 101, 11, 33, 10, 63,
85, 49, 105, 138] fall short of this requirement: most lack essential geometric information such as
camera poses, and their hand annotations are often noisy or inaccurate, making them insufficient
for training a general-purpose world model.
To address this gap, our human-in-the-loop data pipeline unifies collection, multimodal
annotation, and quality checks (Fig. 2). We re-annotate public human and robot recordings,
with substantial manual robot-keypoint annotation and correction, and collect over 6,000 hours
of in-house multi-view video with a four-fisheye-camera rig. This yields Xp-Data, over 10k
human and 3k robot training hours with synchronized RGB, camera trajectories, manipulator
skeletons, and captions; wrist poses and wide-FOV skeletons are included where available. Our
Xp-Teleop benchmark provides about one held-out hour of long-horizon dexterous humanoid
loco-manipulation, recorded in the same schema with an off-the-shelf rig [104] (Sec. A.2).

Data sources. Xp-Data comprises human and robot data. The human collection combines
14 public egocentric datasets [39, 40, 46, 113, 27, 2, 78, 31, 29, 60, 77, 37, 92, 97] with over 6,000
hours of in-house recordings captured using a device equipped with four fisheye cameras,
covering diverse daily activities and object interactions. The robot collection draws from 13
public datasets [15, 58, 30, 135, 101, 11, 33, 10, 63, 85, 49, 105, 138] spanning parallel grippers
and dexterous hands. Together, these collections provide complementary coverage of activities,
camera motion, and manipulator kinematics.
Video filtering. We filter the raw recordings for visual quality and task relevance before
annotation. A vision-language model screens each segment for severe blur, poor or uneven
illumination, over- and under-exposure, and excessive camera jitter, removing clips unsuitable
for reliable geometric and kinematic annotation. We discard segments with neither camera
motion nor visible hands.
Multimodal annotation. We annotate geometry, kinematics, and semantics in a unified format.
For geometry, we obtain camera intrinsics and metric trajectories from available calibration or
estimate them using our in-house SLAM pipeline or DA3 [69]. Grounding DINO [73] identifies
5


dynamic regions, such as hands and people, for exclusion from camera-trajectory consistency
checks. For human kinematics, we project WiLoR-reconstructed [91] MANO [96] keypoints into
images. Captions describe hands, objects, and interactions at task, subtask, and atomic-action
levels.
Robot keypoint annotation and correction. Direct FK projections often misalign with the
hands and grippers in the public robot recordings we process, and many releases lack reliable
camera calibration. We therefore invest substantial effort in manually annotating image-space
keypoints and correcting inaccurate projections. FK provides initial estimates where joint states
and calibration are available; manual annotations supply or refine the image-aligned robot
skeletons used for training. Wrist poses are derived through FK where available.
Quality checks. We verify annotation completeness, correctness, and cross-modal consistency.
Camera trajectories are checked for depth-reprojection consistency and temporal smoothness,
following [110]. Human hand annotations are checked both geometrically and semantically:
projected 3D keypoints are compared against independent 2D detections from MediaPipe [79]
and a fine-tuned MMPose [83] model, while a vision-language model flags false detections and
hands belonging to other people. Robot keypoints are cross-checked by two detectors fine-tuned
on our manually annotated robot-hand data, which recover missed hands and remove duplicate
or misaligned detections. Qwen [6] verifies Gemini [25] captions, with human review resolving
discrepancies. Human spot checks across modalities identify systematic annotation errors.
Training clip construction. From subtasks that pass quality checks, we build 81-frame clips at
640 × 480 (with adjusted intrinsics), each uniformly resampled from a contiguous source interval.
A language model [25] merges the keyframe captions within a window into one window-level
caption. Each clip provides synchronized RGB, projected skeletons, camera parameters, and a
caption (a default caption is used for the small fraction of robot windows that lack a generated
one), plus wide-FOV skeletons and wrist poses where available, in a format shared across
human and robot data.
The overall curation funnel is summarized in Fig. S1 (Appendix A.1). Fig. 3 summarizes the
composition of Xp-Data: the most frequent caption terms, the distribution of environments
and tasks for the robot and human portions, and representative unified skeleton annotations
across human hands, dexterous robot hands, and parallel grippers. Together they show that
the corpus spans diverse scenes and activities while sharing a single annotation format across
embodiments.
4. Model
Ropedia W0 combines a unified kinematic control interface with a video diffusion model
for cross-embodiment egocentric loco-manipulation prediction (Fig. 4). The interface jointly
captures viewpoint changes and manipulation through camera poses and projected skele-
tons, allowing human and robot motion to share a common visual format while preserving
embodiment-specific geometry. We first formulate the prediction task and introduce a shared
representation of camera and manipulator motion (Sec. 4.1). We then describe the conditioning
architecture (Sec. 4.2), training recipe (Sec. 4.3), and distillation (Sec. 4.4).

4.2. Architecture: Kinematics-Conditioned World Model
Ropedia W0 adopts a latent video diffusion transformer conditioned on the initial observation,
text, and kinematic controls. We instantiate it at two scales: a dense 5B model and an A14B
mixture-of-experts (MoE) model based on Wan2.2-I2V-A14B [108] (approximately 27B total
and 14B active parameters). Spatially aligned conditions are added to the video tokens, while
additional context is incorporated through cross-attention.
Spatially aligned conditioning. We encode camera-ray maps and skeleton renderings into
condition latents zcam and zskel, aligned with the video latents in space and time. Separate
projections map these latents to the transformer’s token dimension:
h(0) = Pvid(zτ) + Pcam(zcam) + Pskel(zskel),
(4)
where zτ denotes the video latents at noise level τ, and Pvid is the pretrained video projection.
The camera and skeleton projections are initialized to zero, preserving the backbone’s behavior
at initialization. Text embeddings ctxt enter through the backbone’s cross-attention layers.
Additional kinematic context. Wrist-pose renderings follow the same aligned injection
scheme, adding gwristPwrist(zwrist) to h(0), where gwrist ∈{0, 1} indicates annotation availability.
Wide-FOV skeletons extend beyond the target view and lack direct pixelwise correspondence
with the video. Their encoded features therefore enter through an additional cross-attention
branch. We initialize the wrist projection and the wide-FOV branch’s output projection to zero,
and disable each optional branch when its annotations are unavailable.
4.3. Training: From Human Experience to Cross-Embodiment
We train Ropedia W0 in two stages: human pre-training and human–robot mid-training. Both
stages share the camera-and-skeleton conditioning interface, enabling the model to transfer
motion-conditioned prediction learned from human videos to diverse robot embodiments.
Human pre-training. We first train on large-scale egocentric human videos conditioned on
text, camera trajectories, and projected hand skeletons. This stage learns the visual consequences
of camera and manipulator motion across diverse environments and interactions, establishing a
foundation for cross-embodiment transfer.
Human–robot mid-training. We continue training on a balanced mixture of human and
robot data, sampling each with equal probability. Robot motion is expressed through the same
conditioning interface using embodiment-native kinematics. This stage introduces robot-specific
appearance, manipulator geometry, and interaction patterns while maintaining exposure to
diverse human experience. We also incorporate wrist-pose and wide-FOV skeleton conditions
during this stage, using availability gates to accommodate samples with different annotation
coverage.

5. Xp-Teleop
Xp-Teleop is a humanoid teleoperation benchmark for evaluating long-horizon prediction
during locomotion and dexterous manipulation. It comprises 27 long-horizon takes (about
one hour of egocentric video at 30 fps) recorded on a Unitree G1 humanoid with two six-DoF
dexterous hands through a whole-body teleoperation rig assembled from off-the-shelf hardware
and Unitree’s open-source framework [104]. Two operators drive locomotion and manipulation
concurrently, so walking and dexterous interaction occur within the same take. Every take is
recorded with synchronized RGB-D, camera poses, projected skeletons, robot states, language,
tactile, and head-IMU signals in the same schema as Xp-Data, and is divided into 543 human-
annotated atomic segments spanning 30 skill classes (median 18 segments per take). We keep
failed grasps and recoveries so that the benchmark also probes whether a world model can
simulate off-nominal outcomes, not only successful executions.

coverage and available modalities. Full rig, control, recording, and comparison details are

6. Experiments
6.1. Setup
Benchmarks.
We evaluate action-conditioned video prediction on five cross-embodiment
benchmarks spanning distinct embodiments and data sources: (i) the DreamDojo bench-
marks [34], three Fourier GR-1 robot evaluation sets from DreamDojo: In-lab (100 clips), EgoDex
(65 clips), and DreamDojo-HV (68 clips); (ii) Ψ0 [117], 100 evaluation windows of dexterous hu-
manoid manipulation recorded with the Ψ0 vision-language-action (VLA) system (50 seen-task,
50 unseen-task); (iii) ω-HOME [65], 100 evaluation windows of household loco-manipulation
on a Unitree G1 humanoid with dexterous five-finger hands across 24 tasks (50 seen, 50 from
five held-out tasks); (iv) Xp-Teleop (Sec. 5), 100 evaluation windows of long-horizon dexterous
humanoid teleoperation, split by scene (50 windows from held-out takes of training scenes, 50
from unseen scenes); and (v) AgiBot [15], 50 held-out windows of gripper-based manipulation,
where all compared models include AgiBot data in training. In addition, we report zero-shot
prediction on 200 held-out human egocentric clips (EgoSuite and OpenAoE; Sec. 6.2).
Metrics.
We report visual quality with PSNR, SSIM [116], and LPIPS [134]. For benchmarks
with camera motion we additionally evaluate camera-trajectory accuracy on poses recovered
from the generated video, namely absolute trajectory error (ATE), relative pose error (RPE),
and relative rotation error (RRE), as well as hand-following accuracy via keypoint PCK@0.1
(PCKm on matched hands, with detection rate Det). Where noted, we also report video-quality
metrics (VBench [51] subject/background consistency, dynamic degree, and aesthetic quality)
and FVD [106]. Full metric definitions and the per-benchmark evaluation protocol are given in
Sec. C.1.
11


Post-training protocol.
For each benchmark, we evaluate three adaptation settings: no post-
training (direct transfer), few-shot post-training using a small number of episodes per training
task, and full-data post-training using the entire training split. Throughout, zero-shot (equiva-
lently no post-training / direct transfer) means the model is evaluated on a target dataset it was
never trained on. The target embodiment may still appear in the multi-embodiment mid-training
mixture through other datasets, but the target evaluation dataset itself is never used for training.
“Seen” vs. “unseen” splits within a benchmark refer to seen vs. held-out tasks/scenes of that
benchmark, not to the embodiment. We evaluate both training stages of Ropedia W0: pretrain
rows are initialized from the human-only pre-training stage, and midtrain rows from the subse-
quent mid-training stage that mixes human and multi-embodiment robot data. Checkpoints are
always selected on a held-out validation split, never on the benchmark itself.
Baselines.
We compare against generalist robot world models with different action interfaces:
DreamDojo (2B and 14B), which conditions on continuous latent actions pretrained from large-
scale human video, and Cosmos3 (Nano, 16B; Super, 64B), which treats action as a first-class
modality with per-domain action encoders. On the egocentric human-video and AgiBot bench-
marks we additionally report two recent action-conditioned world models where available,
OSCAR [119] and RynnWorld-Teleop [136]. For data-efficiency comparisons, we additionally
post-train the same architecture as ours initialized from the public video-generation weights
(“official base”), isolating the contribution of our pre-training. All baselines are post-trained
with their official recipes.
6.2. Benchmark Results
Human egocentric video. We evaluate on 200 clips from EgoSuite [68] and OpenAoE [66], two
datasets unseen during training by both Ropedia W0 and all compared methods. Ropedia W0
achieves the lowest camera-trajectory errors, the highest hand-following accuracy, and the best
visual quality (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) (Table 2). These results demonstrate strong generalization to
unseen human egocentric datasets, in both motion following and visual quality.
DreamDojo benchmark. Following DreamDojo’s protocol, we post-train on its GR-1 teleop-
eration data and evaluate on three test sets: In-lab, EgoDex, and DreamDojo-HV. Ropedia W0
outperforms DreamDojo across all evaluated post-training budgets (1%–100%), with the largest
gains in the low-data regime, demonstrating data-efficient transfer to the target embodiment
(Table 3).
Ψ0 and ω-HOME. On these dexterous-humanoid benchmarks (Table 4; breakdowns in Ta-
ble S3), Ropedia W0 leads across all adaptation settings. On both, an A14B midtrain variant
already matches or exceeds every baseline’s full-data result with little or no post-training. With-
out post-training, the A14B midtrain variant also yields the smallest seen–unseen gap on both
benchmarks (0.06/0.69 dB on Ψ0/ω-HOME); this narrowing does not persist after few-shot or
full-data post-training (Table S3). Qualitative comparisons on ω-HOME (Figs. S13 and S14)
illustrate the generated hand motion and object interactions against ground-truth frames.
Xp-Teleop. On long-horizon loco-manipulation split into seen and unseen scenes, A14B
midtrain leads all three settings over the best baseline, and its zero-shot result exceeds every
baseline’s full-data result (Table 4). Post-training lifts unseen scenes in step with seen ones
(Table S3), consistent with adaptation rather than pure memorization. Ropedia W0 also leads
on long-horizon autoregressive rollouts (≈32 s on ω-HOME and ≈30 s on Xp-Teleop), with full
tables in the appendix (Tables S4 and S5).

6.4. Downstream Applications
We study four downstream uses of Ropedia W0, spanning simulation and real robots: policy
evaluation, model-based planning, world–action modeling, and live teleoperation.
Policy evaluation. Used as a proxy environment, Ropedia W0 evaluates policy rollouts
using a task-progress rubric following WorldEval [64] and SIMPLER [62]. Its scores closely
match ground-truth evaluations, achieving Pearson correlations of r = 0.984 across five π0.5 [52]
checkpoints in RoboTwin 2.0 [23] and r = 0.996 across three Ψ0 checkpoints and an expert-
teleoperation reference on a real Unitree G1 loco-manipulation task. Both evaluations yield
MMRV = 0 and preserve the ground-truth checkpoint rankings (Fig. 9(a,b)), supporting the use
of Ropedia W0 for policy comparison and checkpoint selection.

World–action modeling. A world–action model jointly predicts future frames and the actions
that produce them, so a stronger world model should be a better initialization. We test this by
initializing the video backbone of FastWAM [131] from our pretrained 5B world model and
leaving its action head and training recipe unchanged. This improves action learning: trained on
LIBERO [71] and evaluated directly on both LIBERO and the held-out LIBERO-Plus [32], it raises
LIBERO-Plus success from 50.06% to 56.82% and improves every few-shot setting (Table 6),
and on a real G1 loco-manipulation task it reaches 56.7% task progress vs. 33.3% for FastWAM,
competitive with the Ψ0 VLA policy itself (50.0%; Fig. 10).
Live teleoperation. A Quest 3 headset maps the user’s head and hand motion to camera
poses and skeletons, and the distilled 5B student generates egocentric video in real time, letting
users interactively drive viewpoint and manipulation across embodiments (Fig. 11; videos in
the supplement).

7. Conclusion
We presented Ropedia W0, a unified egocentric world model for loco-manipulation across
humans and robots with grippers or dexterous hands. Its explicit kinematic representation com-
bines camera poses for viewpoint motion with projected skeletons for manipulation, preserving
embodiment-native geometry within a shared visual format. This interface enables learning
from human and robot data and remains consistent from pre-training to post-training. Trained
on Xp-Data with 10k hours of human video and 3k hours of robot data, Ropedia W0 demon-
strates strong zero-shot generalization, data-efficient adaptation, and competitive prediction
across diverse benchmarks, including our new Xp-Teleop. Our scaling analysis further shows
that increasing human-video pre-training improves prediction on held-out human video and
transfer to robot benchmarks, highlighting human experience as a scalable source of super-
vision for cross-embodiment world modeling. Beyond video prediction, the model supports
policy evaluation, model-based planning, and world–action modeling, while distillation enables
real-time interactive simulation.

A.2. Xp-Teleop Collection and Benchmark Details
The benchmark overview (Fig. 5) and the dataset comparison (Table 1) appear in the main
text (Sec. 5); here we provide the full rig, control, recording, and comparison details.
Rig.
A Unitree G1 humanoid with 29 actuated degrees of freedom is equipped with two
Inspire RH56DFX dexterous hands, each with six actuated degrees of freedom. An Azure Kinect
DK mounted on a custom 3D-printed helmet rigidly attached to the robot’s head records the
egocentric RGB-D view. Because the G1 has no actuated neck joints, the camera moves only
with the waist and mobile base, matching the motion represented by our camera-pose control
channel (Sec. 4).
Control.
Two operators control the robot concurrently, allowing locomotion and manipulation
to occur within the same take. The manipulation operator wears a Meta Quest 3, whose
controller-free hand tracking provides targets to a Pinocchio-based inverse-kinematics solver
for the two 7-DoF arms and to a dexterous-hand retargeting module. The locomotion operator
independently commands the robot’s base velocity using Unitree’s R3 remote.
Recording.
Each take records the robot and hand joint states, commanded targets, R3 com-
mands, headset tracking, and Kinect stream on a common clock. From these signals, we derive
the two action channels described in Sec. 4: camera poses and hand skeletons. The hand skele-
tons are computed through forward kinematics and projected into the image using the calibrated
helmet-to-camera extrinsics. We retain every take initiated by the operators, including failed
grasps and subsequent recoveries, because Xp-Teleop is intended to evaluate whether a world
model can simulate off-nominal outcomes as well as successful executions.
Dataset composition and annotation granularity.
Xp-Teleop contains 27 long-horizon teleop-
eration takes, totaling approximately one hour of egocentric video recorded at 30 fps. The takes
are divided into 543 human-annotated atomic segments, with each take containing between 8
and 42 segments (median 18). This annotation is substantially finer-grained than the common
convention of assigning a single instruction to an entire episode. The resulting segments cover
30 skill classes. The segment captions also identify the manipulated objects and describe the
temporal progression of each task, providing language supervision aligned with the recorded
observations and actions.
Scope of the dataset comparison.
Table 1 compares publicly described dexterous-humanoid
teleoperation datasets in terms of robot capabilities, task horizon, and available recording
modalities. We divide the datasets into two groups according to how the lower body is used
during data collection. In the tabletop-teleoperation group, the robot’s legs remain locked even
if the release provides base commands or whole-body state. In the loco-manipulation group,
locomotion is actively commanded and can occur within the same episode as manipulation.
Task-horizon metrics.
The three task-horizon columns report episode-level means over the
analyzed portion of each dataset. Duration is the wall-clock length of one episode, computed
as the number of recorded frames divided by the dataset’s frame rate (30 Hz for all datasets
except GR00T-Teleop, 20 Hz). Frame counts are taken from each dataset’s own metadata. For
our dataset we report the released task windows, not the raw recordings. RoboMIND does not
document its frame rate; we assume 30 Hz.
Loco. Range measures how much space an episode covers. For each episode we take the 3-D
32


position trajectory of the robot base (or, when only a camera trajectory is available, the head
camera), compute its axis-aligned bounding box, and report the box diagonal in metres. We use
the bounding-box diagonal rather than integrated path length, because path length at 30 Hz is
dominated by body sway and pose-estimation jitter (on our data it inflates the value by roughly
7×).
Pose sources. Ours: visual-inertial SLAM (metric). Humanoid-Everyday: on-board odometry.
UnifoLM-WBT (new schema): the robot state estimator’s base pose. All other datasets provide
no metric base pose; we run DA3-Streaming with its metric configuration on the egocentric
video (10 fps, 504 px input) to obtain camera poses. Because monocular metric SLAM has a
systematic scale error, we calibrate it once against ground truth: on the 879 Humanoid-Everyday
locomotion episodes the DA3 box diagonal exceeds the odometry value by a median factor of
1.39 (IQR 1.28–1.55), and on our 27 episodes the median factor against SLAM is 1.03; we divide
all DA3-derived values by the pooled median 1.39. After calibration the estimator reproduces
ground truth within 10% on both datasets (ours 1.48 vs 1.61 m; Humanoid-Everyday 0.84 vs
0.82 m). For AgiBot-Beta we estimate on a length-stratified sample of 30 episodes. Unitree G1
Dex1/Dex3, PH2D, and RoboMIND Tien Kung are reported as 0.
Subtasks counts how many sequential sub-goals an episode’s task instruction specifies. We
apply one rule to every dataset: the instruction is lower-cased and split into clauses at commas,
semicolons, periods and the connectives "and", "then", "until", "upon", "after"; a clause counts
as one subtask if its first or second content word is an action verb from a fixed list (pick, place,
put, take, grasp, carry, walk, go, enter, turn, open, close, push, pull, pour, wipe, fold, locate, find,
hang, press, insert, ...). Locomotion clauses count the same as manipulation clauses. Every
instruction counts at least 1. Instruction sources: the per-episode task string for LeRobot datasets,
the HDF5 description attribute for PH2D and RoboMIND, the task name for ω-HOME, and the
human-written task instruction of each take for our dataset (one 19-step instruction excluded
as an outlier). AgiBot-Beta ships no instruction text. Because subtask boundaries follow the
wording and granularity of each release’s instructions, this count is an annotation-level measure
of task horizon rather than a platform-independent action count.
Modality criteria.
The modality columns describe information available in the analyzed
dataset release. For Kpts., we also count image-space keypoints that can be reconstructed from
released joint states and camera calibration using a publicly available URDF. RGB@fps reports
the resolution and frame rate of the principal egocentric RGB stream. Dpt. requires a usable
released depth stream; placeholder or zero-filled channels do not qualify. IMU requires explicit
inertial-sensor measurements. Estimated root poses, torso poses, or camera trajectories alone
are not counted as IMU data. Tac. denotes released tactile measurements.
WB joints requires recorded joint states covering both the upper and lower body, including
the legs. Arm-only or hand-only states, estimated root or torso poses, and commanded base
velocities do not satisfy this criterion. This column describes the coverage of the recorded state
and does not require the leg joints to exhibit substantial motion during the released episodes.
Consequently, a dataset may provide whole-body joint states while still belonging to the tabletop
group.
Cal. requires sufficient camera calibration to relate robot kinematics to the image observations,
including both camera intrinsics and the relevant extrinsics. Camera trajectories or extrinsics
provided without intrinsics do not satisfy this criterion.
Kpts. denotes temporally aligned image-space keypoints, either provided directly or obtain-
able by projecting the relevant robot kinematics using released joint states, sufficient camera
33


calibration, and a publicly available URDF.
Dataset-specific qualifications.
For Unitree Dex1, whole-body joint states are available only in
the v3 release; v2 provides arm and gripper states but not the corresponding lower-body joint
states. Although v3 supports base commands, the observed variation of its leg joints is below
0.03 rad in the analyzed recordings. We therefore treat its measured locomotion range as zero
and place it in the tabletop group.
Ψ0 supports locomotion, but the analyzed release does not provide leg-joint states. Its released
depth channel is zero-filled and is therefore not counted as usable depth. RoboMIND entries
refer specifically to its Xsens dexterous-hand subset. This subset includes depth observations but
does not provide hand-joint states. Humanoid Everyday task-horizon statistics are computed
from its loco-manipulation subset, and its tactile measurements are available only for the
G1/Dex3 embodiment. The analyzed ω-HOME files do not contain a depth stream.
B. Model and Implementation Details
This section describes the video backbones, distillation and interactive inference, and the
optional wide-FOV skeleton-conditioning branch. Figures and measurements are grouped with
the component they illustrate.
B.1. Backbone and Conditioning
Ropedia W0 predicts future egocentric video from an initial visual observation and factorized
camera and manipulator controls (Sec. 4.1). The following details describe the pretrained
backbones used by this shared conditioning interface.
Latent video diffusion backbones.
We instantiate Ropedia W0 at two scales using the Wan2.2
family of pretrained video diffusion models [108]. Wan2.2-TI2V-5B uses a dense diffusion
transformer with approximately 5B parameters. Wan2.2-I2V-A14B uses two experts specialized
for different noise regimes: a high-noise expert and a low-noise expert. Only one expert is active
at each denoising step, yielding approximately 14B active parameters. In our experiments, the
two experts are adapted separately and used in their respective noise regimes during inference.
Both backbones operate on spatiotemporal latents produced by a video VAE and follow
the diffusion transformer architecture [90]. Text embeddings are incorporated through cross-
attention, while sinusoidal timestep embeddings provide adaptive normalization and residual
modulation within each transformer block. We train them with the latent flow-matching
objective [70]: the video VAE encodes each clip into latents, the first temporal latent is retained
as clean visual context, and noise and the loss are applied only to future latents. The camera-ray
and skeleton conditioning branches introduced in Sec. 4.2 augment these backbones through
zero-initialized projections, preserving the pretrained model’s behavior at initialization.
34


B.2. Distillation and Interactive Simulation
Distillation recipe.
We first obtain a 4-step autoregressive generator using Causal Forcing [144,
137], then extend it to long-video streaming using LongLive [125], retaining camera–skeleton
conditioning.
Few-step distillation. An autoregressive diffusion model provides causal initialization. Asym-
metric DMD then trains the causal generator using a frozen bidirectional real-score teacher
(with classifier-free guidance) and an online bidirectional fake-score model. The generator starts
from the causal initialization, while both score networks initialize from the bidirectional camera–
skeleton model. The fake-score model learns a denoising objective on generated samples.
Long-video extension. LongLive adapts the few-step generator for streaming with within-block
bidirectional and cross-block causal attention. A bounded KV cache retains a local window and
an attention sink.

C. Evaluation Protocols
We specify the human-video evaluation procedure and the protocol used for the scaling
curves. Supplementary benchmark results are collected in Sec. D.
C.1. Human Egocentric Video Evaluation
The pooled 200-clip results (100 per dataset from EgoSuite [68] and OpenAoE [66]) are reported
in the main text (Table 2). Here we specify the evaluation-set composition, protocol, and metric
definitions; the per-dataset breakdown is reported in Sec. D.1.
Evaluation set.
We evaluate on 100 clips per dataset (200 total), sampled across high, medium,
and low camera-motion strata.
Evaluation protocol.
Clips contain 81 frames at 640 × 480 and 16 fps. Methods receive the
same initial frame and, when supported, the same caption, with seed 0. Camera and hand
conditions derive from the same camera annotations and projected hand keypoints, converted
to each method’s native interface. Each baseline is run under its native conditioning interface:
DreamDojo with latent actions, Cosmos3 with joint camera-and-hand conditioning.
Metrics.
ATE is absolute trajectory error after rigid SE(3) alignment without scale fitting;
RPE measures stepwise translation error, and RRE measures rotation error relative to the first
frame. Camera poses are estimated from generated videos with DA3 [69]. Hand metrics use
MediaPipe [79] detections at confidence 0.3, matched to projected annotations. PCK@.1 uses a
threshold of 10% of the image diagonal and penalizes missed detections; PCKm@.1 evaluates
matched hands only, and Det is the detection rate. Cons., Dyn, and Aes denote VBench subject
consistency, dynamic degree, and aesthetic quality. FVD16 uses non-overlapping 16-frame
windows, while FVDfull uses the full sequences. FVD is recomputed for each evaluation set
rather than averaged across datasets.
In the human evaluation tables, ATE and RPE are reported in centimeters, RRE in degrees,
HandL2 in pixels, and PSNR in dB. PCK, PCKm, Det, Cons., Dyn, and Aes are reported as
percentages; SSIM, LPIPS, and FVD have no physical units.
C.2. Scaling Protocol
The scaling figures appear in the main text: held-out human video at hand-detection confi-
dence 0.3 (Fig. 6), human-to-robot transfer to Ψ0/Xp-Teleop/ω-HOME (Fig. 7), and the Dream-
Dojo cross-evaluation sets (Fig. 8). We detail the shared protocol here. Each point is a checkpoint
taken at the indicated amount of human-video pre-training data (hours of video processed), with
the data mixture held fixed. Because training stays within a single epoch, the hours processed at
each point equal the amount of unique human data seen, so these curves measure data scaling
rather than repeated exposure. For held-out human video the checkpoint is evaluated zero-shot,
whereas for the DreamDojo and transfer benchmarks it is post-trained on the target with the
same recipe before evaluation.
For transfer scaling, post-training uses one clip for 50 steps on Ψ0 and 144 clips for five epochs
on Xp-Teleop.

F. Limitations and Future Work
1. The distilled student supports real-time, minute-long rollouts (Sec. B.2); stronger mem-
ory mechanisms or periodic re-grounding are promising directions for extending stable
interactive simulation to longer horizons.
2. We study up to a 14B-active model and scale the amount of human-video pre-training
data while holding the data mixture fixed; prediction quality improves monotonically with
the amount of data (Sec. C.2), suggesting that scaling data and model size further, and
broadening embodiment and task coverage, are promising directions toward more capable
and reliable embodied world models.
3. Our kinematic interface captures camera and manipulator motion but not physical contact
or object dynamics; coupling the world model with simulation-ready, physically grounded
3D assets and scenes [18–20] is a promising direction toward modeling the underlying
dynamics rather than only their visual consequences.
