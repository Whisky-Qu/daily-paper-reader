Learning humanoid loco-manipulation from human demonstrations rests on observing how locomotion, posture,
and object interaction evolve together as tasks unfold. Scaling collection across diverse environments, in turn, favors
lightweight, portable capture systems. Yet, existing datasets provide only partial views of this coordination. Thus,
we introduce EgoExplorer, a 100-hour vision-motion-touch observatory of human loco-manipulation built with a
wearable system. With an emphasis on professional activities, its continuous recordings align egocentric video with
reconstructed whole-body motion, fine-grained finger articulation, and tactile observations, accompanied by step-
level descriptions. To achieve this coverage with sparse body sensing, we develop the Calibrate-Complete-Constrain
pipeline. Concretely, reusable calibration fixes participant geometry and sensor attachments before motion inference
to disentangle mounting offsets from articulation. A temporal prior then completes unobserved joint motion, while
layered constraints promote measurement agreement, temporal continuity, and geometric consistency, properties
that motion plausibility alone does not guarantee. Against tracker-fitted references, the method reduces body-point
error by 71.9% relative to the strongest baseline; matched evaluations also show gains in hand stability and contact
estimation. Preliminary studies explore multimodal generation and conditioning alongside humanoid retargeting
with whole-body control in simulation.

1. Introduction
What a humanoid needs to reach, grasp, or carry shapes where it moves and how it positions its body
(Shi et al., 2026). Learning such loco-manipulation skills calls for demonstrations spanning diverse
environments, objects, and task sequences. Robot teleoperation provides such data, but dedicated
hardware, on-site deployment, and sustained operator effort constrain its scalability (Punamiya et al.,
2026; Zhu et al., 2026). Human activity offers a rich alternative across everyday and professional
settings, and recent work shows that egocentric demonstrations can improve robot manipulation and
generalization to novel scenarios (Kareer et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026). Realizing
this potential for loco-manipulation hinges on datasets that jointly record visual context, whole-body
and hand motion, and measured contact throughout continuous tasks.
Building such datasets at scale entails balancing environmental coverage with the richness and
fidelity of the observations. Systems built around fixed camera arrays for optical motion capture (Cao
et al., 2026) or multi-view reconstruction (Bhatnagar et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022) can provide
detailed observations, but require each recording space to be instrumented and calibrated (Kim et al.,
2025; Taheri et al., 2020). Portable capture eases deployment, yet existing datasets often offer only
partial views of this coordination. Particularly, egocentric video preserves diverse visual context but
leaves much of the body unobserved (Grauman et al., 2022). Wearable body tracking adds body
motion, often without fine finger articulation or physical contact measurements (DeTone et al., 2026;
Ma et al., 2024). Conversely, hand-focused datasets capture detailed hand motion and, in some cases
touch, but typically omit the accompanying whole-body movement (Hoque et al., 2026; Song et al.,
2025; Zhou et al., 2026). These partial records obscure the coupling central to loco-manipulation
and therefore fall short of capturing its execution as a coherent whole.
To this end, we introduce EgoExplorer, an egocentric dataset that captures the whole-body
coordination underlying human loco-manipulation through portable vision-motion-touch sensing.
As depicted in Fig. 1, it aligns first-person video with reconstructed body motion, glove-measured
finger articulation, and tactile observations. Collected across five environments with an emphasis on
professional activities, the recordings preserve the transitions between approaching targets, adjust-
ing posture, manipulating objects, and relocating. Step-level descriptions link these synchronized
observations to the actions and progression of each task.
To support this multimodal capture, our lightweight wearable rig combines five trackers, instru-
mented gloves, and a head-mounted camera. Recovering whole-body trajectories requires inferring
unobserved joint motion from these sparse device poses. Yet direct fitting is ambiguous: each pose
depends jointly on its attachment transform and the body’s kinematic chain, allowing different
combinations of mounting offsets and joint configurations to explain the same sparse measurements
(Jiang et al., 2022; Ponton et al., 2023). Differently, our C3 pipeline (Calibrate-Complete-Constrain)
disentangles fixed body-sensor geometry from motion through a calibration protocol: prescribed
participant movements are matched to a reference motion library. Using these correspondences, we
refine initial estimates of body shape and scale and fit body-sensor transforms, forming a profile
reusable across tasks with unchanged sensor placement. A temporal prior model then completes
unobserved articulation from the calibrated anchors, yielding an initial whole-body trajectory, i.e.,
the motion proposal. We refine this proposal under layered constraints to promote measurement
agreement and temporal continuity while penalizing self-interpenetration and ground penetration.
Finally, we incorporate hand motion, touch, and expert-guided step annotations and check multimodal
consistency. The resulting dataset comprises 2,715 egocentric recordings totaling 100 hours (Tab. 1).

3. The EgoExplorer Dataset
Egocentric video captures task semantics at scale, yet leaves key signals for loco-manipulation under-
constrained: hand articulation is obscured; whole-body motion, especially lower-body support,
extends beyond the camera’s field of view; and appearance alone cannot reveal tactile contact.
EgoExplorer addresses this asymmetry by pairing a head-mounted camera with sparse wearable
sensing, grounding body and hand motion in sensor measurements and corresponding touch records.
The dataset contains 2,715 recordings (100 h) across ten task templates and five environments,
spanning laboratories, factories, and homes. Following the workflow in Fig. 2, we first introduce the
capture hardware (Sec. 3.1), then describe sensor calibration, body completion, and constrained
refinement of fine-grained body reconstruction (Sec. 3.2), and enrich the episodes with expert-guided
task boundaries and annotations (Sec. 3.3). Sec. 4 offers a glimpse of the dataset.

3.1. Capture Hardware
Requirements. Fine-grained loco-manipulation capture requires accurate measurements of body
motion, hand articulation, and contact in a lightweight, portable system. Studio motion capture
(Cao et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Taheri et al., 2020) provides dense measurements but confines
collection to a calibrated multi-camera volume, whereas readily deployable video-only capture (Shen
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) must infer occluded motion and visually ambiguous contact. We
therefore complement sparse tracking at key body joints with direct finger sensing and optional touch
for whole-body reconstruction and fine-grained interaction annotation.
Hardware. Five VIVE Ultimate Trackers locate the head, hands, and ankles (App. A), using
onboard cameras for 6-DoF inside-out tracking without external base stations. Head and hand
trackers establish metric camera and end-effector trajectories, while ankle trackers capture foot
placement and constrain stance and support beyond the head camera’s view. Custom 3D-printed
mounts rigidly couple the head tracker to a GoPro HERO13 with Media Mod and attach the others
to the glove dorsum and lateral ankles, with straps for added fixation. Two interchangeable glove
configurations share this layout: MANUS Metagloves measure finger articulation, while Wuji gloves
additionally record tactile signals.
Deployment. The compact, reusable rig simplifies sensor setup and donning, facilitating collection
across environments. Subject and mount calibration is reused across task recordings as long as
the rig remains mounted. Sec. 3.2 details calibration and whole-body reconstruction from sparse
measurements; App. G covers cost and deployment trade-offs.
3.2. Whole-Body Reconstruction from Sparse Measurement
C3: Calibrate-Complete-Constrain. Whole-body reconstruction calls for inferring the body joint
motion from the rig’s sparse tracker poses. Yet, coupled ambiguities arise: tracker poses depend
jointly on body articulation and rigid attachment to anatomical anchors (i.e., head, wrists, and
ankle joint frames), allowing mounting errors to be absorbed into estimated motion. Even calibrated
anchors leave intermediate joints undetermined. We address these with C3 (Calibrate-Complete-
Constrain; Fig. 2), separating fixed body-sensor geometry from time-varying motion. Protocol-guided
calibration aligns a prescribed movement sequence (i.e., the calibration protocol) with a reference
motion library by phase. Robust fitting estimates body shape, scale, and sensor-to-anchor transforms
from these correspondences once per mounting configuration. Prior-guided motion completion uses
a temporal motion prior, a model for completing unobserved articulation, to generate a motion
proposal: an initial whole-body trajectory from anchors. Layered constraints then jointly refine skeletal
measurement agreement, surface clearance, and ground non-penetration. We fuse glove-measured
finger articulation and touch as direct interaction evidence.

Constraint layer 2: surface clearance. Skeletal agreement does not account for body thickness:
an arm may intersect the torso even when its wrist matches the target. We therefore constrain
the posed SMPL-X surface using the participant’s calibrated shape and scale. For nearby points
sampled from distinct body parts, Lsurf penalizes insufficient oriented clearance along the target
surface normal, distinguishing points outside the surface from those that have crossed it. Aggregating
the largest violations per part pair concentrates corrections in problematic regions, discouraging
self-intersection while respecting participant-specific geometry (App. C.4).
Constraint layer 3: body-ground consistency. An ankle anchor above the floor does not ensure
that the sole, heel, or knee also clears it. We apply Lfloor to all posed body vertices, penalizing
their depth below the estimated ground plane. This constrains the body’s placement relative to the
environment, including regions that approach the floor during crouching or kneeling. The term
penalizes penetration without imposing foot-contact or foot-locking constraints.

3.3. Multimodal integration and annotation
Hand and touch fusion. We directly fuse glove-measured finger geometry to the SMPL-X hands in
wrist-local coordinates. Tactile observations are aligned in time and associated with the corresponding
hand regions. Hand fitting and tactile processing are detailed in App. D.1.
Expert-guided procedural context. We condition off-the-shelf VLM tools (Peng et al., 2026)
on egocentric video, task instructions, and expert-provided skill documents outlining workflow
stages, typical progression, and relevant equipment and operations (App. D.2). The tools produce
subtask segments with start/end times, skill labels, object/tool roles, observed outcomes, and concise
descriptions; uncertain outcomes remain unspecified. Video timestamps align the annotations with
motion and tactile streams. Finally, operators manually review video, reconstructed motion, available
touch, and text for temporal alignment and semantic consistency.

5.1. Annotation Reliability
Whole-body reconstruction. Tab. 2 compares three baseline settings: public pretrained checkpoints
with calibrated sensor-to-anchor conversion, direct use of raw tracker poses, and fine-tuning with
five tracker anchors. EgoExplorer outperforms all three settings across every reported metric,
achieving 18.0 mm body-point error and 20.3 mm at non-tracked points. Its lowest errors extend
beyond the observed anchors to other body points, including elbows and knees, indicating better
recovery of full-body articulation. Visual comparisons are provided in Fig. 21.
Hand motion and touch capture. Tabs. 3 and 4 compare our glove settings with visual and
kinematic inference. In matched-configuration hand tests, our glove setting yields 0.4 mm holding
RMS, versus 4.5 mm for HaWoR (Zhang et al., 2025) and 11.3 mm for HandFlow (Xu et al., 2026),
and achieves 100% kinematic target recovery. For regional contact, our glove setting achieves 0.915
IoU, compared with 0.621 for HACO (Jung and Lee, 2025) and 0.324 for hand-pose-only inference.
Together, these tests support direct sensing of finger articulation and contact.
Step text quality. Expert review of model-generated annotations (Tab. 5) finds factual errors in
11.09% of reviewed segments and unsupported claims in 2.46%. The audit also reports missed- and
extra-step rates of 8.16% and 2.05%, respectively, with boundary corrections in 3.08% of segments.
These residual errors motivate manual verification and correction in our workflow before release.

5.3. Ablation Study
Calibration and layered reconstruction. Tab. 7 shows complementary gains from successively
adding Layers 1–3: body-point error drops from 79.0 to 16.1 mm, crossing pairs from 72.8 to 38.7
per frame, and floor violations from 61.4% to 6.0%, respectively. Full has the lowest Avg. (28.4;
mean of nine reported values), despite higher body-point error and jerk than Layer 1 alone. Replacing
calibrated shape and scale with the mean body raises body-point error from 18.0 to 59.5 mm and floor
violations from 6.0% to 50.9%. Omitting mount calibration during training raises body-point error to
95.0 mm, while omitting it only at test time yields 98.1 mm. These results support fixing body-sensor
geometry before motion recovery. Fig. 7 shows reduced self-intersection and floor penetration.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduces EgoExplorer, an egocentric dataset aligning whole-body motion, hand
articulation, contact, and descriptions for human loco-manipulation. The proposed C3 pipeline
recovers metric body motion from sparse wearable sensing through calibration, temporal completion,
and layered constraints. Evaluations show the benefits of direct sensing, and support this portable
approach to capturing and studying coordinated movement and manipulation.
Limitations. Reconstruction cannot guarantee exact recovery of all joints, while tactile sensing has
variable reliability. Preliminary studies leave benefits for real-world humanoid learning unverified.

Extending Sec. 3.1, the capture system comprises a head-mounted camera, five wearable body
trackers, and articulation gloves; the Wuji configuration additionally provides touch sensing. As in
Sec. 3.1, custom 3D-printed mounts rigidly couple the head tracker to the camera and attach the
remaining trackers to the glove dorsum and the lateral ankles, with straps for added fixation (Fig. 8).

through calibrated device-to-joint transformations. Tab. 8 summarizes the acquisition settings and

measurements without introducing additional observations. The calibration parameters are reused as

C.1. Protocol-guided calibration
Reference protocol. Fig. 9 shows the 17 ordered stages used by Sec. 3.2.1, named as in the figure:
(A) body poses and articulation, (01) neutral stance, (02) head rotations, (03) A-pose, (04) T-pose,
(05) forward reach, (06) elbow flexion, (07) wrist rotation, (08) shallow squat, (09) left leg raise,
(10) right leg raise, and (11) ankle motion; (B) hand articulation, (12) spread/close, (13) fist/release,
(14) pinch/oppose, and (15) finger flexion; and (C) root motion, (16) left/right 180◦turns and
(17) two steps forward, two backward. Stages (01)–(11) cover the body actions listed in the main
text, where neutral stance corresponds to standing and forward reach to forward arm extension. A
VPoser decoder (Pavlakos et al., 2019) fits body landmark targets, followed by local elbow, wrist, head,
and ankle corrections. Finger targets use the MANO-compatible hand model (Romero et al., 2017).
Turning and stepping add prescribed root motion and alternating support. The cached library provides
the reference joint positions 𝐽𝑛,𝑖(𝛽) and rotations 𝐺𝑛,𝑖already defined in Eq. (1); joint positions are
reevaluated at the participant’s shape during fitting.

reference recording link characteristic poses to library phases; the matching path transfers these

D. Multimodal Integration and Annotation
D.1. Hand and touch fusion
Hand alignment and fitting. As in Sec. 3.3, finger articulation is fitted in wrist-local coordinates
after body reconstruction. Glove positions are translated to the measured wrist origin and rotated
into its frame; wrist-local Wuji inputs enter directly. Both layouts retain four landmarks per finger,
including the tip (20 non-wrist landmarks); the 25-node layout discards four extra palm/metacarpal
nodes. A sequence-fixed similarity aligns these points to the calibrated model’s flat hand, using the
median of up to 12 frames with the straightest fingers. Scale is the ratio of model/glove RMS radii
about the wrist; rotation uses SVD with reflection correction (Umeyama, 1991). This hand scale is
separate from body scale 𝑠.
The MANO-compatible SMPL-X hand (Pavlakos et al., 2019; Romero et al., 2017) optimizes its 45
local axis-angle coordinates per side. The fitting objective is:
Lhand = 1000Lpoints + 0.015Ldirections + 0.003Lflat + 0.004Lcontinuity + 0.02Langle.
(14)
The point term averages squared coordinate errors over the 20 wrist-local landmarks in meters. The
direction term averages one minus cosine similarity along the 20 finger bones, with vector norms
27


floored at 10−8. The flat-hand term is mean squared pose deviation from the model’s flat-hand
pose. Continuity averages squared adjacent-frame pose differences and adds twice the mean squared
difference from the preceding window’s final pose; the latter is omitted for the first window. The
angle term averages squared excess of each of the 15 joint-rotation magnitudes above 2.7 radians.
Left/right objectives Eq. (14) are summed. At most 480 frames are sampled over the hands’
overlapping time range and fitted in 90-frame windows with 60 Adam iterations and learning rate
0.04. After accounting for host-time origins, hand rotations are SLERPed onto the body timeline;
endpoint holds outside the fitted span retain coverage flags. Fusion replaces hand-pose arrays while
preserving the fitted body, root, shape, scale, and attachments.
Tactile processing. Wearable tactile response weakens with use and depends on wearing tension
and channel health. Raw amplitude therefore cannot by itself distinguish light contact from reduced
sensitivity or missing data. The three interfaces in Tab. 10 retain native values (L0), baseline-
relative changes (L1), and 17-region contact with separate health/validity (L2). Unresponsive taxels
are excluded from regional support; insufficient support makes a region unavailable, not negative.
Baselines, response normalization, and region mappings accompany each recording. These are contact
indicators, not calibrated force or pressure measurements.
Contact is temporally associated with motion, not used to constrain body IK. To preserve brief
touches on the 30,Hz grid, each region’s output is the logical OR of valid native-rate detections within
the 1/30,s bin centered on the output timestamp. Bins without valid samples are masked. This
release rule differs from the controlled press study’s 30,Hz signal averaging, baselines, thresholds,
and scoring masks (App. F.3). The tactile L0–L2 interfaces are unrelated to the three body-constraint
layers.

the bench, and arranged by use. A balance may be tared before solids are weighed and transferred;

2. Reaction setup. Materials are added to a reaction flask. The example procedure describes adding

and stirring and observation of the apparatus.
3. Monitoring. TLC involves sampling, spotting a plate, and UV inspection. HPLC sample prepara-

repeated.

F.4. Evaluation metrics
Body accuracy (Tabs. 2 and 7). Body-point error averages Euclidean distances over 22 SMPL-X
joints (pelvis through wrists), in mm. Non-tracked error uses the 17 joints outside the head, wrists,
and ankles; the pelvis is included, with its error also reported separately as Root. Root error is pelvis
displacement in the fixed calibrated frame. PA-MPJPE centers each pose, matches its Frobenius norm
to the reference, and applies the SVD rotation with reflection correction (Umeyama, 1991); the
norm-matching scale is not separately optimized by least squares. Velocity error averages Euclidean
differences of joint velocities, computed from adjacent positions divided by their timestamp intervals,
in mm/s. Scores use matched timelines and finite observations, averaged within each recording and
then equally across recordings. No per-frame alignment is applied except for PA-MPJPE.
Hand accuracy, stability, and continuity (Tab. 3). Tip averages the Euclidean discrepancy of the
five held fingertips in wrist-local coordinates after matching hand length to the reference. Hold RMS
(root mean square) measures within-hold scatter: subtract each fingertip’s median held position from
its emitted positions, square the Euclidean distances, average across fingertips and valid hold frames,
40


and take the square root (mm). It is a stability measure, not independent pose accuracy. Missing
is the fraction of frames with no finite pose; Gap p95 is the 95th-percentile duration of contiguous
missing runs pooled across recordings. Success assigns the retargeted median pose to the closest of
ten glove-derived prototypes, using RMS landmark distance with weights 3 for tips, 0 for wrist, and 1
elsewhere. A trial succeeds when the nearest prototype matches its instructed configuration; chance
is 10%.
Sensor agreement and geometry (Tab. 7). Tracker RMS squares Euclidean distances between
predicted and observed device positions, averages over valid device–frame pairs, and takes the square
root (mm); recording-level RMS values are then averaged equally. Predictions use each variant’s
mount model, so this measures sensor agreement. Crossing counts intersecting triangle pairs between
distinct, non-adjacent body parts using the same independent checker and 5 Hz sample times for all
variants; frame counts are averaged within recordings, then across recordings. Floor is the fraction of
frames with any posed vertex more than 5 mm below the shared ground plane.
Temporal quality (Tab. 7). Slide is mean horizontal foot speed during common support intervals,
reported in mm/s. Support is inferred from raw ankles with horizontal speed below 100 mm/s, height
within 30 mm of that ankle’s fifth-percentile height, and duration at least 0.3 s; pairs separated by
more than 0.1 s are excluded. Jerk uses pelvis-relative joint positions in meters while preserving
world orientation. After resampling to 30 Hz without bridging invalid samples or gaps above 0.1 s,
take the third forward difference, multiply by 303, and average its vector norm over joint–frame pairs,
then equally over recordings (m/s3). No smoothing is applied. Avg. is the unweighted arithmetic
mean of the nine displayed values in Tab. 7, including jerk and both data cohorts. This summary
depends on displayed units and has no single physical unit.
Regional contact (Tab. 4). After the shared masks in App. F.3, true positives (TP), false positives
(FP), and false negatives (FN) are pooled over retained frame–region decisions, including non-contact
time. IoU is 𝑇𝑃/(𝑇𝑃+ 𝐹𝑃+ 𝐹𝑁) and F1 is 2𝑇𝑃/(2𝑇𝑃+ 𝐹𝑃+ 𝐹𝑁); task-wise scores are not averaged.
Missing sensing support is excluded, not relabeled as no contact.
F.5. Expert review of text annotations
Language review. Tab. 5 reports expert review of model-generated text annotations prior to manual
correction. The audit covers 487 segments. Factual errors concern incorrect actions, objects, tools, or
outcomes; unsupported claims lack evidence in the recording; missed and extra steps denote omissions
and spurious additions, respectively; unjudgeable cases are reported separately. Boundary error is the
fraction of reviewed segments requiring temporal correction: 15 of 487 segments (3.08%), reported
at the segment level rather than as absolute timing deviation, with no frame-accurate boundary
ground truth available. Unjudgeable segments are those for which the available recording does not
support a judgment. Factual-error, unsupported-claim, extra-step, boundary, and unjudgeable rates
use reviewed segments; missed-step rate uses the reference steps that should be described. These
denominators need not coincide. All rates reflect pre-correction audit findings; human verification and
correction follow model annotation. The automated professional-skill ablation is reported separately
in Tab. 15.

Pilot representation and metrics. Tab. 6 summarizes two preliminary Cosmos3-Edge (NVIDIA,

The separate-model row combines the video, action, and touch scores of three independently trained

Training settings. The 5K configuration uses batch size 64 and AdamW with momentum coeffi-
cients (0.9, 0.95), learning rates 5 × 10−5 for the unfrozen backbone and 5 × 10−4 for new modality
heads. Backbone weight decay is 0.01; new heads use no decay. A 200-step warmup precedes cosine
decay, with gradient-norm clipping at 1 and seed 0. Training predicts clean targets from noisy inputs:
video-latent and action MSE have weight 1, regional contact MSE weight 3, and tactile-intensity
MSE weight 1.5. Invalid contact regions are masked. Separate models activate only their own output
losses, while the joint model activates all three streams.
Temporal split and scoring. The loader splits at session level within each task: sorted session
indices congruent to five modulo ten form validation; all windows from a session remain in the same
partition. The pilot uses 16 observed and 16 generated samples at 15 Hz, distinct from the release
grid. Pixel-space PSNR evaluation uses 256 held-out windows, two samples per window, and 20
denoising steps. Prediction and target latents are decoded with the same clean historical context;
PSNR scores the generated interval against the VAE-decoded target, not directly against uncompressed
camera frames. PSNR is 10 log10(1/MSEpixel) in dB for pixels in [0, 1], averaged over windows and
samples. Action MSE averages the 57 normalized features, excluding padding channels. Contact
predictions threshold the mean of sampled binary states above 0.5; F1 pools TP, FP, and FN over valid
region–time entries as in App. F.4. The current output comparison in Tab. 6 reports higher video
PSNR and contact F1 and lower action MSE for joint generation.

cross-modal conditioning rather than prediction from history alone. Action MSE is computed in the
same normalized feature space as above.

F.9. Humanoid retargeting
Downstream humanoid control motivates preserving metric root translation, per-joint rotations,
and hand pose at a common sampling rate rather than normalizing skeletons. Fig. 20 illustrates
the transfer of a 135 s reaction-setup recording to a Unitree G1, including reconstructed SMPL-X
motion, kinematic retargeting, and physics-based execution. The kinematic stage uses GMR (Araujo
et al., 2025) whole-body inverse kinematics (IK) with position and orientation targets, followed
by root-height correction to prevent sole penetration. We compare the corrected trajectories with
GMR-scaled and offset human targets at nine corresponding body origins: the pelvis, feet, knees,
elbows, and wrists. At temporally matched samples, the mean position errors for the reaction-setup
and reagent-weighing clips are 6.8 and 7.0 cm, respectively; the corresponding wrist errors are 7.5
and 7.8 cm. These residuals include discrepancies between human joint centers and robot link origins.
Position errors average Euclidean distances over the stated origins and matched frames. The 14-body
robot-reference score uses pelvis, torso, and bilateral hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, elbow, and wrist
link origins. Root orientation is the angle of the relative quaternion, averaged over frames; it is not a
yaw-only error.
The physics-based stage follows the ZeroWBC (Yang et al., 2026a) training recipe and uses
a reinforcement-learning tracking policy in MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012). Unlike the original
architecture, our implementation feeds short- and long-horizon targets to an MLP and is trained
only on the two clips. After 15,000 PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) iterations, full-clip evaluation at
50 Hz yields mean 14-body tracking errors of 4.6 and 3.6 cm after aligning torso horizontal position
and yaw with the robot reference, compared with 6.6 and 4.8 cm without alignment. No falls occur.
Global wrist errors are 5.8 and 4.0 cm. Root-position errors are 3.8 and 2.5 cm, and root-orientation
errors are 1.8 and 1.3◦, respectively. Relative to the same human targets and nine body origins used
above, the simulated trajectories have mean errors of 8.5 and 6.8 cm and wrist errors of 9.0 and
8.2 cm, without evaluation-time alignment. Thus, human-to-robot mapping error and robot-reference
tracking error are measured against different references and should not be added.
Error sources. The post-IK floor correction raises the root by 2.7–6.7 cm across the two clips.

G. Discussion on Capture Trade-offs
EgoExplorer combines portable deployment with accurate motion reconstruction and comple-
mentary visual, body, hand, and tactile observations. Its wearable rig supports collection in existing
environments, while participant and mount calibration grounds the recovered motion in measured
geometry. The body, hand, and contact comparisons in Tabs. 2 to 4 support the quality of these
annotations relative to the evaluated baselines. Together, portability, annotation quality, and sensing
coverage motivate this design for capturing human loco-manipulation across tasks and workspaces.

Deployment and sensing. Multi-view RGB reconstruction provides an affordable route to whole-
body capture within a calibrated volume. FreeMoCap supports ordinary USB cameras and recommends
three to four views for general motion capture. Such arrays require overlapping views, synchronization,
and intrinsic/extrinsic calibration; moving the cameras requires renewed calibration. Multi-view
methods can also recover hands, although occlusion and the small image scale of fingers make fine
articulation challenging (Zhang et al., 2021). Direct glove sensing complements body capture and
can additionally provide measured contact. Hybrid systems such as ParaHome combine cameras,
an inertial suit, and gloves (Kim et al., 2025); HOT3D uses optical markers for hands and objects
(Banerjee et al., 2025); Nymeria combines an inertial body suit with wearable Aria devices (Ma
et al., 2024). Our rig avoids external camera-array and base-station setup and reuses its body–sensor
calibration while mounts remain fixed. Its inside-out trackers still depend on suitable visual conditions.
