# DiPLO: Self-distilled Diffusion Planning for Loco-manipulation 

Shafeef Omar 1,4 , Sebastian Sanokowski 1, Elham Daneshmand 2, Dian Yu 1 Daniel Cremers 1, Cordelia Schmid 3,4 ,Majid Khadiv 1

Fig. 1: Real-robot executions of our planner-tracker architecture for humanoid loco-manipulation on the Unitree G1. 

Abstract —Humanoid loco-manipulation is particularly chal-lenging because it requires coordinating locomotion, balance, and object interaction, making it difficult to learn from limited demonstrations or from scratch through Reinforcement Learning (RL). We introduce DiPLO (self-distilled Diffusion Planning for Loco-manipulation), a framework that combines a self-distilled diffusion planner with a low-level whole-body tracking policy. Our planner is pretrained via supervised learning on motions derived from human-to-humanoid retargeting, while the tracker is trained via RL to execute them. DiPLO centers around two complementary post-training stages. First, we introduce Self-Distillation Diffusion Policy Optimisation (SD:DPO) to remove the planner’s dependence on strong task conditioning, which we identify as a crucial ingredient for effective pretraining, as downstream fine-tuning does not recover comparable per-formance when it is omitted.. We then fine-tune this distilled model with Diffusion-Augmented Proximal Policy Optimization (DA:PPO), a maximum-entropy RL method for diffusion models, to improve task success and align generated plans with the frozen tracker’s execution capabilities. Together, these stages enable motion generation through state-history and goal con-ditioning alone, enabling ∼ 99% task success in pick-carry-place tasks, compared to just 45% for the pretrained model. The resulting planner also generates motions for unseen object goals, with further evaluations on kicking and pushing/pulling

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid loco-manipulation requires coordinating loco-motion, balance, and object interaction. Human motion data provides useful supervision for learning these behaviors, but collecting large datasets is costly [1], [2]. Our goal is to learn from a small dataset of retargeted motions while keeping task specification simple: the user provides the desired object position and orientation, and the robot determines how to reach it. This requires a planner that can generate trackable motions for different object goals, including goals beyond those represented in the demonstrated behaviours. We introduce DiPLO (self-distilled Diffusion Planning for Loco-manipulation), which combines a diffusion motion plan-ner with an RL-trained tracker. Starting from 29 retargeted robot–object motion sequences from an existing dataset [3] (which amounts to just 2.7 mins of data), we use trajectory op-timization [4] and augmentation to generate dynamically fea-sible training motions for both components. Such a pretrained planner still relies strongly on kinematic reference conditioning, which limits its ability to generate motions to new goals. To remove this dependence after pretraining, we propose Self-Distillation Diffusion Policy Optimization (SD:DPO). SD:DPO transfers guidance from a reference-conditioned teacher to a student conditioned only on state history and the object goal. The student learns to match the teacher’s denoising predictions at states encountered during environment rollouts with the tracker. These rollouts expose the student to the consequences of planning and tracking er-rors, extending its supervision beyond the contexts represented in the training data. SD:DPO thereby enables high survival without requiring a kinematic reference at deployment. Trackable motions alone, however, do not ensure that the object reaches its goal. We therefore follow self-distillation with planner fine-tuning using DA:PPO [5], a maximum-entropy RL method for diffusion models. With the tracker fixed, DA:PPO improves task completion through closed-loop interaction while encouraging diversity in generated plans. SD:DPO and DA:PPO thus address complementary require-ments: removing dependence on reference conditioning and improving task success. Our main contributions are:

1) Learning from retargeted motion data: Retargeted mo-tions provide supervision for learning whole-body tracking policies [6]–[8], but kinematic retargeting [3], [9] alone does not ensure physically feasible robot–object interaction. DynaRetarget [4] addresses this limitation through sampling-based trajectory optimization (SBTO), which refines a kine-matic reference into a dynamically feasible motion. To scale demonstrations beyond those recorded, HumanoidMim-icGen [10] augments loco-manipulation data by adapting object-centric skill segments of a source demonstration to new object poses. However, it plans kinematically and decouples locomotion from stationary manipulation, so dynamic feasibil-ity is not enforced during generation. In contrast, we repurpose SBTO, originally proposed to refine individual demonstra-tions, for data augmentation: starting from perturbed initial conditions, we exploit the stochasticity of the sampling-based optimizer to synthesize locally diverse, dynamically feasible robot–object motions in which locomotion and manipulation remain coupled. 

2) Diffusion planning for loco-manipulation: Diffusion Policy [11] models multimodal action sequences for robotic manipulation. In humanoid locomotion, however, maintaining balance while accomplishing the task motivates delegating execution to a low-level controller [12]. PDP [13] learns action generation from expert policies, while BeyondMimic [14] uses guided state–action diffusion to generate goal-directed behaviors. An alternative is to separate motion generation from execution: [15] combine a diffusion motion generator with a tracking policy for terrain locomotion. Recent works have shown the success of this separation in loco-manipulation settings [16], [17]. REFINE-DP [16] is closest to our ap-proach in combining diffusion planning with RL fine-tuning for humanoid loco-manipulation. Its planner generates motion commands for separate upper- and lower-body controllers. DiPLO instead jointly plans whole-body and object motion for a single generalist tracking policy. Our post-training procedure also differs. We first use SD:DPO to remove the dependence on kinematic reference conditioning introduced during pretraining on retargeted motions. We then apply maximum-entropy fine-tuning with DA:PPO while keeping the tracker fixed. Finally, our generalization evaluation examines whether the same trained planner can reach different requested object positions and orientations that are absent from the retargeted dataset. Our planner also treats rotational features through diffusion on SO(3) . Such processes have been developed for rotational alignment [18], [19], while DiffDock [20] combines diffusion over translational, rotational, and torsional degrees of freedom. We apply this geometric formulation to humanoid–object motion, evaluate its effect on closed-loop survival, and retain it during planner post-training. 

3) Distillation in Robot Learning: Teacher–student distil-lation allows policies to benefit from information unavailable at deployment [21]. Following such a recipe, OmniH2O [8] distills a controller with privileged observations into a policy using deployable observations and sparse motion commands. Our objective concerns the high-level task specification: re-moving the diffusion planner’s dependence on strong task conditioning (kinematic reference trajectory). The closest methodological precedent is Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) [22], which uses a language model conditioned on additional feedback as a self-teacher and distills its next-token predictions into a student. Inspired by SDPO, we propose SD:DPO for diffusion motion planning. A teacher with access to privileged task-prior conditioning su-pervises the student’s denoising predictions on states encoun-tered during closed-loop environment rollouts with the tracker, enabling reward-free credit assignment through denoising-step matching. This distills the guidance provided by the privileged task prior into a planner conditioned only on the robot’s state history and object goal, allowing the task prior to be removed at deployment. We evaluate both the resulting improvement in survival and the ability of the distilled planner to generate motions for object goals absent from the retargeted dataset. 

4) Fine-tuning of diffusion planners: Diffusion-Policy Pol-icy Optimization (DPPO) [23] enables reinforcement learn-ing for diffusion policies by treating denoising transitions as decisions with tractable likelihoods and applying PPO to the resulting augmented process. REFINE-DP [16] applies diffusion-policy fine-tuning to humanoid loco-manipulation, considering both a fixed controller and alternating updates of the planner and controllers. However, DPPO promotes ex-ploration by truncating the denoising process during training, which can lead to jittery motions. Such behavior is particularly undesirable for real-world humanoid control, where smooth and dynamically consistent motions are essential. Diffusion-Augmented MDPs [5] instead provide amaximum-entropy formulation in which ratios of reverse and forward transition densities yield tractable entropy regularization at each denoising step. We use their PPO instantiation, DA:PPO, to fine-tune the planner after SD:DPO. Our setting combines Euclidean and SO(3) diffusion and evaluates generated motions through a frozen generalist tracker. DA:PPO optimizes task completion after kinematic reference conditioning has been removed, while entropy regularization encourages diversity in the generated plans. We separately evaluate tracker adaptation to distinguish improvements due to planner fine-tuning from those obtained by adapting the tracker. 4

C. Removing Task Conditioning with SD:DPO 

Although pretraining includes examples without reference conditioning through masking, removing the reference sub-stantially reduces closed-loop survival (Section V-B) since planning and tracking errors can lead the system into states poorly represented in the training data. Thus, at such states, the data provides limited guidance on how to continue the motion. To mitigate this problem we propose Self-Distillation Diffusion Policy Optimization (SD:DPO) to supply this super-vision, where a student that receives only state history and the object goal learns to match the task-prior-conditioned teacher at states reached during environment rollout. The student Gϕ receives ct and the null reference token ∅,whereas the teacher G ¯ϕ additionally receives the kinematic reference sIK τ . Both are initialized from the pretrained planner. At each planning step, both generate a chunk. We choose either proposal with probability 1/2 and execute its first m frames with the frozen tracker. The resulting measured states update the history used to generate the next chunk. 7

At every planning step, we store the student’s proposal, its starting context, and the corresponding kinematic reference in a buffer B, regardless of which proposal was executed. Failed rollouts terminate and reset, but all previously collected proposals remain in the buffer, including those associated with failure. The tracker supplies the execution contexts used for learning while the teacher supplies the denoising targets. Let ϕold denote the student weights held fixed during collection. D. Improving Task Completion with DA:PPO 

SD:DPO improves generation without a kinematic refer-ence, but producing trackable motion does not by itself ensure that the object reaches its goal. We therefore fine-tune the distilled planner with DA:PPO. The frozen tracker executes its plans in simulation, and the resulting task rewards guide the planner’s updates. We evaluate our pipeline on humanoid loco-manipulation tasks and organise the evaluations around the design choices. In our experiments, we report the Survival Rate which we define as the fraction of rollouts where the robot neither falls nor drops the object. Additionally we define the Task success as the fraction of survived rollouts whose realized final object pose lies within 15 cm and 30 ◦ of the goal position the motion generator was conditioned on. We also report 

MPKPE , which is the mean per-keypoint position error of the executed robot motion against the generated motion from the diffusion model, and Obj. pos. / Obj. ori. the corresponding object position errors aggregated over the whole trajectory. Both metrics measure the trackability of the generated motions of the robot and object. All simulation results are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) over distinct reference motions. Each motion is rolled out 100 times and averaged computing the error over different motions.

B. Importance of Task Conditioning During Pretraining 

During pretraining, we find that using the IK-retargeted robot and object motion as conditioning for the diffusion planner, which provides a prior on how to solve the task, is important to obtain strong results after pretraining. To ablate this choice, we pretrain seven diffusion models on different task prior choices. The task priors range from using the full IK-retargeted motion down to not using retargeted conditioning at all. Each model is evaluated in closed-loop simulation using the same pretrained tracking policy. As shown in Fig. 4, the strongest performance is obtained when using the IK-retargeted robot and object motion as task conditioning as derived from OmniRetarget dataset [3]. We also directly compare against using the corresponding human pose and object pose from the OMOMO [2] dataset without any retargeting and observe a roughly 9% drop in survival rate. Using sparse end-effector and object motion references (only every 5 frames), we do not observe a significant drop in performance, compared to using dense end-effector and object motion conditioning. Removing the remaining priors lowers the closed-loop survival rate further, to ∼70% with only the object motion, and to ∼45% with no task priors. D. Motion-Level vs. Action-Level Diffusion 

Our pipeline separates motion generation from feedback control: a diffusion planner proposes whole-body and object trajectories, and an RL-trained tracking policy executes them. Alternatively, diffusion models can predict actions directly, either alone [13] or jointly with future states [14], [34]. We train these action-level baselines by behaviour cloning on state–action data collected by tracking the SBTO- and symmetry-augmented motions. Although this dataset includes perturbed states and corrective actions, the learned feedback behaviour depends on the coverage of a fixed dataset. Our planner also learns from a limited motion corpus, but its tracker learns via RL in simulation, where it can learn to respond to disturbances and errors encountered during its own execution. This separation allows us to learn motion structure from the available data while optimizing feedback control through interaction. The learning objectives also favour different behaviours. From a maximum-likelihood perspective, generative learning corresponds to forward-KL fitting, which encourages coverage of the data distribution. Under imperfect approximation, a model may cover multiple action modes while also assigning probability to low-density regions between them [35]. For an action-level model, such samples become joint commands and may disrupt balance or contact. Maximum-entropy RL instead admits a reverse-KL for-mulation with a reward-weighted target distribution [36]. This objective discourages probability mass on low-return behaviours and can favour concentrating on a subset of suc-cessful behaviours. Although this mode-seeking tendency can hinder exploration of alternative behaviours, it favours reliable execution when failures receive low reward. This motivates combining a generative motion planner with an RL-trained tracker: the planner represents motion diversity, while the tracker learns to select actions according to their consequences in the environment. We therefore compare motion-level and action-level diffu-sion using the same underlying motion corpus and evaluation tasks to assess whether separating planning from feedback control improves closed-loop survival and task success. Although action/state-action diffusion models can plan actions over a longer horizon, the generated actions operate open-loop until the next plan arrives, making it ex-tremely challenging for legged robots to stabilise at such low frequencies, compared to having a separate high-frequency low-level tracking policy as we do.

In Table I, we also compare the finetuning capabilities of DA:PPO against a DPPO-style update, which ablates on using DA:PPO without temperature (see Section IV-D). The original DPPO [23] uses additional tricks, such as injecting more diffusion noise for exploration, which we removed because they led to unstable and untrackable behaviour. While the DPPO-style update improves overall metrics compared to the pretrained model, we find that using the temperature in DA:PPO updates improves the survival rate to 100% and significantly improves all other metrics when finetuned under identical settings and hyperparameters. These improvements can be attributed to the maximum entropy RL formulation of DA:PPO which encourages exploration while the DPPO-style objective suffers under severe mode collapse [5].

We evaluate our diffusion motion generator and tracking policy on a 29 -DoF Unitree G1 humanoid performing pick-carry-place tasks as shown in Fig. 1. The motion generator runs on the GPU in a separate process, while the tracking executes on the CPU through ONNX Runtime. Joint encoders and the onboard IMU provide proprioceptive states, whereas a motion-capture system tracks the robot base and object, providing robot position and object pose for closed-loop execution. The tracking policy runs at 50 Hz and outputs residual joint positions that are added to the generated reference to form joint-level PD targets, which are tracked by a PD controller running at 200 Hz. The generator uses 10 denoising steps to predict a chunk of 64 robot–object motion, corresponding to a 1.28 s horizon. The tracker continues executing the current chunk from the generator during inference, until a new chunk arrives from the generator process. In our setup, the motion generator can run at a maximum frequency of ∼ 12 .5 Hz, accounting for communication latency and diffusion sampling. Every attempted execution succeeds: ten out of ten times for the repeated motion, and five out of five across the distinct motions. VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented DiPLO and established a key finding for limited-data humanoid loco-manipulation: strong task condi-tioning can be crucial for effective pretraining, while post-training can remove this dependency for deployment. Start-ing from only 29 retargeted pick-carry-place demonstrations, dynamically feasible data augmentation and strong kinematic conditioning enable the diffusion planner to learn trackable whole-body and object motions. Removing this conditioning directly causes a substantial performance drop, while training from scratch without it cannot recover comparable perfor-mance through downstream fine-tuning alone. We address this dependence with SD:DPO, which distills a reference-conditioned self-teacher into a planner conditioned only 12 

on state history and the object goal. This recovers goal-conditioned motion generation and enables the planner to op-erate on goals unseen in pretraining demonstrations. Comple-mentary to this, DA:PPO further optimises the planner through closed-loop interaction with the frozen tracker, aligning gen-erated motions with what the robot can successfully execute. Together, SD:DPO and DA:PPO achieve approximately 99% survival and 99% goal success during pick-carry-place roll-outs, while the same training recipe transfers to substantially different skills, including kicking and pushing/pulling. The resulting planner-tracker also transfers directly to the Unitree G1 without any real-world fine-tuning. These results suggest a broader principle for robot learning: privileged or expensive supervision may be most valuable as a scaffold for pretraining rather than as a permanent deployment requirement. Future work will investigate whether this principle extends to asingle model trained jointly across multiple loco-manipulation skills, more diverse objects and environments, and onboard perception, thereby removing the current reliance on external motion capture. D. Tracking Controller Training Details 

This appendix gives the observation layout and the reset strategy used to pretrain the generalist tracking controller (Section IV-B3). 

Observations. The reference observations of the actor con-sist of reference joint states, body poses, and object-tracking features at future trajectory steps {1, 2, 4, 8} frames (20 –

160 ms). We use an asymmetric actor–critic: the critic addi-tionally receives object velocities, reference contact indicators, and a wider temporal window of reference motion to track. 

Reset sampling. Training resets select both a reference motion and a starting phase, then initialize the robot and object from that reference state with perturbations. These two sampling decisions use separate mechanisms. E. Survival Rate and Termination Conditions 

The survival rate reported in the results tables is the per-centage of evaluation rollouts in which neither a robot fall nor an object drop occurs. As such, the survival rates for kicking and push/pull tasks are high because the object is always on the ground. Task success separately measures whether the final object pose satisfies the goal tolerances. The full set of failure conditions below governs episode termination during tracking controller pretraining (Section IV-B3) and evaluation-based motion reweighting (Appendix D): 1) Robot fall: the absolute height error of the torso or any wrist or ankle exceeds 0.25 m. 2) Object drop: the absolute object-height error exceeds 

1 m. 3) The absolute difference between the z-components of gravity projected into the actual and reference torso frames exceeds 0.8.4) Any component of the base angular velocity exceeds 

8.73 rad/s in magnitude. The object-height threshold follows a piecewise-linear sched-ule through 1.0, 0.3, 0.6, and 0.3 m at iterations 5000 , 15 ,000 ,

20 ,000 , and 25 ,000 , respectively, and is held fixed during each evaluation. Reaching the end of the reference motion or the 

10 s episode limit without a failure is treated as a timeout and counts towards the survival rate.

K. Action-Level Diffusion Baselines 

This appendix describes the action-level baselines of Sec-tion V-D: an action-only diffusion policy in the style of PDP [13], and a state–action diffusion model in the style of BeyondMimic [14]. Both output robot joint PD targets directly, with no tracker in the loop. Table XIV lists their configuration. 18 

Training data. Both baselines are trained via behaviour cloning on rollouts of our pretrained generalist tracking policy over every motion of the SBTO- and symmetry-augmented data corpus, under the randomisation of Table IX. As in PDP [13], the joint targets applied to the robot are perturbed using temporally correlated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise as used in [14], while the tracker’s unperturbed output is stored as the label, so that the data pairs off-nominal states with the actions that correct them. We collect full-length rollouts and 

5 s rollouts started from random frames of each motion, and discard every rollout that fails, generating 15 M frames ( 85 h) of state–action data. 

Models. The state-action diffusion baseline uses the dif-fusion transformer (DiT) architecture of [34], for a faithful reproduction of the results. Here, a DiT learns to denoise a chunk of joint state–action tokens, each with its own noise level, given the current state and a history of state–action pairs; state tokens attend to all states and to no action, and action tokens attend only to states and actions up to their own step (causal attention). PDP [13] only learns to diffuse the action distribution. For the PDP-style baseline, we therefore drop the state prediction from the same network and exclude it from the loss, leaving an action diffusion policy conditioned on the state–action history and the task. Both models replan at every control step ( 50 Hz) with 20 denoising steps and execute only the first action. The network, the diffusion process, the optimizer and the collection of expert rollouts follow Diffuse-CLoC [34] and the first version of BeyondMimic [14], which applies Diffuse-CLoC to the robot we use. Where the two differ (attention heads, horizons, state features) we take the BeyondMimic values. We depart from both works in the following places: 1) The state additionally contains the object’s observations such as its position, orientation and linear velocity. 2) The conditioned models receive the goal and the IK-retargeted motion as additional context tokens. 3) Later versions of BeyondMimic diffuse the latent of an action autoencoder. We diffuse joint targets directly, as Diffuse-CLoC and the first version of BeyondMimic do. 

Unconditioned prior. In [14], [34] the state-action diffusion model is not conditioned on the task: it is an unconditioned prior that is steered at inference using a task-specific guidance cost. Hence, we train a separate diffusion model without the conditioning tokens and evaluate it at test time by steering with the IK-retargeted motion. Unlike the conditioned models, it takes its horizons and its sampling of noise levels from Diffuse-CLoC; these levels include zero, so that the clean keyframes of in-betweening are seen in training. 

Test-time steering. The guidance cost sums, over the pre-dicted states of the horizon, the squared position error and the orientation error 1−⟨ q, q IK ⟩2 of the root and of the object with respect to the IK-retargeted motion, with q a unit quaternion. At every denoising step the predicted clean states are moved against the gradient of this cost, scaled by the guidance weight. Keyframe in-betweening instead inpaints a single state ahead in time: the keyframe is the state of the IK-retargeted motion at the given lookahead, expressed relative to the measured planar position and heading of the robot, and the waypoint cost of [14] pulls the planar root position toward that of the keyframe. L. Ablation on Diffusion Prediction Horizon 

This appendix examines how far into the future the diffusion motion generator can plan. We pretrain five generators that differ only in the prediction horizon n ∈ { 20 , 32 , 48 , 64 , 100 }

frames, i.e. 0.4 s to 2.0 s of motion at 50 Hz. The denoiser remains unchanged; only its input sequence grows, since the 19 

prior and the generated chunk each contribute n tokens. The tracker reads its reference up to 8 frames ahead (Appendix D), so the last 8 frames of a chunk are kept as its lookahead, and the first m = n − 8 frames are executed, as in our default of n = 64 and m = 56 .
