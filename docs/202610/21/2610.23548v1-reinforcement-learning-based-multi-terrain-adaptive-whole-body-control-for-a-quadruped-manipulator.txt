Reinforcement Learning Based Multi-Terrain
Adaptive Whole-Body Control for a Quadruped
Manipulator
Yanzhou Jin1, Fei Suo1, Chang Liu1, Yasuhisa Hasegawa2, Member, IEEE, Liming Chen3, Senior Member,
IEEE, Yusuke Iwasawa1, Yutaka Matsuo1 and Yaonan Zhu1,∗, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Legged robots equipped with manipulators
offer promising capabilities for loco-manipulation tasks
in unstructured environments. However, achieving task-
consistent end-effector behavior during locomotion re-
mains challenging due to terrain-induced disturbances
and the strong coupling between locomotion and ma-
nipulation, particularly for small-scale quadruped plat-
forms. A reinforcement-learning-based whole-body control
framework is proposed for quadruped loco-manipulation
on complex terrain. The proposed framework combines
a hybrid task representation with task-prioritized whole-
body learning to coordinate locomotion and manipulation
while preserving task-consistent end-effector behavior. A
lightweight quadruped manipulator platform is further de-
veloped to validate the proposed framework on a small-
scale quadruped system. Real-world experiments demon-
strate successful deployment on complex terrains such
as slopes up to 30 degrees, stairs, and deformable ter-
rain. These results demonstrate the feasibility of deploying
a learned whole-body control policy for quadruped loco-
manipulation in real-world environments and highlight the
importance of task-consistent whole-body coordination for
terrain-adaptive loco-manipulation.
Index Terms—Robots, Learning Control Systems, Intelli-
gent Control
I. INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in quadruped locomotion have enabled
robust traversal across challenging terrains. By integrating on-
board manipulators, quadruped robots are expected to perform
loco-manipulation tasks that require both mobility and ob-
ject interaction, such as inspection, maintenance, search-and-
rescue, and operation in unstructured environments. However,
combining locomotion and manipulation introduces strong
coupling between base motion and end-effector (EE) behavior,
making precise task execution substantially more challenging
than pure locomotion [1]–[3].

Compared with larger mobile manipulators, small-scale
quadruped manipulators operate under significantly tighter
payload constraints [4]–[6]. Commercially available arms used
on such platforms can occupy a substantial portion of the
quadruped payload capacity and act as moving loads during
locomotion [7], [8]. Their static mass, configuration-dependent
center-of-mass shifts, and dynamic reactions may increase leg
actuator demands and reduce the performance of terrain traver-
sal. Conversely, terrain irregularities and changing foothold
contacts can induce body motions that are transmitted to the
end effector, thereby compromising manipulation performance
[3]. Existing loco-manipulation systems often adjust body
posture to expand the manipulator workspace and improve
end-effector reachability [9], [10]. Although effective on flat
terrain, such posture adjustments may conflict with the body
regulation required for terrain traversal [11], whereas body
motions required for terrain adaptation may disturb end-
effector tracking [12], [13]. Accordingly, maintaining task-
relevant end-effector behavior while preserving locomotion
feasibility on challenging terrain remains a central challenge
for quadruped loco-manipulation.
Reliable locomotion requires adequate body regulation [11],
while manipulation depends on maintaining locomotion feasi-
bility. These dependencies motivate the state-dependent pri-
oritization of body regulation, locomotion, and manipula-
tion. Existing loco-manipulation approaches commonly co-
ordinate locomotion and manipulation through unified op-
timization frameworks [14]–[16], hierarchical locomotion-
manipulation architectures [17], or learning-based whole-
body control (WBC) policies [18], [19]. Optimization-based
approaches can explicitly incorporate whole-body dynamics
and physical constraints [20], but their application to rough
terrain depends on sufficiently accurate modeling of coupled
robot dynamics, changing contacts, and terrain interactions,
together with repeated online optimization. Reinforcement-
learning (RL) is well suited to this setting because it allows
closed-loop whole-body coordination to be optimized offline
over randomized terrain, contact, and loading conditions,
while deployment requires only fixed-cost policy inference.
Recent learning-based studies have demonstrated whole-body
trajectory tracking [8], object interaction [21], [22], and mo-
bile manipulation [23]–[25]. However, explicit state-dependent
coordination among body regulation, locomotion, and manip-
ulation has received limited attention, particularly regarding
how manipulation should be modulated when body regulation

Beyond objective coordination, end-effector position and
orientation play different roles in manipulation tasks. Posi-
tional deviations can often be compensated through robot
repositioning or locomotion planning, whereas orientation
tracking frequently has a direct impact on task execution.
Examples include container transportation, tool manipulation,
and other disturbance-sensitive tasks in which the end-effector
orientation must remain consistent relative to the environment
[26]. Consequently, treating end-effector position and orienta-
tion identically may lead to unnecessary compromises between
task consistency and locomotion feasibility. Effective task
representations that balance these competing requirements,
therefore, remain another key challenge for rough-terrain loco-
manipulation.
To address these challenges, a reinforcement-learning-based
whole-body loco-manipulation framework combining task-
prioritized learning with a hybrid task-space representation
is proposed. The policy is optimized using Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO [27]) because its clipped update objective
supports stable training, while its rollout-based formulation is
compatible with large-scale parallel simulation for continuous
18-DoF control under randomized terrain and contact condi-
tions. Task-prioritized learning modulates the contributions of
lower-priority objectives according to the current performance
of body regulation and locomotion. The hybrid representation
regulates end-effector orientation relative to gravity to preserve
task consistency, while expressing end-effector position in
the body frame to maintain tracking feasibility under terrain-
induced body motions. A lightweight quadruped manipulator
is further constructed by integrating a custom-designed 1.5-
kg Lite Arm with a Unitree Go1 (Fig. 1). The lightweight
design reduces manipulator-induced loading, while the whole-
body policy coordinates the arm and base under the remain-
ing configuration- and motion-dependent coupling. Extensive
simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate consistent
end-effector tracking on challenging terrains, including slopes,
stairs, and deformable surfaces. The contributions of this work
are summarized as follows:

ing a custom-designed Lite Arm and a Unitree Go1 is

II. METHOD
A. System Configuration and Problem Formulation
The key problem is to preserve both locomotion feasibility
and task-consistent end-effector behavior during rough-terrain
traversal. To reduce manipulator-induced interference with
locomotion at the hardware level, a lightweight 6-DoF manip-
ulator was developed, as shown in Fig. 2 (a). The manipulator
weighs only 1.5 kg and provides a maximum reach exceeding
60 cm. Its structure combines PLA-CF components with
embedded carbon-fiber tubes to provide sufficient stiffness
without imposing excessive additional mass. Based on this
platform, rough-terrain loco-manipulation is formulated as a
unified whole-body control problem involving body regulation,
locomotion tracking, and end-effector regulation.

C. Training Protocol
The policy is trained using PPO with the asymmetric actor–
critic architecture in Isaac Lab. Training is performed in 4096
parallel environments on a NVIDIA 5090 GPU. Each episode
runs for 1000 policy steps at 50 Hz (20 s; physics timestep
0.005 s, decimation = 4), without early termination.
The base-velocity and end-effector-pose commands remain
fixed during the first half of each episode and are jointly
resampled once at the midpoint to represent a change in
task intent. End-effector target sampling is conditioned on the
base-velocity command. Under stationary commands, target
positions are sampled from the full reachable workspace of
the manipulator to encourage broad manipulation capability
and diverse arm configurations. During locomotion, targets are
sampled from a locomotion-oriented region within the reach-
able workspace, favoring targets that remain feasible under
continuous body motion and terrain disturbances while retain-
ing sufficient freedom for task-relevant end-effector regulation.
This strategy concentrates training samples on locomotion-
compatible targets while retaining task diversity.
Training uses procedurally generated terrains, including flat
ground, slopes, stairs, boxes, and random obstacles. A terrain-
level curriculum strategy gradually increases terrain difficulty
from completely flat ground (difficulty 0.0) to the highest
challenging terrain configuration (difficulty 1.0), as illustrated
in Fig. 3(a–h).
Domain randomization is applied to dynamics parameters,
payload conditions, and external disturbances to facilitate sim-
to-real transfer. In particular, the PD stiffness and damping are
scaled by [0.8,1.3] and [0.5,1.5], each arm-link mass is scaled
by 1.0–1.8x, and an additional mass of 0–0.5 kg is randomly
attached to the end-effector to simulate varying payloads. An
action delay of 0–3 control steps (0–60 ms) is also applied.
External disturbances include an episode-long trunk wrench
with each force and torque component sampled from [−2,2]
N and [−2,2] N·m, respectively, together with horizontal base-
velocity perturbations in [−0.5,0.5] m/s applied every 10–15s.

performance.

parallel simulation rollouts. The evaluation metrics include
linear and angular base velocity tracking errors, end-effector

favorable, the resulting policy lacks the locomotion capability

In contrast, disabling Priority III component has little effect
on locomotion performance but significantly increases end-
effector tracking error. As shown in Fig. 3 (k–l), the position
error rises sharply while the orientation error remains relatively
low. This indicates that the policy can satisfy orientation
constraints while staying near locally comfortable arm con-
figurations, but fails to actively approach the target. Without
Priority III component disabled, the manipulator does not fully
exploit its workspace for goal-directed behavior, resulting in
poor position tracking. This highlights the role of Priority III
in promoting effective end-effector tracking.
Disabling Priority I component degrades both locomotion
and manipulation performance, indicating that body regula-
tion and contact-related objectives provide the foundation for
higher-level behaviors. Without this layer, both mobility and
end-effector performance deteriorate.

Among the manipulation-enabled methods, WBC consistently
achieves lower base-motion error, EE tracking error, and
joint overload exposure than Hier, indicating that coordinated
whole-body control reduces the effects of arm–body coupling
and terrain-induced disturbances. WBC-heavy exhibits higher
joint overload exposure than WBC, particularly on difficult ter-
rains, suggesting that reducing manipulator mass can alleviate
actuator loading under the evaluated conditions.
Arm-motion speed and payload. Repeated lateral end-
effector motions were evaluated at 0.4 and 0.8 m/s with
payloads of 0.2 and 0.5 kg. For Hier, the failure rate rose
from 8.50% under the slow/light condition to 13.77% under
the fast/heavy condition, while the EE root-mean-square error
(RMSE) over successful rollouts increased from 0.070 to 0.108
m. In comparison, WBC produced only two failures over all
4096 rollouts and maintained an EE RMSE of 0.057–0.069
m. These results indicate that WBC is less sensitive to arm-
motion speed and payload.
B. Real-World Experiments
1) Representative Terrain Traversal Demonstrations: To
provide representative real-world demonstrations of the pro-
posed framework, the robot was tested on challenging uneven
terrains, including a 30◦slope and a sequence of 10 cm stairs,
while carrying a container with two 36 mm elastic balls.
The carried object serves as a visual indicator of end-effector
disturbance during locomotion. The WBC policy runs onboard
on an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX at 49.9 Hz, with a mean
inference time of 1.53 ms and a sensor-to-command latency
of 2.76 ms, while low-level joint control runs at 500 Hz.
As shown in Fig. 4, the robot traverses both terrain types
while maintaining coordinated locomotion and manipulation
behaviors. During slope ascent, descent, and stair traversal, the
end-effector remains approximately upright despite substantial
body pitch and roll variations. The observed behavior is
consistent with the proposed hybrid task-space formulation,
where end-effector orientation is regulated with respect to
gravity while position targets are represented in a body-centric
coordinate system.

b) Grasping Trials and Failure Analysis: To evaluate the
proposed framework in realistic task settings, we conducted
autonomous semantic-guided grasping experiments on two
challenging terrain categories: deformable terrain and lateral
stair descent, as shown in Fig. 6. The target object is specified
through a natural-language query (e.g., ’hand over paper cup’
or ’give me the electric screwdriver’). Each trial starts several
meters away from the target object, traverses the terrain, ap-
proaches the target, and autonomously executes the grasp once
the object enters the reachable workspace. A trial is successful
only if the robot both traverses the terrain and lifts the target
object clear of the surface. Four everyday objects with diverse
geometries and appearances are involved, including a paper
cup, a transparent glass container, a transparent tube, and a
600 g electric screwdriver. Twenty trials were performed under
each terrain condition, for a total of 40 trials.
The deformable terrain is built from sandbags and sponges,
introducing compliant contacts and contact uncertainty that
were not encountered during simulation training; 17/20 trials
succeeded. In the lateral stair descent, the four feet continu-
ously occupy footholds at different heights, producing large
pitch and roll variations of the support polygon; 15/20 trials
succeeded. The overall success rate is 80% (32/40).
The eight failures (Fig. 6, right) are distributed across
distinct stages of the task rather than concentrated in a single
subsystem. Two occurred during traversal, where the robot
lost balance on the steepest footholds. The other six occurred
after the terrain had been crossed: four from inaccurate target
localization on the transparent tube and container, one where
the base did not stop in time and contacted the table edge,
and one from a grasp that closed but did not retain the object
during the lift. Grouped by cause, three failures involve motion
control (the two falls and the collision with the table), while
the remaining five stem from localization or grasp execution.

Recent quadruped loco-manipulation systems have demon-
strated increasingly capable whole-body control. However,
most are evaluated on flat ground, mild outdoor environments,
or manipulation-focused tasks [8], [21]–[23], [30]. In contrast,
the proposed system emphasizes task-consistent whole-body
loco-manipulation during challenging terrain traversal. As
summarized in Table I, few existing quadruped manipulation
systems report task execution on steep slopes or highly com-
plex terrains. Our platform demonstrates loco-manipulation on
slopes up to 30◦and complex terrain while maintaining task-
consistent end-effector behavior.
From the perspective of task-space representation, exist-
ing approaches typically formulate manipulation objectives
either in body-centric coordinates [23] or world-centric coor-
dinates [8], [22]. The proposed hybrid task-space formulation
instead combines body-frame position tracking with world-
frame-aligned orientation regulation, keeping the end-effector
z-axis parallel to the world z-axis, and provides a practical
compromise between kinematic feasibility and task consis-
tency under terrain-induced disturbances.
Compared with existing quadruped manipulation systems
summarized in Table I, the proposed system achieves terrain-
adaptive loco-manipulation on a compact 13.5 kg platform
while maintaining task-consistent end-effector behavior on
challenging terrain.
V. CONCLUSION
This paper presents a reinforcement-learning-based whole-
body control framework for quadruped manipulators operating
on challenging terrain. By combining a hybrid task-space
representation with task-prioritized whole-body learning, the
proposed framework enables coordinated locomotion and ma-
nipulation while preserving task-consistent end-effector behav-
ior. Simulation and real-world experiments on slopes, stairs,
and deformable terrain demonstrate that coordinated whole-
body control enables terrain-adaptive loco-manipulation while
maintaining task-consistent end-effector behavior and terrain
traversal capability on small-scale quadruped manipulators.
Several limitations remain. Because external motion-capture
coverage was unavailable in the uneven-terrain test area, the
quantitative real-robot evaluation was based on onboard iner-
tial and joint measurements together with forward kinematics.
In addition, the current validation is limited to a single robot
platform and a representative but limited set of terrain and
manipulation conditions.
Future work will focus on large-scale loco-manipulation
data collection, improving hardware design, and extending the
framework to more complex manipulation and transportation
tasks in dynamic environments.
