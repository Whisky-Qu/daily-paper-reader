# Fast Motion Retargeting Pipeline for Scalable Humanoid Robot Learning 

Daniel Marew ∗, Sangjun Park ∗, Nisal Perera, and Donghyun Kim 1

Abstract — While large-scale human motion capture datasets provide a rich source of demonstrations for humanoid robot learning, retargeting the diverse motions to different humanoid robots remains a nontrivial challenge. Existing kinodynamic trajectory optimization pipelines are computationally expen-sive and often require manual adjustments per motion. We present FRDV (Fast Retargeting with Dynamics Validation), a scalable framework consisting of rapid constrained kinematic retargeting and an additional dynamics validation pipeline. Its kinematic stage integrates a custom Sequential Quadratic Programming solver, Real-Time Iteration, and parallel chunk-based processing, achieving up to 9,156 frames per second — approximately up to 136 × faster than GMR and ProtoMotions — while robot-specific constraints are incorporated into the optimization to reduce common retargeting artifacts, such as ground penetration and self-collision. For motions requiring additional verification, a contact implicit iLQR controller is utilized to test the retargeted reference under full rigid body dy-namics. This dynamics-based validation identifies dynamically infeasible motions and refines contact-related artifacts such as foot floating and foot skating. We evaluate retargeting quality by training motion tracking reinforcement learning policies using several state-of-the-art pipelines and comparing their tracking fidelity and success rates. Furthermore, sim-to-real transfer is demonstrated on a Unitree G1 humanoid. Videos and code are available at: https://daroslab.github.io/frdv/ .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large-scale human motion capture (MoCap) datasets are increasingly used to train humanoid robots to reproduce diverse locomotion, manipulation, and whole-body behav-iors [1]–[5]. Datasets such as AMASS [6], LAFAN1 [7], OMOMO [8], and BONES-SEED [9] collectively contain hundreds of thousands of human motion sequences. How-ever, these motions are hard to directly use for humanoid learning due to differences in morphology, joint structure, and hardware limitations. A common approach is to adapt human motion data to the morphology and kinematic con-straints of a target robot through motion retargeting [10]– [12]. Despite its widespread use, existing retargeting pipelines face two major challenges when applied to large-scale datasets. The first is computational efficiency . For example, our runtime evaluation shows that existing retargeting meth-ods, such as ProtoMotions [13], require more than 40 hours to process the AMASS dataset (see Table II), which contains approximately 11,000 motion sequences spanning over 40 hours of motion data from more than 300 subjects [6]. The second challenge is retargeting quality . Differences in body proportions, joint limits, and kinematic structures

To address these challenges, we introduce FRDV (Fast

B. Numerical Optimization for Inverse Kinematics 

The inverse kinematics problem can be formulated as a constrained nonlinear least squares problem. General-purpose nonlinear programming solvers such as IPOPT [19] and SNOPT [20] can solve such problems robustly, but their generality can introduce computational overhead in large-scale, frame-wise retargeting. Existing pipelines use different numerical formulations. GMR [12] uses the Mink differential inverse kinematics framework [21], which formulates each differential IK step as a quadratic program. PyRoki [22] uses a Levenberg–Marquardt optimizer and supports hard constraints through an augmented Lagrangian formulation. ProtoMotions [14] uses PyRoki in its motion retargeting workflow. Our method instead utilizes an SQP solver specialized for explicit constraint handling, achieving high-throughput retargeting. At each iteration, the nonlinear constraints are linearized, and the Hessian is approximated by the Gauss– Newton matrix. Because the objective is a weighted sum of squared residuals with nonnegative weights, this approxima-tion is positive semidefinite, yielding a convex QP that can be solved efficiently using OSQP [23]. Furthermore, the Real-Time Iteration (RTI) scheme [24], originally developed for nonlinear model predictive control, reduces the per-frame cost to a single SQP step by exploiting temporal continuity as a warm start. Our solver combines these ideas and specializes them for the motion retargeting setting. 

C. Dynamics Verification and Contact-Implicit Control. 

Assessing and improving the dynamic consistency of retargeted motions requires considerations about full-body dynamics and contact. Methods based on explicit contact mode sequences generally require the mode schedule to be prescribed, whereas contact implicit trajectory optimization methods optimize the motion and contact behavior jointly without requiring a predefined contact sequence [25], [26]. More recently, sampling-based dynamics refinement methods such as SPIDER [16] and DynaRetarget [27] have been proposed to transform or refine kinematic demonstrations into dynamically consistent robot trajectories. In this paper, we use a gradient based, contact implicit optimal control approach [15], [26], in which contact is embedded directly in the dynamics model and differentiated through, allowing contact transitions to emerge during optimization. We build upon and modify the MuJoCo MPC framework [15] to track IK retargeted reference motions under MuJoCo’s full rigid body dynamics and contact model. Actuator limits are enforced during optimization, ensuring that the simulated trajectories remain within the torque bounds while reducing contact related artifacts such as foot floating and foot skating. References that do not yield a valid optimized rollout are excluded from the dataset used to train downstream motion tracking policies. III. M ETHODOLOGY

C. Parallel Motion Processing 

To address the computational bottleneck of processing large scale datasets, we partition each motion sequence into temporal chunks, retarget the chunks concurrently, and then concatenate the resulting trajectories. Since each chunk is initialized independently from a nominal T-pose, we use additional SQP iterations for its first few frames to obtain a reliable initial solution. After convergence, the solver switches to Real-Time Iteration mode ( K = 1 ) and warm-starts each frame from the previous solution to exploit temporal coherence. However, since each chunk is optimized independently, the merged trajectory is not guaranteed to be smooth, particularly at chunk boundaries. Given the retargeting objective is non-convex, neighboring chunks may converge to different local minima even when the underlying motion is continuous. For the boundary between chunks k and k + 1 , we characterize the configuration mismatch as 

∆qb = q(k+1) start ⊖ q(k)end ,

where q(k)end and q(k+1) start denote the final and initial retargeted joint configurations of two consecutive chunks, respectively. A large configuration mismatch can produce sudden changes in the resulting joint velocities and accelerations. We com-pute finite difference approximations of the configuration velocities and accelerations:

D. Contact-Implicit iLQR Dynamics Verification 

CI-iLQR reliably tracks a highly dynamic LAFAN1 single-leg hopping sequence with limited contact support in full rigid-body simulation on both G1 and Dash, without platform-specific tuning (Figure 4). Figure 5 shows that CI-iLQR enforces contact through MuJoCo dynamics, removing foot-floating and foot-skating artifacts from the retargeted G1 reference that are difficult to correct using kinematics alone. For quantitative evaluation, we use the first 26 s of LAFAN1 Dance1-S1, which includes loss of balance and recovery to standing. Tracking this recovery requires mo-mentum regulation and consistent foot–ground contact while respecting the robot’s actuator torque limits. We train identi-cal RL policies on IK-only and CI-iLQR-corrected references and evaluate them using Eg-mpbpe , Ea-mpbpe , and Empboe .Table V shows that CI-iLQR generally improves global position and orientation tracking, by reducing contact ar-tifacts and torque spikes. However, we observe a slight increase in anchor-relative error, indicating small deviations in limb placement relative to the anchor body. The median anchor relative position error increases by approximately 0.4 mm. This increase in error may reflect slight jitter introduced during CI-iLQR correction that is not fully tracked by the learned RL policy.

We presented FRDV, a scalable motion retargeting frame-work that combines high-throughput constrained IK with op-tional contact implicit dynamics validation. FRDV achieved substantial speedups over existing methods, generalized across humanoid morphologies, produced references with competitive downstream RL performance, and achieved suc-cessful transfer to Unitree G1 hardware. Although FRDV substantially improves kinematic retar-geting throughput, the optional CI-iLQR dynamics validation and refinement stage adds substantial processing time. Given its high computational cost, CI-iLQR is best applied selec-tively to motions exhibiting residual contact artifacts, such as foot floating during intended support or foot skating during stance, and to motions whose dynamic feasibility remains uncertain after kinematic retargeting. In these cases, CI-iLQR can assess trackability under the simulated dynamics and may refine contact artifacts before downstream RL training. Furthermore, processing temporal chunks in parallel increases throughput for long sequences but can produce inconsistent local solutions due to the nonconvex optimiza-tion, even with R EFINE BOUNDARY enabled. For the highest trajectory consistency, we recommend using a single worker within each motion sequence and applying parallel process-ing across independent motion files. The batch retarget 

script provided in the repository supports batch processing of multiple motion files. Future work will focus on faster dynamics validation and more consistent parallel retargeting. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
