Abstract —Whole-body control plays a crucial role in enabling humanoid robots to execute expressive motions. Faithful ex-ecution requires preserving both the intended action and its expressive characteristics. Existing whole-body trackers typically balance joint and keypoint tracking with physical stability across diverse motions, but these objectives do not necessarily priori-tize the expressive characteristics of each action. Our analysis shows that lower average positional tracking error does not guarantee better preservation of dynamic and postural motion characteristics. To address this gap, we propose a residual learning framework with compensation objectives targeting am-plitude, energy, body expansion, body lean, and pelvis height. To concentrate compensation while limiting interference with the underlying tracking behavior, motion-aware gating allocates correction capacity according to body-part activity, preserving larger correction ranges in more active parts and restricting them elsewhere. For each frozen tracker, we train motion-specific residual policies and distill them into a unified policy shared across motion categories. Simulation and physical experiments on the Unitree G1 across eight motion categories demonstrate superior preservation of dynamic and postural motion charac-teristics compared with the corresponding baselines. 

Index Terms —Whole-Body Motion Planning and Control, Human and Humanoid Motion Analysis and Synthesis, Rein-forcement Learning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Whole-body motion allows humanoid robots to convey intent and express themselves through physical behavior. Ges-tures and movements carry meaning through both the actions performed and how they unfold. Movement dynamics and posture are thus integral to the behaviors a robot seeks to re-produce. Recent advances in whole-body control have enabled hu-manoids to reproduce a broad repertoire of reference motions with a shared tracking policy [3], [4]. Maintaining balance and satisfying physical constraints, however, may require devia-tions from the reference motion [7]. While aggregate tracking errors measure whole-body motion fidelity, expressiveness may depend on particular postural and dynamic features, such as hand movement amplitude during waving or forward lean during bowing [8], [9]. How can we recover these characteristics while maintaining stable execution and accounting for tracking accuracy? 

Previous work has leveraged residual learning to improve tracking and adapt pretrained controllers to challenging mo-tions [10], object interaction [11], and deployment differ-ences [12]. Residual policies refine execution by correcting nominal actions while retaining the underlying controller. This offers a way to address expressive characteristic discrepancies while keeping the pretrained tracker fixed. To this end, we propose a residual learning framework for preserving expressive motion characteristics. Inspired by analyses of expressive movement [9], [13], we characterize dynamics through amplitude and energy, and posture through body expansion, body lean, and pelvis height. Discrepancies in these characteristics guide compensation by a unified residual policy shared across motion categories for each frozen tracker. Our contributions are threefold. 1) We analyze postural and dynamic deviations across motions and trackers, revealing that lower mean posi-tional error does not necessarily correspond to better preservation of expressive motion characteristics. 2) We propose a motion-aware residual learning framework that augments a frozen whole-body tracker with a unified policy to recover expressive motion characteristics. 3) We conduct extensive evaluations across eight motion categories in simulation and on the physical Unitree G1. A. Expressive Motion Analysis 

Human movement studies relate expressive qualities to movement dynamics and body posture, including trunk and arm configuration, movement direction, and intensity [9], [13]. Research on bodily expression organizes these aspects into interpretable motion factors and quantitative measures [14]. Laban-based studies associate combinations of movement el-ements with emotions elicited through movement [15], while computational approaches combine spatial body representa-tions with energy-based temporal features for emotion recog-nition [16]. Dynamic and postural features have also been examined in virtual avatars. Head inclination, head position, body openness, and joint velocities have been used to ex-tract representative static poses from expressive walking, with perceptual evaluations supporting the transfer of expressive information [17]. Extending expressive movement to robots requires accounting for task requirements and physical embod-iment. Work in this direction has explored learning expressive motion objectives from human feedback [18] and combining human motion style transfer with physics-aware generation and whole-body tracking [19]. Drawing on these studies, we focus on the dynamic and postural characteristics that contribute to each motion’s expressive qualities and use them to define explicit compensation targets for humanoid motion execution. 

B. Whole-Body Motion Tracking and Residual Adaptation 

Whole-body motion tracking enables humanoids to repro-duce diverse reference motions while maintaining balance [2]– [4], [20]–[22]. Expressive control has progressed from coordi-nating upper-body imitation with velocity-controlled locomo-tion [6] to reproducing dynamic full-body motions [7]. Recent residual and adapter-based methods further extend pretrained controllers to challenging motion distributions [10], [23], object interaction [11], and changing task requirements [24]. Related approaches address dynamics mismatch [25] and deployment-interface differences [12]. In contrast to these adaptation objectives, our work focuses on preserving ex-pressive motion characteristics by explicitly compensating for deviations in posture and movement dynamics. III. B. Data Curation 

We select eight motion categories from AMASS [27] using BABEL annotations [28]: walk, kick, punch, crouch, wave, clap, cross arms, and bow, covering diverse limb movements and postural changes. After excluding unsupported interactions and mixed actions, we retarget the motions to G1 using SOMA [29], resample them to 50 Hz , and apply PHC-based preprocessing [30] to obtain robot reference trajectories. To compute motion characteristics over complete move-ments or sustained poses, we segment references into units

Limb-trajectory extrema, direction reversals, and inferred foot con-tacts guide movement boundaries; pelvis height, torso incli-nation, and relative hand positions identify posture changes and holds. Cycles span successive same-phase events, while one-shot units span onset to completion. We record each unit’s type and frame boundaries according to its observed motion pattern, using the same intervals for characteristic computation and comparison across methods.

Initialization and termination. Episodes are initialized from reference poses and velocities at motion-unit boundaries or preceding run-ins. Run-ins provide tracking context before characteristic rewards become active. An episode ends when the reference motion is exhausted or a fall occurs, detected when pelvis height above ground drops below 0.15 m . A fall replaces the final-step reward with −1. Incomplete units retain their framewise rewards but receive no unit-completion terms. 

Domain randomization. The underlying trackers are pre-trained with domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer [3], [4]. We train residual policies under nominal dynamics.

We evaluate our framework through three questions. 

• Q1. Do lower positional tracking errors imply better preservation of dynamic and postural characteristics? 

• Q2. How effectively does residual compensation preserve these characteristics, and how does it affect tracking accuracy and motion completion? 

• Q3. How does motion-aware gating affect residual inter-vention, tracking accuracy, and characteristic recovery? A. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate our framework in simulation and on the physical Unitree G1 across eight motion categories. We train residual policies in MuJoCo Warp [32] with 1,024 parallel environments on NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPUs. Using the AMASS motions curated in Sec. III-B, we compare SONIC [3], GMT [4], and TWIST2 [21] with shared reference motions, unit boundaries, and evaluation starts. All trackers op-erate at 50 Hz . Labels w/ Res. and w/o Res. denote execution with and without residual compensation. 

Tracking baseline: We include Track-Res, a tracking baseline that trains motion-specific residual policies to min-imize mean pelvis-relative joint-position error. It uses two 256-unit hidden layers, as in our unified policy. We omit characteristic rewards but retain gating, regularization, and all constraints, including those on posture and body expansion.

V. L IMITATIONS 

We use five descriptors to capture complementary aspects of expressive motion, but they do not cover all dynamic and postural characteristics. Agreement on these measures therefore does not imply preservation of every expressive cue. Unit-mean energy and lean errors can mask temporal discrepancies, and total energy can mask redistribution across body groups. Recovery also depends on the underlying tracker. SONIC can exhibit limited waist excursion despite retaining all three waist control channels. Adjusting body lean may then involve compensatory hip and leg motion. Such adjustments can alter limb amplitude, motion energy, and pelvis height, creating conflicts between recovery targets. Improving one characteristic may therefore worsen another. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce a motion-aware residual learning framework for preserving expressive motion characteristics in humanoid robots. We use motion amplitude, motion energy, body expansion, body lean, and pelvis height as explicit compensation targets for a residual policy that augments a frozen whole-body tracker. Our comparisons show that lower mean positional tracking error alone is insufficient to ensure preservation of these characteristics. In the reported simulation comparison, residual compensation achieves lower aggregate errors in all five characteristics than tracking-oriented resid-uals, with higher positional error.
