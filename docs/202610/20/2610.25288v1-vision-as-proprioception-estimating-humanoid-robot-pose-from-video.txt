Abstract — Estimating a humanoid robot’s internal state from video has a wide range of real-world applications but remains unexplored. During interactions with a robot, its onboard state information may be unavailable due to privacy, ownership, or communication constraints. Meanwhile, online and AI-generated robot videos provide rich sources of data but typically lack corresponding joint configurations, limiting their direct use in robot learning. To address these issues, we present HumanoidPose, a new framework that treats vision as proprio-ception to recover full-body joint configurations in world space from video alone. Specifically, we combine pretrained human-pose features with robot kinematics in a unified model that predicts each embodiment’s joint configurations. We retarget human motions to sixteen different humanoid embodiments, enabling scalable learning entirely from synthetic data. To im-prove real-world generalization, we further leverage unlabeled online videos through self-training to develop a 2D robot-pose detector. In our evaluation, HumanoidPose improves joint-angle and joint-position accuracy over the evaluated baselines and reduces joint-angle error by 18 –32% relative to the best-performing baseline on each of the three real humanoid platforms. We further show improved global localization and generalization to unseen embodiments. HumanoidPose enables visual understanding of other robots during interaction and extraction of robot motion from online and generated videos. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Understanding and interacting with humanoid robots re-quires interpreting how their bodies move. People and other robots may need to anticipate a reaching arm, recognize an action, or coordinate with the robot. Although onboard sensors measure the robot’s configuration, privacy, owner-ship, or communication constraints may prevent access to these measurements, making vision the primary means for an external observer to recover it. The same need extends to learning from video: online robot videos capture motion in varied environments without accompanying joint-state logs, while AI-generated videos provide candidate motion demon-strations with no physical robot or encoder measurements at all. Together with real-robot recordings and synthetic videos, these sources motivate recovering articulated robot motion directly from visual observations (Fig. 1). Expressing this motion in the robot’s joint coordinates makes it available for analysis, prediction, and learning. We study visual proprioception for humanoid robots: 

recovering full-body joint configurations in world space from ordinary video without access to onboard state. This setting introduces several challenges. Humanoids differ in body proportions, joint layouts, and mechanical limits, requiring an estimator that can accommodate different kinematic struc-tures and output spaces. Occlusion and monocular depth ambiguity make articulation underdetermined from appear-ance alone, calling for learned priors that generalize across embodiments, motions, and visual conditions. Recovering world-space trajectories further requires separating robot motion from camera motion. Learning these priors requires large-scale data pairing im-ages with robot configurations across motions, embodiments, and environments. Such data are difficult to obtain: col-lecting encoder-synchronized footage across many platforms is costly. Existing robot pose estimators address articulated manipulators [1]–[3], but humanoid motion recovery requires full-body estimation across robot designs and appearances. Human mesh recovery offers a way to reuse priors learned arXiv:2610.25288v1 [cs.RO] 18 Oct 2026 from extensive human imagery [4], [5]: estimate a human pose and retarget it to the robot [6]. However, human articulation differs from robot articulation, and retargeting can amplify prediction errors. We instead transfer pretrained human-pose features to directly predict robot joint poses. We present HumanoidPose , a framework for recovering full-body robot pose and world-space motion from video. Its key design is a shared decoder that combines frozen human-pretrained image features with queries derived from robot kinematics to predict joint configurations and root pose across embodiments. We combine the predicted root pose with estimated camera motion to recover world-space trajec-tories. Queries follow each robot’s joint layout, allowing a shared decoder and prediction head. Our synthetic pipeline retargets motions from the Archive of Motion Capture as Surface Shapes (AMASS) [7] to sixteen humanoids using General Motion Retargeting (GMR) [6] and renders paired images and robot states under varied conditions. These data supervise the pose decoder and initialize a YOLO11-based detector for robot boxes and 2D keypoints, and unlabeled online videos further adapt this detector. Across three real platforms, the unified model improves joint-angle and joint-position accuracy over the evaluated baselines. Our contributions are:  

> •

We formulate visual proprioception for humanoid robots, the problem of recovering full-body joint con-figurations and world-space motion from video without access to onboard state.  

> •

We introduce a unified, kinematically conditioned model for 3D robot pose estimation that predicts joint configurations and root pose across humanoid embodi-ments without robot-specific prediction heads.  

> •

> •

Robot Pose Estimation. Camera-to-robot calibration meth-ods such as DREAM and PoseFusion combine image key-points with known 3D robot keypoint locations to esti-mate the camera transform [8]–[10]. Recovering unknown joint states additionally requires estimating articulation, ad-dressed through rendering-based refinement in RoboPose and GISR [1], [11], [12], geometric lifting [13], [14], and feed-forward regression in HoRoPose [2]. More recent methods such as RoboPEPP and RoboTAG learn visual represen-tations that reflect robot articulation [3], [15], [16]. For humanoids, Amini et al. and Heo et al. [17], [18] estimate 2D image keypoints across robot platforms, without recov-ering full 3D articulation or actuated joint configurations. In contrast, HumanoidPose estimates full-body 3D humanoid robot poses in robot coordinates across embodiments. 

Human Pose and Motion Estimation. Parametric body models and image fitting [19]–[21] underpin learned body re-covery [22]–[24], while kinematic formulations connect joint positions and body rotations [25]–[27]. SAM 3D Body [28] supports keypoint and mask prompts for single-image body recovery. Video methods progress from temporal motion pri-ors [29] to global motion reconstruction [30], [31]. WHAM and TRAM [32], [33] explicitly account for camera mo-tion, while GVHMR [34] uses gravity-view coordinates. PromptHMR [5] incorporates spatial and semantic prompts into full-image SMPL-X recovery and extends to tempo-rally coherent reconstruction from video. Complementary approaches combine egocentric tracking and exocentric ob-servations for full-body motion capture [35]. However, hu-manoid morphology, joint limits and actuated coordinates make human-body representations indirect for robot-state recovery. HumanoidPose directly estimates robot joint co-ordinates and world-space motion from video. 

Synthetic Data and Sim-to-Real Transfer. Synthetic human data [36] and domain randomization [37] establish rendering as a source of visual supervision. Motion archives [7] and increasingly realistic datasets [38]–[42] broaden coverage of appearance, motion, and scenes, while procedural tools [43] automate rendering and annotation. Rendered robot images and adaptation techniques also support transfer to real robot observations [1], [8], [9], [18]. Motion retargeting [44] further connects human motion resources to new embod-iments, supporting humanoid control and imitation [45]– [48]. Recent pipelines such as UH-1 and GMR retarget human motion capture and internet videos into humanoid robot references [6], [49], showing that human motion priors can provide scalable supervision for humanoid behaviors. We combine retargeted motions with robot-specific rendering to pair images with the exact joint configurations used to generate them. This directly supervises actuated coordinates, enabling our decoder to train entirely on synthetic images and transfer to real robot videos. III. M ETHOD

B. Training Data and Detector Adaptation 

Synthetic data generation. Our synthetic pipeline turns human motion archives into paired robot images and states across diverse mechanisms. We first retarget AMASS human motions [7] to sixteen humanoid URDF/MJCF models using GMR [6], producing robot joint trajectories and root poses in each embodiment’s own coordinates. We render these con-figurations in Unreal Engine [50] with randomized scenes, lighting, camera viewpoints and robot appearances [37]. For each rendered frame, we project a shared set of robot body landmarks through the rendering camera to obtain 2D keypoint annotations and derive bounding boxes for detector training. Each RGB image is paired with its camera intrin-sics, root pose, exact joint configuration and corresponding 2D annotations. These pairs supervise robot configurations and 2D pose across different heights, proportions and joint counts. A common compiled robot model defines coordinate conventions, joint limits and forward kinematics (FK), keep-ing labels, descriptors and training losses consistent. 

2D robot pose estimation and self-training. To localize humanoids across embodiments, we finetune a YOLO11 pose model [51], initialized from human keypoint weights, on the paired images and 2D annotations generated by our synthetic pipeline. The model learns to predict a robot bounding box and 15 shared body landmarks: pelvis, neck, head, and the left and right hips, knees, feet, shoulders, elbows and wrists. This common landmark definition supports robots with different actuated joint layouts. We further adapt the model to real-world appearances through self-training on unlabeled online videos. Confident detections with sufficient visible keypoints serve as tem-poral anchors, and expanded keypoint extents define their bounding boxes. We fill short detection gaps by interpolating jointly visible keypoints between nearby anchors, retaining only single-robot pairs with overlapping boxes and excluding ambiguous detections. The resulting pseudo-labeled frames are mixed with synthetic images to finetune the model. We repeat pseudo-label generation and training for two rounds, excluding evaluation recordings. The 3D decoder trains solely on synthetic poses. At inference, the adapted model localizes the robot and defines a square RGB crop with robot-height fraction ρ =0.55 . The 3D decoder predicts robot pose from the crop’s visual features and robot descriptors. The predicted 2D landmarks also provide the observations for the geometric fitting baseline in our experiments.

To express the recovered poses in a common world frame, we combine each predicted root-to-camera transform with the camera-to-world transform. Here TA←B maps coordi-nates from frame B to frame A:

For a moving camera, we mask the robot to exclude its motion from camera estimation and use the PromptHMR camera pipeline [5] with DROID-SLAM [56]. Metric depth supplies the translation scale. The world frame is defined independently for each continuous shot. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

Our experiments assess HumanoidPose as an external source of robot-state information, evaluating its ability to recover joint configurations and global motion from visual observations. We examine accuracy and generalization across embodiments and domains, the contribution of the main design choices, and adaptation with additional visual data. 

A. Experimental setup 

Data and metrics. We use our sixteen-robot synthetic dataset (Sec. III-B) with separate training and test images. Real evaluation uses held-out, encoder-synchronized videos of Unitree G1, EngineAI PM01 and Fourier N1 performing locomotion, arm movements, crouching and supported poses, with synchronized optical motion capture on G1 for global root motion. Mean per-joint angle error (MPJAE, ◦) averages absolute wrapped joint-angle errors. Mean per-joint position error (MPJPE, cm) averages Euclidean FK body-origin errors in the robot base frame, with both root poses fixed to zero translation and identity rotation. We pool real frames within each robot and average synthetic metrics equally across robots. Global root translation is evaluated separately in Sec. IV-B. 

Baselines. Since full-body 3D humanoid pose estimation from RGB images or videos remains underexplored, we construct and adapt the following baselines for this task: PromptHMR [5] followed by GMR [6], the same pipeline with synthetic finetuning (SynFT) on our data, keypoint fitting, HoRoPose [2], and RoboPEPP [3]. Finetuning updates PromptHMR’s SMPL-X decoder and camera embedding with its image encoder frozen. The train-ing configuration for HumanoidPose is given in Sec. III-C. Synthetic finetuning uses the corresponding AMASS source labels for supervision. Synthetic retargeting receives the source global pose and body shape. On real data, all methods use square crops with robot-height fraction 0.55 ,and GMR receives predicted SMPL-X body rotations with a fixed canonical body shape and zero global orientation and translation. Retargeting solves each frame independently with the same GMR call for every method. 

Keypoint fitting is inspired by keypoint-based pose esti-mation [3], [8]. For each image, it fits joint angles and base pose to the 15 2D keypoints predicted by our robot-adapted YOLO11 model. The optimization minimizes confidence-weighted reprojection error through forward kinematics, sub-ject to joint limits, and starts from the zero configuration and a box-based translation estimate. It shares detections, crops and frames with all other methods and requires no training. We adapt HoRoPose [2] and RoboPEPP [3], originally evaluated on robot manipulators, to humanoids and train a separate model for each robot using their original code. HoRoPose retains its original losses and uses a joint-angle branch registered for each humanoid. RoboPEPP pretrains its embedding on our renders before finetuning.

D. Transfer to new embodiments 

We evaluate pose-decoder transfer while retaining the trained detector, whose training includes the target robots’ synthetic images and public videos. We separately train decoders with a frozen PromptHMR encoder, holding out both G1 variants or PM01 and leaving fourteen or fifteen training robots. At test time, the target’s kinematic descrip-tion supplies joint queries without learning new embeddings. Pooled real MPJAE is 11 .27 ◦ on G1 and 11 .44 ◦ on PM01, with median root errors of 8–16 cm across their sequences. Finetuning the G1-held-out decoder on increasing amounts of synthetic G1 data reduces synthetic test error (Fig. 5), with the image encoder frozen and no real pose labels.

V. L IMITATIONS 

HumanoidPose supports shared pose estimation across embodiments and transfer from synthetic images to real observations. Our evaluation focuses on individual robots while multi-robot scenes with sustained mutual occlusion remain to be explored. Such scenes require maintaining robot identities and pose consistency as visual evidence becomes intermittent, motivating temporal modeling and tracking. Downstream interaction and learning from demonstrations require further evaluation to establish how pose errors affect motion prediction, coordination and learned behaviors. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented HumanoidPose, a framework for recovering humanoid joint configurations in world space from video. Combining pretrained human-pose features with robot kine-matics allows one model to learn across embodiments from synthetic data, while self-training on unlabeled videos adapts the detector to real appearances. Experiments demonstrate improved pose accuracy over the evaluated baselines on real humanoids and support transfer and adaptation to new embodiments. These findings support vision as an external source of robot-state information when onboard measure-ments are inaccessible. HumanoidPose provides a basis for understanding other humanoids during interaction and for extracting internal states from online and generated videos.
